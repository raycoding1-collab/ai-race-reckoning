The Lobby

Everyone in the industry says it wants rules. The money shows which ones.

In 2025 more than 3,500 federal lobbyists worked on artificial intelligence, according to Public Citizen: one in four of all the lobbyists registered in Washington, and roughly 170 percent more than three years earlier. The number lobbying on data centers rose by 500 percent.{{fn:public-citizen}}

The most ambitious push so far failed. In mid-2025 a provision in the President's budget bill would have barred states from enforcing their own AI laws for ten years. After opposition from state officials and child-safety advocates in both parties, the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip it out.{{fn:time-moratorium}}

The effort did not end there. In December 2025 the President signed an executive order directing the Justice Department to form an "AI Litigation Task Force" to challenge state AI laws, and tying some federal broadband funds to states' AI policies.{{fn:lawfare-eo}}

Rules arrived anyway. California's SB 53, signed in September 2025, requires the largest AI developers to publish safety frameworks and report serious incidents.{{fn:fpf-sb53}} New York's RAISE Act, signed in December, takes effect in January 2027.{{fn:ny-raise}} In the European Union, obligations for general-purpose AI models have applied since August 2025, although lawmakers agreed this year to postpone the strictest rules for high-risk systems to December 2027 and August 2028.{{fn:gibson-eu}}

The money is now moving into elections. Leading the Future, a super PAC formed in August 2025 to support candidates who favor a single, light-touch federal standard over state rules, had raised more than $140 million by April. Its donors include the venture capitalists Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz and OpenAI's president, Greg Brockman, and his wife, Anna, at $12.5 million each.{{fn:ltf-wiki,notus-ltf}} On the other side, Anthropic has given $40 million to Public First Action, a group backing candidates who support AI safeguards.{{fn:cnbc-pfa,hill-pfa}} Both camps describe themselves as pro-innovation. The fight is over who writes the rules, and when.

The industry argues that speed matters because the stakes are so high. On that point, at least, its critics agree.
