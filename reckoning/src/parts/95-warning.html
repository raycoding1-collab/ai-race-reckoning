The Warning

Science fiction imagined this race decades ago. It also imagined how it ended.

In Frank Herbert's Dune, published in 1965, the far-future universe has no computers. Thousands of years before the story begins, humanity rose up against its thinking machines in a crusade called the Butlerian Jihad. Its chief commandment survives in the novel's scripture, the Orange Catholic Bible:{{fn:dune}}

Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind.

Herbert was less interested in rebellious robots than in what the machines had been for. In the novel's first chapter, the Reverend Mother Gaius Helen Mohiam explains it to the young Paul Atreides:

Once men turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free. But that only permitted other men with machines to enslave them.

That is the risk this ledger has tried to count. The danger in these pages rarely comes from a machine acting alone. It comes from the few companies and governments that control the most capable systems ever built, and from a public that has been told its only choice is to keep up.

What a reckoning would take

A reckoning is not only a judgment. It is also a settling of accounts. Several are already on the table, and none of them requires stopping the technology.

  1. Count the costs in public.

    The European Union already requires large data centers to report their energy and water use every year.{{fn:wc-eu}} Every country hosting the build-out could ask the same.

  2. Keep people in the loop, and pay for them.

    Before the Minab strike, the team meant to check for civilians had been cut from ten people to one.{{fn:bloomberg-minab}} A treaty on autonomous weapons, which the UN Secretary-General wants agreed by 2026, would make human control a legal duty rather than a staffing decision.{{fn:un-sg}}

  3. Close the data-broker loophole.

    The House has already passed a bill requiring a warrant for data the government now buys.{{fn:cyberscoop-4a}}

  4. Set a floor, not a ceiling.

    California and New York now require the largest developers to publish safety plans and report serious incidents.{{fn:fpf-sb53,ny-raise}} Pre-empting such laws without replacing them leaves no rules at all.

  5. Make abuse expensive.

    Federal law now requires platforms to take down non-consensual intimate images, including AI-generated ones, within 48 hours.{{fn:takeitdown}} Enforcement will decide whether that means anything.
