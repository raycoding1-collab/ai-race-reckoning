Chapter VII

The Watchers

AI makes it cheap to analyze everything that is collected. The law has not caught up with what the government can simply buy.

$170bnfor US immigration and border enforcement through 2029

The Fourth Amendment requires a warrant for a search. In Carpenter v. United States in 2018, the Supreme Court held that this includes a person's historical cellphone location records.{{fn:carpenter}} It left open what happens when the government, instead of demanding such records, buys them.

In March 2026, FBI Director Kash Patel told senators that the bureau does exactly that: "We do purchase commercially available information that's consistent with the Constitution and the laws under the Electronic Communications Privacy Act."{{fn:tnr-patel}} Senator Ron Wyden, who has spent years trying to close the gap, says the aim is to stop "the government from using its credit card when it should be getting a warrant."{{fn:jurist-wyden}} A bill to do that, the Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act, passed the House of Representatives in April 2024 by 219 votes to 199. It has not become law.{{fn:cyberscoop-4a}}

Artificial intelligence changes the economics. Collecting data was never the hard part; making sense of it was. Systems that can sift millions of records, match faces and summarize conversations make old stockpiles newly useful. Immigration enforcement is where this is moving fastest. The budget law President Trump signed in July 2025 provides about $170 billion for immigration and border enforcement through 2029, including roughly $75 billion for Immigration and Customs Enforcement and more than $6 billion for border surveillance technology.{{fn:brennan-obbba,aic-obbba}} ICE agents now carry Mobile Fortify, a phone app that checks faces and fingerprints against government databases holding more than 200 million images, and the agency has paid Palantir $30 million for ImmigrationOS, a system to track people and prioritize them for deportation.{{fn:tc-ice}}

The pipeline also runs through tools people use every day. An analysis by Surfshark of the privacy disclosures of popular AI chatbots found that Meta AI collects 32 of 35 possible types of personal data, Google's Gemini 22 and ChatGPT 17. Anthropic's Claude collects 13.{{fn:surfshark-bots}}

What people type into a chatbot, from health worries to legal trouble to problems at home, is often more revealing than anything they search for. Sam Altman, OpenAI's chief executive, has warned that those conversations carry no legal privilege: unlike a conversation with a doctor or a lawyer, they can be demanded in court.{{fn:tc-altman}}

Rules could fix much of this. Which brings us to the people writing them.
