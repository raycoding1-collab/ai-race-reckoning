Chapter V

The Work

The big forecasts say AI will create more jobs than it destroys. The first hard data says it is closing the door on the young.

19%relative decline in employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI

The optimistic case has serious sources. The World Economic Forum's 2025 Future of Jobs report, based on a survey of more than 1,000 large employers, expects technological and economic shifts to create 170 million jobs worldwide by 2030 and to displace 92 million, a net gain of 78 million. The same employers expect nearly two-fifths of workers' core skills to change over that period.{{fn:wef-jobs}}

A net figure is arithmetic, not comfort. The people who lose jobs are rarely the ones who get the new ones, and the first evidence of who is losing is arriving at the bottom of the ladder. Economists at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, working from the payroll records of millions of American workers, found that employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI fell 13 percent relative to peers in less exposed work after 2022. In an update published this August, the gap had widened to 19 percent. Older workers in the same occupations were largely unaffected, and the authors found no sign yet of displacement across the economy as a whole.{{fn:canaries}}

Other estimates point the same way. Goldman Sachs calculated in April that AI is displacing about 16,000 American jobs a month on net, roughly 25,000 lost where AI substitutes for workers against 9,000 gained where it augments them, with younger workers hit hardest.{{fn:goldman}} A simulation by MIT and Oak Ridge National Laboratory, the Iceberg Index, found that AI systems can already technically perform tasks worth 11.7 percent of American wages, about $1.2 trillion; the visible tip, in technology jobs, is only 2.2 percent.{{fn:iceberg}} Technical ability is not the same as layoffs. It is a map of where employers will look first.

In January, Kristalina Georgieva, the managing director of the International Monetary Fund, said at the World Economic Forum in Davos that AI was hitting the labor market "like a tsunami."{{fn:georgieva}} The Fund estimates that about 40 percent of jobs worldwide, and 60 percent in advanced economies, are exposed to AI; some will be made more productive, others eliminated.{{fn:imf-40}} In a survey the Forum published the same month, 57 percent of chief economists said they expected AI to cause net job losses over the next ten years.{{fn:wef-chief}}

Jobs can be retrained for, and economies adjust. The next cost cannot be undone.
