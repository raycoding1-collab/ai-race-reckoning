A to-scale model

Inside an AI accelerator

Frontier AI models are trained on packages like this one. Scroll to take it apart, from the compute die down to the 3,456 solder balls underneath.

Detail A Scale 3 : 1

The compute die

A single piece of silicon about 25 by 32 mm, close to the largest area a lithography scanner can expose in one pass. The outer regions hold the matrix engines; the band across the middle is on-chip cache.

Process
3 nm class

Detail B Scale 4 : 1

Stacked memory

Six stacks of high-bandwidth memory sit beside the die. Each has eight DRAM layers, thinned to about 30 µm and connected vertically by through-silicon vias. Most AI workloads are limited by how quickly data reaches the die, which is why so much of the package is memory.

Stacks
6 × 8-high
Capacity
144 GB
Bandwidth
About 7.2 TB/s

Section C–C Exploded

The interposer

Die and memory are mounted side by side on a passive slab of silicon with its own fine wiring. The short connections through it are what make that memory bandwidth possible. Here the layers are separated to show the bump arrays between them.

Size
46 × 35 mm
Microbump pitch
About 45 µm

Detail D Scale 8 : 1

Decoupling capacitors

The die's current draw can change by hundreds of amps within microseconds. Ceramic capacitors placed around it supply those short bursts and keep the core voltage stable.

Count
368 top, 72 underside
Size
0402 (1.0 × 0.5 mm)

View E From below

Ball grid array

All power and signals reach the board through solder balls on the underside. Most of them carry power and ground: at a core voltage below 1 V, a 1,000 W package draws well over 1,000 A.

Balls
3,456
Pitch
1.0 mm

Detail F Thermal

Power and heat

Almost all of the electrical power ends up as heat in a few square centimetres. At this density, fans give way to liquid cold plates, and data centres are increasingly planned around water supply as well as electricity.

Board power
1,000 W
Cooling
Direct liquid
