IXMeiji and Taishō1868 – 1926

明治

The New Age

An emperor restored, a railway over the sea, a new calendar, a constitution, and an empire.

In 1868 samurai from domains in the southwest, above all Satsuma and Chōshū, overthrew the shogunate in the name of a fifteen-year-old emperor, Meiji. In April his government issued the Charter Oath, five short articles promising assemblies, open discussion and an end to “evil customs of the past”, and ending with a resolve to seek knowledge throughout the world.{{fn:charter-oath}} In September Edo was renamed Tokyo, the eastern capital, and the emperor moved into the shoguns’ castle.{{fn:tokyo-1868}}

Rails over the sea

Japan’s first railway opened on 14 October 1872, twenty-nine kilometres from Shinbashi to Yokohama. For part of the way it ran on an embankment built across the shallows of Edo Bay at Takanawa, 2.7 kilometres long, which is what the print behind this chapter shows. In 2019 workers building a new station, Takanawa Gateway, uncovered 770 metres of it, and it is now a national historic site.{{fn:takanawa}} It was from this train that Edward Morse saw the shell mounds of Ōmori, in chapter I.

That same year the government changed the calendar. The old lunisolar calendar was abandoned, and the day after the second day of the twelfth month of Meiji 5 became 1 January 1873. The rest of December 1872 never happened in Japan.{{fn:calendar-1873}} Also in 1872 a state silk mill opened at Tomioka with French machines and engineers; it is now a World Heritage site.{{fn:tomioka}} Half the government, led by Iwakura Tomomi, spent 1871 to 1873 travelling through the United States and Europe to study how they worked.{{fn:iwakura}}

The samurai lost their stipends and their right to wear swords. In 1877 Saigō Takamori, a hero of the restoration, led the samurai of Satsuma in rebellion against the government he had helped create, and died when it was crushed.{{fn:satsuma}}

A constitution and an empire

On 11 February 1889 the emperor granted a constitution, drawn from German and British models. It created an elected house of parliament, the Imperial Diet, which first met in 1890, and it left the emperor supreme command of the army and navy.{{fn:meiji-constitution}}

The new state expanded at its edges. In 1869 it annexed and colonized the northern island it renamed Hokkaido, and settlers took the land of the Ainu, who were forced to assimilate; a law recognizing them as an indigenous people came only in 2019.{{fn:ainu,ainu-act}} In 1879 the kingdom of Ryukyu was abolished and made Okinawa Prefecture.{{fn:ryukyu}}

Then came wars abroad. Victory over Qing China in 1894 and 1895 won Taiwan.{{fn:sino-japanese}} In 1905, in the Tsushima Strait, the Japanese fleet destroyed the Russian fleet sent from the Baltic, in the only decisive battle ever fought between fleets of modern steel battleships.{{fn:tsushima}} Not everyone cheered. In September 1904 the poet Yosano Akiko published a poem to her younger brother, a soldier at the siege of Port Arthur, called 君死にたまふことなかれ, “Do not die, my brother”, and was denounced for it.{{fn:yosano}} In 1910 Japan annexed Korea.{{fn:korea-1910}}

Taishō

Meiji died in 1912, and the brief reign of his son Taishō brought party governments and mass politics. Just before noon on 1 September 1923 an earthquake of magnitude 7.9 struck Tokyo and Yokohama. It killed about 105,000 people, most of them in the fires that followed. In the chaos, malicious rumours about Koreans spread, and mobs armed with swords, iron bars and bamboo sticks, joined by soldiers, hunted Koreans down. The government of the day admitted a few hundred deaths; independent estimates put them at about 6,000.{{fn:kanto}}
