百人一首

One Hundred Poets

One poem each by a hundred poets, chosen by Fujiwara no Teika, and the card game that keeps them alive.

Some time between 1235 and 1241 the poet Fujiwara no Teika chose a hundred poems, one by each of a hundred poets, from Emperor Tenji in the seventh century to Emperor Juntoku in his own day. He is said to have copied them out to decorate the sliding doors of a villa near Mount Ogura, outside Kyoto, for his son’s father-in-law, Utsunomiya Yoritsuna. The Ogura Hyakunin Isshu, “one hundred people, one poem each”, has been the best known collection of Japanese poetry ever since.{{fn:hyakunin,hyakunin-origin}}

Every poem is a waka of thirty-one syllables in lines of five, seven, five, seven and seven. Most are about love or the seasons, and many turn on a double meaning that English cannot hold; the notes under some of the translations explain them. Here are all hundred, in Teika’s order, in the original and in a new translation made for this site.

Karuta

At New Year Japanese families play uta-garuta, poem cards. A reader chants the first three lines of a poem from one deck; the players race to find the card that carries the last two lines, written in kana alone. In competitive karuta the best players know each poem by its kimariji, the deciding syllables: the point in the reading at which only one card can match. For seven poems a single syllable is enough, む す め ふ さ ほ せ, which players learn as a word, musume fusahose. For six, the reader must reach the sixth syllable.{{fn:karuta}}
