The Warriors

A shogun in the east, two fleets from the Mongol empire, and new ways to the Buddha for ordinary people.

The victor of the Genpei War, Minamoto no Yoritomo, did not go to Kyoto to rule. He stayed in Kamakura, his base in the east, and governed the warriors from there. The Tale of the Heike, the epic of the war, was chanted for centuries by blind monks playing the biwa, a lute; the version most read today was compiled by one of them, Kakuichi, in 1371.{{fn:heike}}

When did it begin?

Japanese schoolchildren long learned that the Kamakura shogunate began in 1192, when the court appointed Yoritomo sei-i taishōgun, great general for quelling the barbarians. Many historians prefer 1185, when he won the right to place his own men as military governors, shugo, and land stewards, jitō, across the country. Textbooks now avoid giving a single year.{{fn:kamakura}} Either way, for almost seven hundred years Japan would have two centres of authority: an emperor who reigned and a shogun who ruled.

The Mongol fleets

Khubilai Khan, ruler of the Mongol empire and of China, sent envoys demanding submission. The shogunate refused. In 1274 a fleet of some 500 to 900 ships carrying 30,000 to 40,000 men, most of them Chinese and Korean, landed at Hakata Bay in Kyushu. After a day of fighting the invaders went back to their ships, and a storm wrecked many of them.{{fn:mongol,kamikaze}}

The shogunate prepared for a second attack. From March 1276 a stone wall twenty kilometres long was built along the shore of Hakata Bay; parts of it still stand.{{fn:borui}} In 1281 two fleets came, from Korea and from southern China, together said to number 4,400 ships and 140,000 men. The defenders held the beaches for weeks. Then a typhoon struck and destroyed most of the fleet.{{fn:mongol,kamikaze}} The storms came to be called kamikaze, the divine wind. Everything passes

War, fire, famine and earthquake gave urgency to Buddhist teachings about impermanence. In 1175 the monk Hōnen began to teach that anyone, however unlearned, could be reborn in Amida Buddha’s Pure Land by calling on his name: Namu Amida Butsu. Shinran, Dōgen and Nichiren followed, each founding a school of his own.{{fn:honen}}

In 1212 Kamo no Chōmei, a poet who had become a monk, described his life in a hut ten feet square in the hills outside Kyoto. His short book, the Hōjōki, records the disasters he had lived through and opens with a river.{{fn:hojoki}}

The shogunate outlived the Mongols by only half a century. In 1333 warriors loyal to Emperor Go-Daigo took Kamakura and ended it.{{fn:kamakura}}
