昭和

The Dark Valley

An army that made its own war, fifteen years of it, and the two cities that ended it.

On the night of 18 September 1931 a bomb exploded on a Japanese-owned railway near Mukden in Manchuria. Officers of Japan’s army in Manchuria had planned it themselves, without the authority of their government, as a pretext; within months they had occupied the whole region. In March 1932 Tokyo proclaimed a puppet state, Manchukuo. When the League of Nations condemned the invasion, Japan left the League, in March 1933.{{fn:mukden,league}}

The war in China

On 7 July 1937 Japanese and Chinese troops exchanged fire at the Marco Polo Bridge outside Beijing, and the clash grew into full-scale war.{{fn:marco-polo}} In December the Japanese army took Nanjing, the Chinese capital, and for weeks killed captured soldiers and civilians and raped women. Estimates of the dead range from about 100,000 to more than 300,000.{{fn:nanjing}}

The Pacific War

On the morning of 7 December 1941, Hawaii time, Japanese carrier aircraft attacked the American fleet at Pearl Harbor and killed 2,403 Americans. Japan was already at war with China; now it was at war with the United States and Britain as well.{{fn:pearl-harbor}} By 1945 the war had come home. On the night of 9 to 10 March 1945, 334 American B-29 bombers dropped incendiaries on Tokyo. The fires killed more than 100,000 people in a single night; estimates run from 88,000 to 200,000. It was the deadliest air raid of the war.{{fn:tokyo-raid}} On Okinawa, from April to June, about 100,000 civilians died, a quarter of the island’s people.{{fn:okinawa-battle}}

On 6 August an atomic bomb destroyed Hiroshima, and on 9 August another destroyed Nagasaki. By the end of the year about 140,000 people had died in Hiroshima and 74,000 in Nagasaki, from the blast, the fire and radiation.{{fn:atomic-deaths}}

At noon on 15 August the emperor’s recorded voice was broadcast by radio. Most of his people had never heard it. In the formal language of an imperial rescript he told them that the government had accepted the Allies’ terms; he never used the word surrender.{{fn:gyokuon-en}}

Japan’s government counts about 3.1 million Japanese dead in the war from 1937, some 2.3 million of them soldiers and sailors, more than half of whom died of disease and hunger rather than in battle.{{fn:war-dead}} The dead of the countries Japan invaded were far more numerous: in China alone, the historian Rana Mitter estimates, fourteen million or more.{{fn:china-dead}}

The dome

The building in the print behind this chapter was Hiroshima’s Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall, designed by the Czech architect Jan Letzel and finished in 1915. The bomb exploded about 600 metres above a point 160 metres away. Everyone inside was killed, but the walls stood, and the steel frame of the dome. Kept as it was, the A-Bomb Dome was added to the World Heritage List in 1996.{{fn:dome}} The next spring oleanders bloomed on the burned ground, where people had said nothing would grow for seventy-five years; the oleander is now the city’s flower.{{fn:oleander}} Since 1947, on the evening of 6 August, thousands of paper lanterns carrying prayers for peace are floated down the Motoyasu River past the dome, as in the night version of the print.{{fn:toro-nagashi}}

The inscription at the top of this chapter leaves out the subject of its sentence, as Japanese allows. Its author, Saika Tadayoshi, meant it to speak for all humanity, and a plaque added in 1983 says so.{{fn:cenotaph}}
