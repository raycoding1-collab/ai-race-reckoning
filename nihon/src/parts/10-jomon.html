縄文

The First Potters

Cord-marked clay, villages that lasted for millennia, and a tower of chestnut trunks.

Japan’s own chronicles begin with gods. Archaeology begins with pots. In 1998 excavators at Ōdai Yamamoto, a site near the northern tip of Honshu, recovered forty-six fragments of earthenware. Carbon on five of them, left by cooking, was dated at Nagoya University to about 16,500 years ago. That makes them among the oldest pottery known anywhere.{{fn:odai}}

A name from a train window

The name of the age comes from a zoologist. In June 1877 the American Edward S. Morse, riding the new railway from Yokohama to Shinbashi, noticed banks of shells beside the line at Ōmori. He came back that October to dig, and published Shell Mounds of Omori in 1879. He described the pots he found as “cord marked”, pressed with twisted rope while the clay was soft. In Japanese, cord pattern is 縄文 jōmon, and the word became the name for the people and the ten thousand years that made such pots.{{fn:morse,omori}} The railway he was riding is the subject of chapter IX.

The Jōmon people hunted, fished and gathered, and for most of this long age they did not farm rice. They did settle down. They built pit dwellings, sunk into the ground under roofs of thatch, and lived in them for generations. They made lacquer, carved bone and antler, and fired figures of clay called dogū and vessels whose rims burst into flame-like crests.

Sannai-Maruyama

The print behind this chapter shows the most famous of their villages. Sannai-Maruyama, near Aomori, was lived in for about two thousand years. Excavations there began in 1992 and found pit dwellings, long houses, roads, storage pits, lacquered pots and tools of bone and stone, all laid out with a plan.{{fn:sannai}} Its landmark is a reconstruction: a tower three storeys and nearly fifteen metres high, raised on six massive chestnut posts, standing where the postholes of the original were found.{{fn:jomon-whs}}

In July 2021 UNESCO inscribed Sannai-Maruyama with sixteen other sites in Hokkaido, Aomori, Iwate and Akita as the Jomon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan.{{fn:jomon-whs}}
