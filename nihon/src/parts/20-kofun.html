Rice and Kings

Paddy fields and bronze, a gold seal from the Han, a queen called Himiko, and tombs the size of hills.

Wet-rice farming came to the islands from the continent, and with it bronze, iron and weaving. Textbooks long dated the start of this Yayoi period to about 300 BCE. In 2003 researchers at the National Museum of Japanese History announced radiocarbon dates that pushed the beginning back by several centuries, to around 1000 to 800 BCE.{{fn:yayoi}}

Seen from China

The first written notices of Japan are Chinese, and they call it Wa. In 1784 a solid gold seal was found on the island of Shikanoshima in Fukuoka Bay. Its five characters, 漢委奴國王, name the King of Na of Wa under the Han. It is believed to be the seal that Emperor Guangwu gave an envoy from the islands in 57 CE, and it weighs 108.7 grams. Today it is a National Treasure, on display at the Fukuoka City Museum.{{fn:gold-seal}}

Two centuries later the historian Chen Shou described Wa in his Records of the Three Kingdoms. Wa had once been more than a hundred countries; after years of war among them, they chose a queen. Himiko lived secluded, served by a thousand women, and was seen by few. In 238 or 239 her envoys reached the Wei court, which named her “Queen of Wa, Friend of Wei” and sent her a gold seal, silks, swords and one hundred bronze mirrors.{{fn:himiko,wajinden}} Where her country of Yamatai lay, in Kyushu or in the Nara basin, is argued to this day.

The keyhole tombs

From the late third century the rulers of the Yamato plain were buried in great mounds, kofun, most of them shaped like a keyhole: a round rear mound joined to a square front, ringed by moats and set about with clay figures, haniwa. In 2019 UNESCO listed forty-nine of them in the Mozu and Furuichi groups near Osaka, built between the late fourth and late fifth centuries. The largest, the Daisen Kofun, is 486 metres long and said to be the tomb of Emperor Nintoku. It is the largest burial mound in Japan and one of the largest in the world.{{fn:mozu}}

A sword from a tomb in Saitama shows how far these kings reached. Along its blade 115 characters are inlaid in gold. They give the maker’s ancestry through eight generations and say he served King Wakatakeru, usually identified with Emperor Yūryaku, in a year of the sexagenary cycle generally read as 471.{{fn:inariyama}}
