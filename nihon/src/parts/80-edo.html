VIIIEdo1603 – 1868

江戸

The Great Peace

Two and a half centuries without war, a city of a million, the floating world, and ships that would not go away.

The Tokugawa shoguns ruled from Edo, a castle town on a marshy bay that they turned into a capital. They kept the peace by keeping the lords apart and busy. Under the system of alternate attendance, made compulsory for the outside lords in 1635 and for all of them in 1642, a daimyō lived one year in Edo and the next in his domain, and his wife and heir stayed in Edo. The processions on the roads and the great houses in the city cost the domains a large part of their wealth.{{fn:sankin}}

The closed country

Christianity was banned, and in 1637 a rising of peasants and masterless samurai at Shimabara, many of them Christian, took months to crush.{{fn:shimabara}} In 1639 Portuguese ships were forbidden to come. From 1641 the Dutch, who did not preach, were the only Europeans allowed to trade, from Dejima, a small artificial island in Nagasaki harbour, until 1854.{{fn:dejima}} Chinese ships still came to Nagasaki, and the domains of Tsushima, Satsuma and Matsumae dealt with Korea, the Ryukyu kingdom and the Ainu. The word for this policy, 鎖国 sakoku, closed country, was not used at the time. A translator, Shizuki Tadao, coined it in 1801 while putting into Japanese a book by the German traveller Engelbert Kaempfer.{{fn:sakoku}}

A city of a million

By the early eighteenth century more than a million people lived in Edo, probably more than in any other city in the world.{{fn:edo}} All distances in the country were measured from one bridge. Nihonbashi, built in 1603, was the starting point of the five great roads, the Tōkaidō and the Nakasendō among them, and the zero point of Japan’s roads is still marked there.{{fn:nihonbashi}} Hiroshige began his Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō, in 1833 or 1834, with a daimyō’s procession crossing it at dawn. The print behind this chapter borrows his bridge.{{fn:hiroshige-nihonbashi}}

In the pleasure quarters and theatres of the cities, merchants whom the official order ranked lowest of all spent their money on the ukiyo, the floating world. Its pictures, ukiyo-e, were printed from carved cherry blocks, one block for each colour. In late 1831 Hokusai published The Great Wave off Kanagawa, the first of his Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. Its blue is partly Prussian blue, the first modern synthetic pigment, invented in Germany and brought to Japan through the Dutch and Chinese trade.{{fn:great-wave,aizuri}} The prints on this page are made in the same way, one colour at a time, and their blue is that blue.

An old pond

Bashō spent his life travelling and made the seventeen-syllable verse into an art. In the spring of 1686, at a gathering in his hut at Fukagawa where every poem was to be about frogs, he wrote the most famous haiku of all.{{fn:basho-frog}}

In 1689 he set out with a companion, Sora, on a journey of five months through the north. He revised his account of it until 1694, and it was published in 1702, after his death.{{fn:basho}}

The black ships

On 8 July 1853 four American warships under Commodore Matthew Perry anchored at Uraga, at the mouth of Edo Bay, and demanded that Japan open its ports. People called them the black ships. Perry came back with seven ships the next spring, and on 31 March 1854 the shogunate signed the Convention of Kanagawa. A commercial treaty with the United States followed in 1858, and others with European powers.{{fn:perry}} Fifteen years after Perry’s first visit, the shogunate was gone.
