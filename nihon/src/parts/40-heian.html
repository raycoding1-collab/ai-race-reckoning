平安

The Capital of Peace

A new script, the women who wrote in it, the regents who ruled through marriage, and the warriors who ended it all.

In 794 Emperor Kanmu moved his court to a new city on the Kamo River. He called it Heian-kyō, the capital of peace and tranquility. We call it Kyoto, and it remained the seat of the imperial court for most of the next thousand years.{{fn:heiankyo}}

A script of their own

Man’yōgana spelled Japanese with whole Chinese characters, which was slow to write. Written quickly with a brush, the characters wore down into simple flowing shapes, and by the tenth century these had become a script of their own: hiragana, each sign the cursive ghost of one character. Monks reading Chinese texts took a different route, keeping only a part of a character as a small mark, and their signs became katakana.{{fn:manyogana,hiragana}}

{{kana}}

The oldest record of the Iroha, a poem that uses every kana exactly once, dates from 1079. For centuries it served as the Japanese alphabet song and the order of dictionaries.{{fn:iroha}}

The women who wrote in it

Men at court wrote official Chinese; kana was the script of letters, poems and women. Ki no Tsurayuki, the editor of the first imperial anthology of Japanese poetry, the Kokinshū, knew both. When he wrote the diary of his journey home from a governorship in Tosa, in 934 and 935, he wrote it in kana, in the voice of a woman.{{fn:kana-preface,tosa}}

Within a century the greatest prose in the language was being written by women in the service of empresses. Sei Shōnagon, lady-in-waiting to Empress Teishi, filled her Pillow Book with lists, sketches and judgments, and finished it around 1002.{{fn:pillow}}

Murasaki Shikibu, who served Empress Shōshi, wrote The Tale of Genji, fifty-four chapters on the loves and griefs of an emperor’s son by a low-ranking consort. It was complete by about 1021, when a girl in the provinces recorded in her diary the joy of owning a full copy.{{fn:genji-iz2}}

Rule by marriage

Real power belonged to the Fujiwara, who married their daughters to emperors and ruled as regents for their grandsons. At a banquet in 1018, at the height of his power, Fujiwara no Michinaga improvised a poem that the courtier Fujiwara no Sanesuke copied into his diary, the Shōyūki.{{fn:michinaga}}

His son Yorimichi turned a family villa at Uji into a temple in 1052, and the next year built the Phoenix Hall, an Amida hall whose wings spread over its pond like a bird alighting, with two gilt bronze phoenixes on the roof. It is in the print behind this chapter, and on the back of every ten-yen coin since 1951.{{fn:byodoin}}

The warriors

The provinces were kept by armed families, and in the twelfth century two of them, the Taira and the Minamoto, were drawn into the court’s quarrels and then fought each other for the realm. The Genpei War ended in 1185 in a sea battle at Dan-no-ura. As the Taira went down, the six-year-old Emperor Antoku, a Taira grandson, was carried into the sea and drowned rather than be captured.{{fn:heike}}
