VIMuromachi1336 – 1573

室町

Gold and Silver

Two courts, a golden pavilion, the art of Noh, tea and gardens of stone, and a war that burned Kyoto.

Emperor Go-Daigo’s restoration lasted three years. In 1336 the general Ashikaga Takauji set up a rival emperor in Kyoto, and Go-Daigo fled south into the mountains of Yoshino. For more than half a century Japan had two imperial courts, Northern and Southern, each with its own emperors and its own era names, until they were reunited in 1392.{{fn:nanbokucho}} Takauji became shogun in 1338, and his family’s shogunate took its name from the Muromachi district of Kyoto, where they lived.

Gold

The third Ashikaga shogun, Yoshimitsu, who reunited the courts, bought a villa in the northern hills of Kyoto in 1397 and built there a pavilion covered in gold leaf. After his death his son made it a Zen temple.{{fn:kinkaku}} The Golden Pavilion stood for more than five centuries. On 2 July 1950 a twenty-two-year-old novice monk set fire to it, and it burned to the ground. The pavilion in the print behind this chapter is the one rebuilt in 1955; Mishima Yukio turned the arson into a novel, The Temple of the Golden Pavilion, in 1956.{{fn:kinkaku}}

Silver

Yoshimitsu’s grandson Yoshimasa was a poor ruler and a great patron. In 1482 he began a retreat of his own in the eastern hills, modelled on his grandfather’s. The Silver Pavilion was never covered in silver. Around it grew what is called Higashiyama culture: the tea ceremony, flower arrangement, ink painting, Noh and the arts of the garden, which spread far beyond the court.{{fn:ginkaku}} At the Zen temple Ryōan-ji, fifteen stones lie in raked white gravel, placed so that from anywhere on the veranda you can see only fourteen of them at once.{{fn:ryoanji}}

The flower

Noh took its classic form under Yoshimitsu’s patronage in the work of the actor Kan’ami and his son Zeami, who wrote down his father’s teaching and his own in the Fūshi kaden. The flower of his title is the living charm of a performance, which depends on what the actor holds back.{{fn:zeami}}

At the start of the age, around 1330, the monk Kenkō began the book of short essays that Japanese students still read in school, Essays in Idleness.{{fn:tsurezure}}

The war that burned Kyoto

In 1467 the rivalry between two great families, the Hosokawa and the Yamana, entangled with a dispute over who would succeed Yoshimasa, became a war fought street by street in the capital. By the time it ended in 1477 much of Kyoto had been burned, the shogunate’s authority was gone, and the country had broken up among local lords.{{fn:onin}}
