日本

A history of Japan in twelve prints

Where the Sun Rises

Sixteen thousand years, from the first potters to the present: rice and kings, poets and warriors, a long peace, a headlong modern century, war and what came after. At the end, all one hundred poems of the Hyakunin Isshu, a game to play them with, and a key to Japan’s names for time.

Begin

IJōmonc. 14,500 BCE – first millennium BCE

縄文

The First Potters

Cord-marked clay, villages that lasted for millennia, and a tower of chestnut trunks.

Japan’s own chronicles begin with gods. Archaeology begins with pots. In 1998 excavators at Ōdai Yamamoto, a site near the northern tip of Honshu, recovered forty-six fragments of earthenware. Carbon on five of them, left by cooking, was dated at Nagoya University to about 16,500 years ago. That makes them among the oldest pottery known anywhere.3

A name from a train window

The name of the age comes from a zoologist. In June 1877 the American Edward S. Morse, riding the new railway from Yokohama to Shinbashi, noticed banks of shells beside the line at Ōmori. He came back that October to dig, and published Shell Mounds of Omori in 1879. He described the pots he found as “cord marked”, pressed with twisted rope while the clay was soft. In Japanese, cord pattern is 縄文 jōmon, and the word became the name for the people and the ten thousand years that made such pots.4,5 The railway he was riding is the subject of chapter IX.

The Jōmon people hunted, fished and gathered, and for most of this long age they did not farm rice. They did settle down. They built pit dwellings, sunk into the ground under roofs of thatch, and lived in them for generations. They made lacquer, carved bone and antler, and fired figures of clay called dogū and vessels whose rims burst into flame-like crests.

Sannai-Maruyama

The print behind this chapter shows the most famous of their villages. Sannai-Maruyama, near Aomori, was lived in for about two thousand years. Excavations there began in 1992 and found pit dwellings, long houses, roads, storage pits, lacquered pots and tools of bone and stone, all laid out with a plan.6 Its landmark is a reconstruction: a tower three storeys and nearly fifteen metres high, raised on six massive chestnut posts, standing where the postholes of the original were found.7

In July 2021 UNESCO inscribed Sannai-Maruyama with sixteen other sites in Hokkaido, Aomori, Iwate and Akita as the Jomon Prehistoric Sites in Northern Japan.7

IIYayoi and Kofunfirst millennium BCE – 538 CE

古墳

Rice and Kings

Paddy fields and bronze, a gold seal from the Han, a queen called Himiko, and tombs the size of hills.

Wet-rice farming came to the islands from the continent, and with it bronze, iron and weaving. Textbooks long dated the start of this Yayoi period to about 300 BCE. In 2003 researchers at the National Museum of Japanese History announced radiocarbon dates that pushed the beginning back by several centuries, to around 1000 to 800 BCE.9

Seen from China

The first written notices of Japan are Chinese, and they call it Wa. In 1784 a solid gold seal was found on the island of Shikanoshima in Fukuoka Bay. Its five characters, 漢委奴國王, name the King of Na of Wa under the Han. It is believed to be the seal that Emperor Guangwu gave an envoy from the islands in 57 CE, and it weighs 108.7 grams. Today it is a National Treasure, on display at the Fukuoka City Museum.10

Two centuries later the historian Chen Shou described Wa in his Records of the Three Kingdoms. Wa had once been more than a hundred countries; after years of war among them, they chose a queen. Himiko lived secluded, served by a thousand women, and was seen by few. In 238 or 239 her envoys reached the Wei court, which named her “Queen of Wa, Friend of Wei” and sent her a gold seal, silks, swords and one hundred bronze mirrors.11,8 Where her country of Yamatai lay, in Kyushu or in the Nara basin, is argued to this day.

The keyhole tombs

From the late third century the rulers of the Yamato plain were buried in great mounds, kofun, most of them shaped like a keyhole: a round rear mound joined to a square front, ringed by moats and set about with clay figures, haniwa. In 2019 UNESCO listed forty-nine of them in the Mozu and Furuichi groups near Osaka, built between the late fourth and late fifth centuries. The largest, the Daisen Kofun, is 486 metres long and said to be the tomb of Emperor Nintoku. It is the largest burial mound in Japan and one of the largest in the world.12

A sword from a tomb in Saitama shows how far these kings reached. Along its blade 115 characters are inlaid in gold. They give the maker’s ancestry through eight generations and say he served King Wakatakeru, usually identified with Emperor Yūryaku, in a year of the sexagenary cycle generally read as 471.13

IIIAsuka and Nara538 – 794

奈良

The Buddha and the Law

A new faith from Korea, a state on the Chinese model, a capital of straight streets, and a Buddha sixteen metres tall.

In the middle of the sixth century King Seong of Baekje, on the Korean peninsula, sent the Yamato court an image of the Buddha and a set of sutras. The Nihon Shoki dates the gift to 552; other sources give 538. Neither date is secure, but the arrival of Buddhism through diplomacy in these years is.15

Prince Shōtoku and the rising sun

Tradition gives the new order a founder: Prince Shōtoku, regent for Empress Suiko. The Nihon Shoki credits him with a Seventeen-Article Constitution in 604, a set of moral precepts drawn from Buddhism and Confucius rather than a constitution in the modern sense. Many scholars now doubt he wrote it.14 Temples were built in his time. Hōryū-ji, founded in 607, burned in 670 and was rebuilt by 711; its buildings of the late seventh and early eighth centuries are among the oldest wooden buildings in the world, and in 1993 they were among the first Japanese sites on the World Heritage List.16

Also in 607, the envoy Ono no Imoko carried a letter to the emperor of Sui China. It opened with the words at the top of this page, from “the Son of Heaven where the sun rises” to “the Son of Heaven where the sun sets”. It is the earliest known use of a phrase meaning land of the rising sun for Japan. Emperor Yang, who expected tribute from a lesser kingdom, was offended by the claim to equal rank.1

A state on the Chinese model

In 645 Prince Naka no Ōe and Nakatomi no Kamatari, the founder of the Fujiwara house, had Soga no Iruka, the most powerful man at court, cut down in front of the empress. The Taika Reforms that followed began to remake Yamato as a centralized state on the model of Tang China.17 Taika, “great change”, was the first of Japan’s era names; there have been 248 of them since, and chapter XIII can tell you which one any year belongs to.18 By the end of the century the ruler had the title 天皇 tennō, heavenly sovereign, and the country the name 日本 Nihon, origin of the sun.19 The Taihō Code of 701 gave the state its laws.20

In 710 the court moved to Heijō-kyō, the capital we call Nara. It was laid out on a grid after Chang’an, the Tang capital, though without its walls, and at its height it held between 50,000 and 100,000 people.21 Here the court had its past written down. The Kojiki, “record of ancient matters”, was presented to Empress Genmei in 712. Ō no Yasumaro compiled it with the help of Hieda no Are, who, it was said, could remember anything after hearing it once.22 The Nihon Shoki followed in 720.23

The Great Buddha

Emperor Shōmu made Buddhism the protector of the state. At Tōdai-ji, the “great eastern temple”, he had a bronze Buddha cast sixteen metres high, in eight castings over three years. At its eye-opening in 752 an Indian monk, Bodhisena, painted in the eyes before ten thousand monks. The hall around it, rebuilt in 1709, is the one in the print behind this chapter.24

The monk Jianzhen, Ganjin in Japanese, tried five times between 743 and 748 to sail from China to Japan to teach the rules of ordination. He lost his sight on the way. He landed at last in 753, blind, and founded Tōshōdai-ji in Nara in 759.25

Ten thousand leaves

The Man’yōshū, “collection of ten thousand leaves”, gathers about 4,500 poems in twenty books, the latest from 759. Emperors, soldiers and farmers’ wives are among its poets. It is written entirely in Chinese characters, many used only for their sound to spell out Japanese words, a system called man’yōgana.26

These words introduce thirty-two poems on plum blossoms composed at a party given by Ōtomo no Tabito at Dazaifu in 730. On 1 April 2019 the government announced that the next era would be called Reiwa, from 令 and 和 in this passage. It was the first era name taken from a Japanese classic rather than a Chinese one.28,27

IVHeian794 – 1185

平安

The Capital of Peace

A new script, the women who wrote in it, the regents who ruled through marriage, and the warriors who ended it all.

In 794 Emperor Kanmu moved his court to a new city on the Kamo River. He called it Heian-kyō, the capital of peace and tranquility. We call it Kyoto, and it remained the seat of the imperial court for most of the next thousand years.30

A script of their own

Man’yōgana spelled Japanese with whole Chinese characters, which was slow to write. Written quickly with a brush, the characters wore down into simple flowing shapes, and by the tenth century these had become a script of their own: hiragana, each sign the cursive ghost of one character. Monks reading Chinese texts took a different route, keeping only a part of a character as a small mark, and their signs became katakana.31,32

The oldest record of the Iroha, a poem that uses every kana exactly once, dates from 1079. For centuries it served as the Japanese alphabet song and the order of dictionaries.33

The women who wrote in it

Men at court wrote official Chinese; kana was the script of letters, poems and women. Ki no Tsurayuki, the editor of the first imperial anthology of Japanese poetry, the Kokinshū, knew both. When he wrote the diary of his journey home from a governorship in Tosa, in 934 and 935, he wrote it in kana, in the voice of a woman.29,34

Within a century the greatest prose in the language was being written by women in the service of empresses. Sei Shōnagon, lady-in-waiting to Empress Teishi, filled her Pillow Book with lists, sketches and judgments, and finished it around 1002.35

Murasaki Shikibu, who served Empress Shōshi, wrote The Tale of Genji, fifty-four chapters on the loves and griefs of an emperor’s son by a low-ranking consort. It was complete by about 1021, when a girl in the provinces recorded in her diary the joy of owning a full copy.36

Rule by marriage

Real power belonged to the Fujiwara, who married their daughters to emperors and ruled as regents for their grandsons. At a banquet in 1018, at the height of his power, Fujiwara no Michinaga improvised a poem that the courtier Fujiwara no Sanesuke copied into his diary, the Shōyūki.38

His son Yorimichi turned a family villa at Uji into a temple in 1052, and the next year built the Phoenix Hall, an Amida hall whose wings spread over its pond like a bird alighting, with two gilt bronze phoenixes on the roof. It is in the print behind this chapter, and on the back of every ten-yen coin since 1951.39

The warriors

The provinces were kept by armed families, and in the twelfth century two of them, the Taira and the Minamoto, were drawn into the court’s quarrels and then fought each other for the realm. The Genpei War ended in 1185 in a sea battle at Dan-no-ura. As the Taira went down, the six-year-old Emperor Antoku, a Taira grandson, was carried into the sea and drowned rather than be captured.40

VKamakura1185 – 1333

鎌倉

The Warriors

A shogun in the east, two fleets from the Mongol empire, and new ways to the Buddha for ordinary people.

The victor of the Genpei War, Minamoto no Yoritomo, did not go to Kyoto to rule. He stayed in Kamakura, his base in the east, and governed the warriors from there. The Tale of the Heike, the epic of the war, was chanted for centuries by blind monks playing the biwa, a lute; the version most read today was compiled by one of them, Kakuichi, in 1371.40

When did it begin?

Japanese schoolchildren long learned that the Kamakura shogunate began in 1192, when the court appointed Yoritomo sei-i taishōgun, great general for quelling the barbarians. Many historians prefer 1185, when he won the right to place his own men as military governors, shugo, and land stewards, jitō, across the country. Textbooks now avoid giving a single year.41 Either way, for almost seven hundred years Japan would have two centres of authority: an emperor who reigned and a shogun who ruled.

The Mongol fleets

Khubilai Khan, ruler of the Mongol empire and of China, sent envoys demanding submission. The shogunate refused. In 1274 a fleet of some 500 to 900 ships carrying 30,000 to 40,000 men, most of them Chinese and Korean, landed at Hakata Bay in Kyushu. After a day of fighting the invaders went back to their ships, and a storm wrecked many of them.42,43

The shogunate prepared for a second attack. From March 1276 a stone wall twenty kilometres long was built along the shore of Hakata Bay; parts of it still stand.44 In 1281 two fleets came, from Korea and from southern China, together said to number 4,400 ships and 140,000 men. The defenders held the beaches for weeks. Then a typhoon struck and destroyed most of the fleet.42,43 The storms came to be called kamikaze, the divine wind. The print behind this chapter imagines that night.

Everything passes

War, fire, famine and earthquake gave urgency to Buddhist teachings about impermanence. In 1175 the monk Hōnen began to teach that anyone, however unlearned, could be reborn in Amida Buddha’s Pure Land by calling on his name: Namu Amida Butsu. Shinran, Dōgen and Nichiren followed, each founding a school of his own.45

In 1212 Kamo no Chōmei, a poet who had become a monk, described his life in a hut ten feet square in the hills outside Kyoto. His short book, the Hōjōki, records the disasters he had lived through and opens with a river.46

The shogunate outlived the Mongols by only half a century. In 1333 warriors loyal to Emperor Go-Daigo took Kamakura and ended it.41

VIMuromachi1336 – 1573

室町

Gold and Silver

Two courts, a golden pavilion, the art of Noh, tea and gardens of stone, and a war that burned Kyoto.

Emperor Go-Daigo’s restoration lasted three years. In 1336 the general Ashikaga Takauji set up a rival emperor in Kyoto, and Go-Daigo fled south into the mountains of Yoshino. For more than half a century Japan had two imperial courts, Northern and Southern, each with its own emperors and its own era names, until they were reunited in 1392.49 Takauji became shogun in 1338, and his family’s shogunate took its name from the Muromachi district of Kyoto, where they lived.

Gold

The third Ashikaga shogun, Yoshimitsu, who reunited the courts, bought a villa in the northern hills of Kyoto in 1397 and built there a pavilion covered in gold leaf. After his death his son made it a Zen temple.50 The Golden Pavilion stood for more than five centuries. On 2 July 1950 a twenty-two-year-old novice monk set fire to it, and it burned to the ground. The pavilion in the print behind this chapter is the one rebuilt in 1955; Mishima Yukio turned the arson into a novel, The Temple of the Golden Pavilion, in 1956.50

Silver

Yoshimitsu’s grandson Yoshimasa was a poor ruler and a great patron. In 1482 he began a retreat of his own in the eastern hills, modelled on his grandfather’s. The Silver Pavilion was never covered in silver. Around it grew what is called Higashiyama culture: the tea ceremony, flower arrangement, ink painting, Noh and the arts of the garden, which spread far beyond the court.51 At the Zen temple Ryōan-ji, fifteen stones lie in raked white gravel, placed so that from anywhere on the veranda you can see only fourteen of them at once.52

The flower

Noh took its classic form under Yoshimitsu’s patronage in the work of the actor Kan’ami and his son Zeami, who wrote down his father’s teaching and his own in the Fūshi kaden. The flower of his title is the living charm of a performance, which depends on what the actor holds back.48

At the start of the age, around 1330, the monk Kenkō began the book of short essays that Japanese students still read in school, Essays in Idleness.53

The war that burned Kyoto

In 1467 the rivalry between two great families, the Hosokawa and the Yamana, entangled with a dispute over who would succeed Yoshimasa, became a war fought street by street in the capital. By the time it ended in 1477 much of Kyoto had been burned, the shogunate’s authority was gone, and the country had broken up among local lords.54

VIISengoku and Azuchi–Momoyama1467 – 1603

戦国

The Warring States

Guns and missionaries from Portugal, three unifiers, and the castles they built.

For a century after the Ōnin War, local lords, the daimyō, fought one another for land. Into this world, in 1543, a storm drove a Chinese junk carrying three Portuguese adventurers onto the island of Tanegashima, south of Kyushu. They were the first Europeans known to set foot in Japan. The island’s lord bought two of their matchlock guns and set a swordsmith to copy them; Japanese smiths were soon making their own, and the gun became known as a tanegashima.56,57 In 1549 the Jesuit Francis Xavier landed at Kagoshima and began the Christian mission to Japan.57

Three unifiers

Three men put the country back together. The first, Oda Nobunaga, a minor lord from Owari, made his name in 1560 at Okehazama, where his small force surprised and killed the invading warlord Imagawa Yoshimoto.55 In 1575 at Nagashino his musketeers helped crush the army of the Takeda, and the next year he began Azuchi Castle on Lake Biwa, completed in 1579. In June 1582 one of his own generals, Akechi Mitsuhide, surrounded him at the temple of Honnō-ji in Kyoto, and Nobunaga took his own life inside the temple.58

The second, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who had risen from a foot soldier, avenged his lord and finished the conquest. In 1588 he ordered the sword hunt, which took weapons from farmers and fixed the line between those who fought and those who worked the land.59 His tea master Sen no Rikyū, who had shaped the tea ceremony into an art of simplicity, fell out of favour and was ordered to kill himself in 1591.60 In 1597 twenty-six Christians, six foreign missionaries and twenty Japanese, three of them boys, were crucified at Nagasaki.61

Hideyoshi also invaded Korea, twice, in 1592 and 1597. His armies took Seoul and Pyongyang, but the Korean navy under Admiral Yi Sun-sin, a Ming army and Korean irregulars stopped them, and the campaign ended with his death in 1598.62

Sekigahara

On 21 October 1600, at Sekigahara, the eastern army of the third, Tokugawa Ieyasu, met the western army of Hideyoshi’s loyalists. The fight was even until midday, when a western commander on the hills above the field, won over in advance, turned on his own side. Three years later Ieyasu was named shogun.63

The print behind this chapter shows a castle in the style of the one that was completed at Himeji in 1609, on the site of an older fortress granted to Ikeda Terumasa after Sekigahara. Its white walls and stacked roofs survive intact, and in 1993 it became the first Japanese castle on the World Heritage List.64

VIIIEdo1603 – 1868

江戸

The Great Peace

Two and a half centuries without war, a city of a million, the floating world, and ships that would not go away.

The Tokugawa shoguns ruled from Edo, a castle town on a marshy bay that they turned into a capital. They kept the peace by keeping the lords apart and busy. Under the system of alternate attendance, made compulsory for the outside lords in 1635 and for all of them in 1642, a daimyō lived one year in Edo and the next in his domain, and his wife and heir stayed in Edo. The processions on the roads and the great houses in the city cost the domains a large part of their wealth.66

The closed country

Christianity was banned, and in 1637 a rising of peasants and masterless samurai at Shimabara, many of them Christian, took months to crush.67 In 1639 Portuguese ships were forbidden to come. From 1641 the Dutch, who did not preach, were the only Europeans allowed to trade, from Dejima, a small artificial island in Nagasaki harbour, until 1854.68 Chinese ships still came to Nagasaki, and the domains of Tsushima, Satsuma and Matsumae dealt with Korea, the Ryukyu kingdom and the Ainu. The word for this policy, 鎖国 sakoku, closed country, was not used at the time. A translator, Shizuki Tadao, coined it in 1801 while putting into Japanese a book by the German traveller Engelbert Kaempfer.69

A city of a million

By the early eighteenth century more than a million people lived in Edo, probably more than in any other city in the world.70 All distances in the country were measured from one bridge. Nihonbashi, built in 1603, was the starting point of the five great roads, the Tōkaidō and the Nakasendō among them, and the zero point of Japan’s roads is still marked there.71 Hiroshige began his Fifty-three Stations of the Tōkaidō, in 1833 or 1834, with a daimyō’s procession crossing it at dawn. The print behind this chapter borrows his bridge.72

In the pleasure quarters and theatres of the cities, merchants whom the official order ranked lowest of all spent their money on the ukiyo, the floating world. Its pictures, ukiyo-e, were printed from carved cherry blocks, one block for each colour. In late 1831 Hokusai published The Great Wave off Kanagawa, the first of his Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji. Its blue is partly Prussian blue, the first modern synthetic pigment, invented in Germany and brought to Japan through the Dutch and Chinese trade.73,74 The prints on this page are made in the same way, one colour at a time, and their blue is that blue.

An old pond

Bashō spent his life travelling and made the seventeen-syllable verse into an art. In the spring of 1686, at a gathering in his hut at Fukagawa where every poem was to be about frogs, he wrote the most famous haiku of all.75

In 1689 he set out with a companion, Sora, on a journey of five months through the north. He revised his account of it until 1694, and it was published in 1702, after his death.65

The black ships

On 8 July 1853 four American warships under Commodore Matthew Perry anchored at Uraga, at the mouth of Edo Bay, and demanded that Japan open its ports. People called them the black ships. Perry came back with seven ships the next spring, and on 31 March 1854 the shogunate signed the Convention of Kanagawa. A commercial treaty with the United States followed in 1858, and others with European powers.76 Fifteen years after Perry’s first visit, the shogunate was gone.

IXMeiji and Taishō1868 – 1926

明治

The New Age

An emperor restored, a railway over the sea, a new calendar, a constitution, and an empire.

In 1868 samurai from domains in the southwest, above all Satsuma and Chōshū, overthrew the shogunate in the name of a fifteen-year-old emperor, Meiji. In April his government issued the Charter Oath, five short articles promising assemblies, open discussion and an end to “evil customs of the past”, and ending with a resolve to seek knowledge throughout the world.77 In September Edo was renamed Tokyo, the eastern capital, and the emperor moved into the shoguns’ castle.78

Rails over the sea

Japan’s first railway opened on 14 October 1872, twenty-nine kilometres from Shinbashi to Yokohama. For part of the way it ran on an embankment built across the shallows of Edo Bay at Takanawa, 2.7 kilometres long, which is what the print behind this chapter shows. In 2019 workers building a new station, Takanawa Gateway, uncovered 770 metres of it, and it is now a national historic site.79 It was from this train that Edward Morse saw the shell mounds of Ōmori, in chapter I.

That same year the government changed the calendar. The old lunisolar calendar was abandoned, and the day after the second day of the twelfth month of Meiji 5 became 1 January 1873. The rest of December 1872 never happened in Japan.80 Also in 1872 a state silk mill opened at Tomioka with French machines and engineers; it is now a World Heritage site.81 Half the government, led by Iwakura Tomomi, spent 1871 to 1873 travelling through the United States and Europe to study how they worked.82

The samurai lost their stipends and their right to wear swords. In 1877 Saigō Takamori, a hero of the restoration, led the samurai of Satsuma in rebellion against the government he had helped create, and died when it was crushed.84

A constitution and an empire

On 11 February 1889 the emperor granted a constitution, drawn from German and British models. It created an elected house of parliament, the Imperial Diet, which first met in 1890, and it left the emperor supreme command of the army and navy.85

The new state expanded at its edges. In 1869 it annexed and colonized the northern island it renamed Hokkaido, and settlers took the land of the Ainu, who were forced to assimilate; a law recognizing them as an indigenous people came only in 2019.87,88 In 1879 the kingdom of Ryukyu was abolished and made Okinawa Prefecture.89

Then came wars abroad. Victory over Qing China in 1894 and 1895 won Taiwan.90 In 1905, in the Tsushima Strait, the Japanese fleet destroyed the Russian fleet sent from the Baltic, in the only decisive battle ever fought between fleets of modern steel battleships.91 Not everyone cheered. In September 1904 the poet Yosano Akiko published a poem to her younger brother, a soldier at the siege of Port Arthur, called 君死にたまふことなかれ, “Do not die, my brother”, and was denounced for it.92 In 1910 Japan annexed Korea.93

Taishō

Meiji died in 1912, and the brief reign of his son Taishō brought party governments and mass politics. Just before noon on 1 September 1923 an earthquake of magnitude 7.9 struck Tokyo and Yokohama. It killed about 105,000 people, most of them in the fires that followed. In the chaos, malicious rumours about Koreans spread, and mobs armed with swords, iron bars and bamboo sticks, joined by soldiers, hunted Koreans down. The government of the day admitted a few hundred deaths; independent estimates put them at about 6,000.94

XEarly Shōwa1926 – 1945

昭和

The Dark Valley

An army that made its own war, fifteen years of it, and the two cities that ended it.

On the night of 18 September 1931 a bomb exploded on a Japanese-owned railway near Mukden in Manchuria. Officers of Japan’s army in Manchuria had planned it themselves, without the authority of their government, as a pretext; within months they had occupied the whole region. In March 1932 Tokyo proclaimed a puppet state, Manchukuo. When the League of Nations condemned the invasion, Japan left the League, in March 1933.96,97

The war in China

On 7 July 1937 Japanese and Chinese troops exchanged fire at the Marco Polo Bridge outside Beijing, and the clash grew into full-scale war.98 In December the Japanese army took Nanjing, the Chinese capital, and for weeks killed captured soldiers and civilians and raped women. Estimates of the dead range from about 100,000 to more than 300,000.99

The Pacific War

On the morning of 7 December 1941, Hawaii time, Japanese carrier aircraft attacked the American fleet at Pearl Harbor and killed 2,403 Americans. Japan was already at war with China; now it was at war with the United States and Britain as well.100 By 1945 the war had come home. On the night of 9 to 10 March 1945, 334 American B-29 bombers dropped incendiaries on Tokyo. The fires killed more than 100,000 people in a single night; estimates run from 88,000 to 200,000. It was the deadliest air raid of the war.101 On Okinawa, from April to June, about 100,000 civilians died, a quarter of the island’s people.102

On 6 August an atomic bomb destroyed Hiroshima, and on 9 August another destroyed Nagasaki. By the end of the year about 140,000 people had died in Hiroshima and 74,000 in Nagasaki, from the blast, the fire and radiation.103

At noon on 15 August the emperor’s recorded voice was broadcast by radio. Most of his people had never heard it. In the formal language of an imperial rescript he told them that the government had accepted the Allies’ terms; he never used the word surrender.104

Japan’s government counts about 3.1 million Japanese dead in the war from 1937, some 2.3 million of them soldiers and sailors, more than half of whom died of disease and hunger rather than in battle.106 The dead of the countries Japan invaded were far more numerous: in China alone, the historian Rana Mitter estimates, fourteen million or more.107

The dome

The building in the print behind this chapter was Hiroshima’s Prefectural Industrial Promotion Hall, designed by the Czech architect Jan Letzel and finished in 1915. The bomb exploded about 600 metres above a point 160 metres away. Everyone inside was killed, but the walls stood, and the steel frame of the dome. Kept as it was, the A-Bomb Dome was added to the World Heritage List in 1996.108 The next spring oleanders bloomed on the burned ground, where people had said nothing would grow for seventy-five years; the oleander is now the city’s flower.109 Since 1947, on the evening of 6 August, thousands of paper lanterns carrying prayers for peace are floated down the Motoyasu River past the dome, as in the night version of the print.110

The inscription at the top of this chapter leaves out the subject of its sentence, as Japanese allows. Its author, Saika Tadayoshi, meant it to speak for all humanity, and a plaque added in 1983 says so.95

XIShōwa, Heisei, Reiwa1945 – today

戦後

After the War

A peace constitution, the fastest growth the world had seen, a train at 210 kilometres an hour, and the long years after the boom.

For the first time in its history Japan was occupied by a foreign army. The American-led occupation rewrote its institutions. A new constitution, drafted largely by the occupation’s staff and passed by the Diet, was promulgated on 3 November 1946 and took effect on 3 May 1947. It made the emperor a symbol of the state, and in its ninth article renounced war.112 Women voted for the first time on 10 April 1946 and elected thirty-nine of their number to the Diet.113

The peace treaty was signed at San Francisco on 8 September 1951, and the occupation ended when it came into force on 28 April 1952, except in Okinawa.114 Okinawa stayed under American administration for twenty-seven years and returned to Japan on 15 May 1972; the American bases there remain.115

The miracle

Between 1955 and 1973 the economy grew by about nine percent a year on average, and in 1968 Japan’s national product passed West Germany’s to become the second largest in the world.116 On 1 October 1964, nine days before Tokyo opened the Olympic Games, the Tōkaidō Shinkansen began running between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka. Its trains, the 0 series, reached 210 kilometres an hour and cut the journey to four hours. It was the first railway in the world built for regular service above 200 kilometres an hour; the print behind this chapter shows one crossing the Fuji River.117

The bubble and after

Land and share prices rose through the 1980s until, on 29 December 1989, the Nikkei stock index closed at 38,915.87. Then the bubble burst. The index did not pass that figure again until 22 February 2024.118 The years between are called Japan’s lost decades.

The ground itself moved. The Great Hanshin earthquake of 17 January 1995 killed 6,434 people in and around Kobe, by the official count.119 On 11 March 2011 an earthquake of magnitude 9.1 off the northeast coast raised a tsunami that left more than 18,000 people dead or missing and caused meltdowns in three reactors at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power plant.120

Japan’s population peaked at about 128 million around 2008 and has fallen every year since; the census of 2025 counted about 123 million.121,122

Reiwa

On 30 April 2019 Emperor Akihito stepped down, the first emperor to abdicate since Kōkaku in 1817. His son Naruhito succeeded him the next day, and the era of Reiwa began.123,28

This year, 2026, is the year of the fire horse, 丙午 hinoe-uma, which comes once in sixty years. A superstition held that girls born in such a year would grow up headstrong and hard to marry, and in 1966, the last fire horse year, births in Japan fell by a quarter. In 2026 the superstition seems to have lost its hold: births in January were slightly up on the year before.124,125

百人一首

One Hundred Poets

One poem each by a hundred poets, chosen by Fujiwara no Teika, and the card game that keeps them alive.

Some time between 1235 and 1241 the poet Fujiwara no Teika chose a hundred poems, one by each of a hundred poets, from Emperor Tenji in the seventh century to Emperor Juntoku in his own day. He is said to have copied them out to decorate the sliding doors of a villa near Mount Ogura, outside Kyoto, for his son’s father-in-law, Utsunomiya Yoritsuna. The Ogura Hyakunin Isshu, “one hundred people, one poem each”, has been the best known collection of Japanese poetry ever since.126,127

Every poem is a waka of thirty-one syllables in lines of five, seven, five, seven and seven. Most are about love or the seasons, and many turn on a double meaning that English cannot hold; the notes under some of the translations explain them. Here are all hundred, in Teika’s order, in the original and in a new translation made for this site.

紫式部

大弐三位

ありま山ゐなの笹原風吹けばいでそよ人を忘れやはする

赤染衛門

相模

三条院

Karuta

At New Year Japanese families play uta-garuta, poem cards. A reader chants the first three lines of a poem from one deck; the players race to find the card that carries the last two lines, written in kana alone. In competitive karuta the best players know each poem by its kimariji, the deciding syllables: the point in the reading at which only one card can match. For seven poems a single syllable is enough, む す め ふ さ ほ せ, which players learn as a word, musume fusahose. For six, the reader must reach the sixth syllable.128

XIIIGengō645 – today

元号

The Names of Time

Two hundred and forty-eight era names, and the year you were born in one of them.

Japanese documents are still dated by era: this year is Reiwa 8. The custom came from China with the Taika era in 645. In all there have been 248 era names, and since 1868 there has been one to each reign.18 Before then a new name could be proclaimed for an omen, a disaster or the start of a new cycle of the zodiac, and some lasted only months. For most of the seventh century there were none at all, and between 1331 and 1392 the rival Northern and Southern Courts each counted years in their own.49

About this page

Text and translations of all Japanese passages are new, made for this site. The twelve prints were drawn by a program written for them, in the manner of ukiyo-e landscape prints. Their scenes are reconstructions and homages, not records: the Sannai-Maruyama tower, for instance, is today’s reconstruction, and the pavilion is the Golden Pavilion rebuilt in 1955.

Japanese texts: Kamo no Chōmei, Ki no Tsurayuki, Chen Shou, the Kojiki as edited by Takeda Yūkichi, Fukuzawa Yukichi and the two constitutions are quoted from Aozora Bunko. Hyakunin Isshu: the transcriptions in the npm packages ogura-hyakunin-isshu (Unlicense), hyakunin-isshu (MIT) and kimariji_karuta (ISC). Era names: Harumi by Code for History (MIT); readings for the Edo era names from nengo-full (MIT). Stroke order for the brush-written titles: KanjiVG, copyright Ulrich Apel, under the Creative Commons Attribution-Share Alike 3.0 licence. Type: Literata and Bodoni Moda for English, Shippori Mincho and Yuji Syuku for Japanese, all under the SIL Open Font License.
