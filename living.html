Part II

What's Worth Focusing On

A reading of the research on how to live well now, when most of what surrounds you is competing for your attention.

September 2026 · about twenty minutes

Part I of this site was a diagnosis: power gathering in a few companies, a web filling with synthetic content, work being automated, attention sold by the minute. It ended on the line “That choice is still ours to make.” This is an attempt to say what making that choice looks like for one person in an ordinary week.

I went looking for the strongest evidence I could find on two things: what makes a life go well, and what has changed about the conditions we now live in. They turn out to be closely connected.

The problem

By material measures this is the best time there has ever been to be alive. Two centuries ago roughly one child in two died before adulthood. By 2020 it was four in a hundred.1 But on the things that make up a good day, several trends run the other way.

Nobody chose these declines. They arrived along with conveniences that people did choose: the feed with no bottom, the meal delivered for one, the phone on the nightstand, the tool that writes the first draft. Each is reasonable on its own terms. Taken together they make up the defaults of modern life, and most of them were designed by someone optimizing for something other than whether your life goes well.

So the practical question is mostly one of subtraction: deciding what to leave out when so much is built to be hard to leave out.

My answer, after the reading below, is that attention is the thing to guard, because it is what everything else is made of. It should go first to other people, then to your body, then to work that uses your own mind for something outside yourself. The rest of this page is the evidence for that ordering, including where the evidence is weak.

1Other people

Evidence: strong and consistent, mostly observational.

The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed the same group of men, and later their families, since 1938. Its best-known finding is that how satisfied people were with their relationships at fifty predicted their health at eighty better than their cholesterol levels did.6 A meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 people found that those with stronger social ties were about 50% more likely to survive over the follow-up period, an effect on the same order as smoking.7 In 2025 the WHO's Commission on Social Connection estimated that loneliness is linked to about 871,000 deaths a year.3

Some of the most striking evidence is also the most ordinary. The 2025 World Happiness Report found that how often people share meals predicts their wellbeing about as well as income or unemployment does, across 142 countries. In 2023 about one American in four had eaten every meal of the previous day alone, up 53% since 2003.8

The caveat is that nearly all of this is correlational. Healthier and happier people may simply find it easier to keep friends. Still, the finding has held across decades, countries and methods, and the biology behind it (stress hormones, inflammation, health habits) is plausible. There are few sturdier results in the social sciences.

In practice

  • Make seeing people something that happens without a decision. A standing arrangement, the same people at the same time each week, does more than occasional big plans.
  • Eat with other people when you can.
  • Reach out first. Most people are waiting for someone else to.
  • Put more into a few close relationships than into a wide network, and repair arguments soon.
  • Keep the phone away when you're with the people physically around you.

2Your attention

Evidence: moderate. Some good trials, and real disagreement about effect sizes.

In 2010 Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert contacted thousands of people at random moments and asked what they were doing and what they were thinking about. People's minds were elsewhere 46.9% of the time. Whether someone's mind was wandering explained more of how happy they felt (10.8% of the variance) than the activity itself did (4.6%).9

Gloria Mark's research on office work found that people stayed on one screen for about two and a half minutes on average in 2004, and about 47 seconds in studies from 2016 onward.4 That measures switching, not a biological limit on concentration, but it describes how fragmented an ordinary workday has become.

The best recent experiment blocked mobile internet on 467 adults' phones for two weeks, leaving calls and texts working. 91% improved on at least one of three measures (mental health, wellbeing or sustained attention), and the drop in depressive symptoms was comparable to what is typically seen with cognitive behavioral therapy. The gains came through more time offline, more time with people in person, and more sleep.10

Other studies find much smaller effects. Deactivating Facebook for six weeks before the 2020 election improved an index of emotional state by 0.06 standard deviations, and the Instagram result was not statistically significant.11 A large analysis of adolescents found that digital technology use explained at most 0.4% of the variation in their wellbeing. Jonathan Haidt and others dispute that framing, especially for girls and social media.12

I read these together as saying the phone does most of its harm by displacement. What matters is what it crowds out: sleep, company, and stretches of uninterrupted thought. That would explain why removing mobile internet altogether works better than quitting one app, where the time simply flows to another.

In practice

  • Turn off every notification that doesn't come from a person. Move feeds off the phone, or remove them.
  • Charge the phone outside the bedroom.
  • Keep one block of the day, an hour or two, for a single task before opening email or feeds.
  • Leave some walks and waits without audio.
  • When you pick up a screen, ask whether you chose what you're about to see.

3Your body

Evidence: strong. Large cohorts and pooled analyses.

Among 122,007 patients who took a treadmill test, the least fit had about five times the adjusted mortality of the most fit. The authors found that low fitness carried as much risk as smoking, diabetes or coronary artery disease, or more. They found no level of fitness above which the benefit stopped.13

The amounts needed are modest. A pooled analysis of fifteen cohorts found that the mortality benefit of walking leveled off at about 8,000–10,000 steps a day for adults under sixty, and 6,000–8,000 for older adults.14 Thirty to sixty minutes a week of strength training was associated with a 10–20% lower risk of death.15 Both short and long sleep predict mortality in data on 1.38 million people,16 and about 30% of American adults sleep under seven hours.5 People who spent at least two hours a week in nature reported better health and wellbeing, whether that came in one visit or several. Below two hours there was no detectable benefit.17

In practice

  • Seven to nine hours of sleep, at roughly the same time each night.
  • Around 8,000 steps a day, much of it outdoors.
  • Two short strength sessions a week that cover pushing, pulling, squatting, hinging and carrying.
  • One or two sessions a week that leave you out of breath.
  • Combine where you can. A long walk on a trail with a friend counts toward four of these seven sections.

4Your own mind, alongside AI

Evidence: early. Small studies and self-reports, plus some solid labor data.

The labor data is starting to arrive. Stanford economists using payroll records found that since generative AI spread, employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations fell about 13% relative to less exposed jobs. The gap had widened to about 19% by the August 2026 update. The losses are concentrated where AI automates the work rather than assisting the person doing it.18

The evidence on thinking is earlier and weaker. In a survey of 319 knowledge workers, the more people trusted AI with a task, the less critical thinking they reported doing. Confidence in their own ability pointed the other way.19 In a small MIT study (54 people, first released as a preprint and since criticized), people who wrote essays with an LLM from the start showed the weakest brain connectivity, and 83% couldn't quote a sentence they had just written. People who wrote on their own first and then used the tool showed more engagement, not less.20

What I take from this is mostly about order: do the thinking first, then bring in the tool.

In practice

  • Draft or attempt something yourself before asking a model, then use it to critique and check your work.
  • Keep one skill you practice without assistance, so you know what you can do unaided.
  • Invest in what compounds and is slow to automate: judgment, deep knowledge of one field, relationships in which people trust you, physical skills, and learning quickly.
  • Prefer work where the tools make you more valuable over work where they make you cheaper to replace.
  • The large-scale risks described in Part I are political questions. Vote and organize on them.

5Meaning and contribution

Evidence: moderate. Consistent cohort findings, hard to measure.

Among 6,985 Americans over fifty, those with the lowest sense of purpose were about 2.4 times as likely to die during the study as those with the highest, after adjusting for other factors.21 Research on regret points in a related direction. People regret things they did more in the short term, and things they didn't do more over the long run, although replications find a weaker version of that pattern.22

Purpose is difficult to measure, and illness may reduce it rather than the other way round. I include it anyway because the cohort evidence is consistent, and because it is what most of the philosophical tradition, from Aristotle to Viktor Frankl, would have predicted.

In practice

  • Have one project larger than yourself that someone else benefits from.
  • Make things as well as consume them: writing, building, teaching, cooking for people.
  • A few times a year, ask what you would regret not having tried, and do a small real version of it.

6Money: enough, then time

Evidence: moderate. Large surveys and one field experiment.

The idea that happiness stops rising at about $75,000 didn't hold up. Daniel Kahneman and Matthew Killingsworth had published conflicting results. When they reanalyzed their data together with Barbara Mellers, they found that for most people emotional wellbeing keeps rising with income well past $100,000. For the least happy fifth it rises until about $100,000 and then flattens. That suggests money relieves money problems but not the other kind.23

How money is spent matters too. In samples from four countries totaling 6,271 people, those who paid to save time reported higher life satisfaction. A field experiment found that a time-saving purchase made people happier than a material one.24

In practice

  • Build a cash buffer before anything else.
  • Spend on time, and on experiences with other people, more than on things.
  • Be wary of raising your spending in ways that tie you to work you would otherwise leave.

7What you read and believe

Evidence: moderate. Well-replicated in education research.

Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew asked historians, Stanford undergraduates and professional fact-checkers to judge unfamiliar websites. The fact-checkers did best, and fastest, because they left the page almost immediately to see what other sources said about it. Asked to compare a real pediatricians' association with a similarly named advocacy group, 65% of the students and half of the historians picked the wrong one. Every fact-checker got it right.25 The habit can be taught in a short time.26 With synthetic media spreading (Part I covers this), it has become an everyday skill.

In practice

  • Before believing or sharing something, find out who is behind it from somewhere other than the page itself.
  • Read news weekly rather than hourly. Little breaking news asks anything of you.
  • Follow fewer and better sources, including one you often disagree with.
  • Treat anything that makes you angry quickly as suspect until you've checked it.

What to let go of

Focusing on something means neglecting other things on purpose. These are the ones I would neglect first.

  • Trying to get through everything. An eighty-year life is about four thousand weeks, as Oliver Burkeman points out.27 The list won't be finished, so choose what stays undone.
  • Anger about things you can't affect. Epictetus's distinction still holds: act on what is in your control, and stay informed but calm about the rest.
  • Comparing yourself with people online, whose lives you only see edited.
  • Measuring everything. Once the basics are in place, more tracking mostly takes attention away from living.
  • Following bad news as a pastime. The risks are real, but reading about them all day is not a response to them.
  • Accumulating things. People get used to new possessions quickly. Time and shared experiences last longer.

A routine

The aim is to change your defaults, so that the right thing becomes the easy thing and you don't have to rely on willpower.

WhenWhat
Every day
  • A regular bedtime, with the phone outside the bedroom
  • One focused block before email or feeds
  • About 8,000 steps
  • One real conversation, not only messages
  • Think before prompting
Every week
  • A standing arrangement with the same people
  • Several shared meals
  • Two strength sessions, one or two hard cardio sessions
  • Two hours outdoors, ideally with someone
  • Half a day offline
  • Time on the project that's bigger than you
Every few months
  • Who do I want to be closer to, and how?
  • What skill am I building that AI makes more valuable?
  • What would I regret not trying?
  • Cancel apps, subscriptions, sources and commitments that no longer earn their place

Where to start

The ordering above is for an average person. For you, the best place to start is whichever important area is weakest. Rate each one from 0 (neglected) to 5 (going well) for the past month. The page weights the gaps by the ordering and suggests one. Nothing is sent anywhere. Your answers are kept only in this browser.

What the evidence can't tell you

Most of the studies here are correlational, so some of what looks like cause and effect may run the other way. Randomized trials exist for a few questions (blocking mobile internet, deactivating social media, spending to save time) and not for most. Many samples come from rich Western countries. Serious researchers disagree, in good faith, about how large the effects of social media are. And averages are not individuals. An introvert may need fewer, closer ties. A shift worker can't keep a regular bedtime. A carer may already have more purpose than time.

What gives me confidence in the overall picture is that very different kinds of evidence agree. Long cohort studies, meta-analyses, a handful of trials, surveys across 142 countries and a long philosophical tradition all come back to the same few things: people, attention, the body, and some purpose outside yourself.

“Once men turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free. But that only permitted other men with machines to enslave them.”

Frank Herbert, Dune (1965)

Part I ended by saying the choice is still ours. For one person in an ordinary week, that choice comes down mostly to where attention goes: to the people around you, to a body that has to carry you for decades, and to work that uses your own mind for something beyond yourself. Almost everything else in the modern environment is trying to decide that for you.
