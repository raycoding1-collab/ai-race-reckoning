Part II · After the Reckoning

What's worth focusing on.

Part I was the diagnosis: power concentrating, attention harvested, reality getting harder to verify, work being automated. Part I ended with "That choice is still ours to make." This part is about making it. What does the evidence say a person should actually put their limited time and attention into, here and now?

01 · The problem, precisely stated

We solved scarcity and built a problem of abundance.

By almost any material measure, this is the best time in history to be alive. But the same numbers show something else. The things that matter most for a good life are declining, and they're declining because of what modern life makes easy.

We have too much food, information, stimulation and choice. We have too little sleep, movement, sustained attention, and each other. None of that is a personal failing. It's the result of defaults. Your phone's home screen, your feed, your commute, your calendar, your food supply and your work tools were mostly designed by people optimizing for something other than your life going well.

So the modern problem isn't "how do I get more?" It's "how do I choose what to leave out, when everything is built to be hard to leave out?"

Your life is, in the end, what you paid attention to. The modern economy is built to capture that attention. So the most important skill is deciding on purpose where your attention goes.

The research is surprisingly consistent about where it should go. People come first. Then a body that can carry you. Then work that uses your own mind and matters to someone else.

PRIORITY 01Evidence: strong

Other people.

If you focus on only one thing, focus on this. The evidence here is larger, more consistent and older than for anything else on this page.

  • The Harvard Study of Adult Development has followed its participants since 1938. It found that how satisfied people were with their relationships at 50 predicted their physical health at 80 better than their cholesterol did. The quality of close relationships beat wealth, fame, social class, IQ and genes as a predictor of long-term health and happiness. Harvard Gazette · Waldinger & Schulz, The Good Life (2023)
  • A meta-analysis of 148 studies and 308,849 people found a 50% higher likelihood of survival for people with stronger social relationships. That effect is comparable to well-known risk factors such as smoking. Holt-Lunstad et al., PLoS Medicine, 2010
  • Lacking social connection raises the risk of early death by more than 60%, according to the WHO's 2025 Commission on Social Connection. It's linked to heart disease, stroke, dementia and depression. WHO, June 2025
  • How often people share meals predicts their wellbeing about as strongly as income or unemployment does, across 142 countries. Meanwhile, about 1 in 4 Americans ate every meal alone the previous day in 2023, up 53% since 2003. World Happiness Report 2025
  • Most of this evidence is observational. Healthier, happier people may simply find it easier to keep friends. But the result has held across decades, countries and study designs, and there are plausible biological mechanisms (stress hormones, inflammation, health behaviors). Few findings in the social sciences are sturdier.

What to actually do

  • Make seeing people the default, not a decision. Set up a recurring, same-time, same-people commitment: a weekly dinner, a run club, a game night, a congregation, a team. Regular contact beats occasional big gestures.
  • Eat with others as often as you can. It's the cheapest intervention on this page.
  • Be the one who reaches out. Most people are waiting for someone else to go first. The 2025 World Happiness Report also found that people are much kinder than we expect.
  • Go deep, not wide. A few relationships where you can be fully known matter more than a big network. Repair conflicts quickly. Ask better questions.
  • Protect the people who are physically near you (partner, kids, neighbors) from the device in your hand.

Loneliness is now a public-health emergency on the scale of smoking. Showing up for people is one of the few remedies that also makes you happier along the way.

PRIORITY 02Evidence: moderate

Your attention.

Attention isn't just one priority among the others. Every other priority depends on it, and it's the one that's being contested most aggressively.

  • People's minds are elsewhere 46.9% of their waking hours. Whether someone's mind was wandering explained more of their momentary happiness (10.8%) than what they were actually doing (4.6%). "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind." Killingsworth & Gilbert, Science, 2010 · 250,000 experience samples
  • In office work, the average stretch of attention on one screen fell from about 2.5 minutes (2004) to 47 seconds in studies from about 2016 onward. That's time before switching, not a biological limit, but it shows how fragmented a typical workday has become. Gloria Mark, Attention Span (2023)
  • In a randomized trial, 467 adults had all mobile internet blocked on their phones for two weeks. Calls and texts still worked. 91% improved on at least one of three measures: mental health, wellbeing or sustained attention. The drop in depressive symptoms was larger than typical antidepressant effects and similar to CBT. The improvements came through more time offline, more face-to-face contact and more sleep. Castelo et al., PNAS Nexus, 2025
  • Not every study finds large effects. Deactivating Facebook for six weeks improved an index of emotional state by just 0.06 SD, and the Instagram result (0.04 SD) wasn't statistically significant. In teenagers, digital-technology use explains at most about 0.4% of the variation in wellbeing in one large analysis. Jonathan Haidt and colleagues dispute that framing, particularly for girls and social media. Allcott et al., NBER 2025 · Orben & Przybylski, Nature Human Behaviour, 2019

Here's how to reconcile these. The phone probably isn't a poison. Its main effect is displacement. The damage comes from what it crowds out: sleep, people, unbroken focus, boredom that turns into ideas. That's why interventions that give those hours back (like blocking mobile internet) work better than quitting a single app, where the time just goes to another one.

What to actually do

  • Make the phone boring. Turn off every notification that doesn't come from a real person. Delete feed apps from the phone, or use them only on a laptop. Try grayscale.
  • The phone sleeps outside the bedroom. This one habit protects your sleep, the first hour of your day and your relationship.
  • Protect one deep block a day (60–120 min, one task, nothing else open) before you check feeds or email.
  • Leave some gaps unfilled. Walk, commute or wait without audio sometimes. A lot of thinking happens in those moments.
  • Ask one question: "Did I choose this, or was it chosen for me?" Use it on every screen session.
PRIORITY 03Evidence: strong

Your body.

Sleep, fitness, strength and time outdoors are the biggest cheap effects in medicine. They also improve your mood, focus and patience with other people.

  • In 122,007 patients given treadmill tests, the least-fit group had a 5× higher adjusted mortality than the most fit (HR 5.04). The risk from low fitness was as large as or larger than smoking, diabetes or coronary artery disease. There was no upper limit to the benefit. Mandsager et al., JAMA Network Open, 2018
  • The benefit of walking levels off at ~8,000–10,000 steps/day for adults under 60 and ~6,000–8,000 for those 60 and over. You don't need extreme volumes. Paluch et al., Lancet Public Health, 2022 · 15 cohorts
  • Just 30–60 minutes/week of strength training was associated with a 10–20% lower risk of death from all causes, as well as lower rates of heart disease, cancer and diabetes. Momma et al., BJSM, 2022
  • Sleep that's consistently short (and, as a marker of illness, sleep that's unusually long) predicts all-cause mortality. That's from 1.38 million participants. Nearly a third of US adults are short sleepers. Cappuccio et al., Sleep, 2010 · CDC 2024
  • People who spent 120+ minutes a week in nature reported better health and wellbeing. It didn't matter whether that came in one visit or several. Below 120 minutes there was no measurable benefit. White et al., Scientific Reports, 2019 · n = 19,806

The minimum effective dose

  • Sleep: 7–9 hours, at a consistent time. Keep the bedroom dark, cool and free of phones.
  • Walk: ~8,000 steps a day, much of it outside. Walking to things counts.
  • Lift: two sessions a week, 20–30 minutes each, covering the big movements (push, pull, squat, hinge, carry).
  • Breathe hard: one or two sessions a week that raise your cardio fitness. It's the strongest single fitness predictor of lifespan.
  • Stack priorities: a long walk with a friend on a trail covers people, attention, movement and nature in one go.
PRIORITY 04Evidence: emerging

Your own mind, in the age of AI.

AI is the defining technology of this period. The personal question isn't whether to use it. It's whether you're getting more capable because of it or handing over the capacities that make you valuable.

  • Since generative AI arrived, workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed occupations have seen a 13% relative drop in employment. The August 2026 update puts the gap near 19%. Job losses cluster where AI automates tasks rather than augments the people doing them. Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab
  • Among 319 knowledge workers, the more they trusted AI to do a task, the less critical thinking they reported doing (β = −0.69). Workers who were more confident in their own abilities reported doing more. Lee et al., Microsoft Research & CMU, CHI 2025
  • Essay writers who used an LLM from the start showed the weakest brain connectivity on EEG, and 83% couldn't quote a line from essays they'd just written. People who wrote on their own first and then used AI showed more neural engagement. Kosmyna et al., MIT Media Lab, 2025
  • The MIT study was small (n = 54), came out as a preprint and has published critiques. The Microsoft/CMU data is self-reported. Treat these as early warnings, not settled findings. What they agree on is that the order you do things in matters.

What to actually do

  • Think first, then prompt. Draft, outline or attempt the solution yourself. Then use AI to critique, extend or check it. That way it adds to your thinking instead of replacing it.
  • Keep at least one skill you practice without help, like writing, math, an instrument or a craft, as a baseline for your own ability.
  • Build what compounds and is hard to automate: judgment and taste, deep knowledge of one area, relationships built on trust, physical-world skills, and the ability to learn quickly.
  • Aim for jobs that augment you. Look for roles where AI makes you more valuable rather than a cheaper substitute for you.
  • Stay a citizen, not just a user. The risks in Part I are political. Vote, organize and support the groups working on AI governance.
PRIORITY 05Evidence: moderate

Meaning & contribution.

Pleasure doesn't last because we adapt to it. Purpose holds up better. It's having something to do that someone else benefits from.

  • In 6,985 US adults over 50, those with the lowest sense of life purpose were 2.4× as likely to die during follow-up as those with the highest (HR 2.43), after adjusting for other factors. Alimujiang et al., JAMA Network Open, 2019
  • Regrets follow a time pattern: things we did sting more in the short term, but things we didn't do weigh more over a lifetime. Replications find the same pattern with weaker effects. Gilovich & Medvec, 1994 · Collabra replication, 2022
  • Purpose is hard to measure, and sick people may report less of it, which would reverse the causation. Still, the pattern shows up across cohorts, and philosophy has said the same for millennia, from Aristotle's eudaimonia to Frankl's logotherapy.

What to actually do

  • Have one project bigger than yourself that someone else benefits from: a craft, a cause, a community role, raising people well.
  • Make more than you consume. Writing, building, teaching, gardening and cooking for others all count.
  • Run the regret test once a quarter: "What will I regret not having tried?" Then do the smallest real version of it.
PRIORITY 06Evidence: moderate

Money: enough, then time.

Money matters more than the "$75k plateau" myth suggests. It matters most as a floor, and it goes furthest when it buys back time.

  • An adversarial collaboration between former opponents settled the debate. For most people, emotional wellbeing keeps rising with income past $100,000. But for the least happy ~20%, it rises only until about $100k and then flattens: more money doesn't fix what's actually wrong. Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers, PNAS, 2023
  • In surveys of 6,271 people in four countries, those who spent money to save time (outsourcing chores they disliked) reported higher life satisfaction. A field experiment showed the effect was causal: a time-saving purchase beat a material one. Whillans et al., PNAS, 2017

What to actually do

  • Build a cash buffer first. Financial slack is also mental slack.
  • Spend on time and shared experiences rather than things. Buy back hours from chores you hate, and put them into priorities 1–5.
  • Watch lifestyle inflation. It can lock you into work that costs you everything above this line.
PRIORITY 07Evidence: moderate

Your information diet.

In a world of synthetic media (see Part I), knowing how to check what you read has become a basic survival skill.

  • Professional fact-checkers beat both PhD historians and Stanford students at judging websites because they read laterally. They left the page almost immediately to see what other sources said about it. Asked to compare a real pediatrics association with an advocacy group using a similar name, 65% of students and 50% of historians picked the wrong one. Every fact-checker got it right, and faster. Wineburg & McGrew, Stanford, 2017/2019
  • The same Stanford group found that lateral reading can be taught quickly. It's a skill, not a talent. Stanford GSE

What to actually do

  • Leave the page to check the source. Before you believe or share something, look up who's behind it.
  • Read slow news: a weekly summary or long-form source instead of an hourly feed. Most breaking news isn't something you can act on.
  • Follow fewer, better sources, including at least one you often disagree with that argues in good faith.
  • Assume emotional virality is a warning sign. Content built to make you furious is built to be shared, not to be true.
03 · The other half of focus

What to stop focusing on.

Focusing on something means turning down everything else. These are the most common drains on attention that don't give much back.

Getting on top of everything

An 80-year life is about 4,000 weeks. You'll never clear the queue, so choose what you'll neglect. (Oliver Burkeman's Four Thousand Weeks makes this case well.)

Outrage you can't act on

Separate what you can influence from what you can't, as Epictetus did. Put your energy into the first and keep an informed peace with the second.

Status comparison online

You're comparing your unedited life with other people's highlights. Nobody wins that.

Optimizing everything

Tracking every metric can become its own distraction. The goal is a life, not a dashboard. Get the basics right and stop there.

Doom as a hobby

The large-scale risks are real (Part I is 11 chapters on them). Scrolling about them isn't action. Build, organize, vote and live well.

More stuff

People adapt quickly to new possessions. Experiences, especially shared ones, and free time last longer.

06 · Intellectual honesty

What the evidence can and can't tell you.

Much of it is correlational. Healthy people socialize more, happy people report more purpose, and fit people may have been healthier to begin with. Randomized trials exist for some findings (phone blocking, deactivation, buying time) and not for others.

Samples skew toward rich Western countries. The meal-sharing and loneliness data are global. Much of the rest isn't.

Effect sizes are disputed, especially for social media, where serious researchers disagree in public and in good faith.

Averages aren't you. An introvert needs fewer, deeper ties. A shift worker can't have a perfect sleep schedule. A caregiver's "bigger than me" project may already fill the week.

So why be confident at all? Because independent lines of evidence converge. Longitudinal cohorts, meta-analyses, randomized trials, cross-cultural surveys and 2,500 years of philosophy all point to the same few things: people, presence, a body that works and a purpose outside yourself. When evidence from very different methods agrees, it deserves more weight than any single study.

If you remember one thing

Guard your attention. Spend it on people, on a body that carries you, and on work that uses your own mind for something beyond yourself, roughly in that order. Everything else in the modern world is competing to spend it for you.

"Once men turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free. But that only permitted other men with machines to enslave them." Frank Herbert, Dune (1965)
