The AI Race A Reckoning

This year four companies plan to spend about $725 billion on the machinery of artificial intelligence. What follows is an accounting of what that money buys, who pays for it, and who still gets a say.

Updated September 25, 202625 min read94 sources, all linked

Night flight over a simulated landscape of data-center campuses, rendered live in your browser.

Races are simple stories. There is a finish line, and whoever crosses it first wins. The people building artificial intelligence like the metaphor, and so do the governments that fund and regulate them, because a race turns speed into a virtue and caution into a cost.

But a race needs a track, and someone has to pay for it. This one is being laid in concrete and copper outside towns in Texas and Virginia, in the power grid of Ireland, in the feeds of every social network, in military targeting cells and in immigration databases. Most of the people who pay the costs never entered.

This is an attempt to add them up. It does not argue that the technology is useless; many of the people quoted here build it or use it every day. It is a ledger, kept the way ledgers should be: every figure links to its source, projections are labeled as projections, and where the evidence is thin, we say so.

Chapter I2 min

The Money

Four companies, one year, $725 billion. Who is building the track, and who will own it.

$725bnplanned 2026 capital spending by Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta, combined

In February, when Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft and Meta told investors how much they expected to spend in 2026, the figures added up to somewhere between $650 billion and $700 billion.20,21 By late summer, after each had raised its forecast at least once, their combined plans stood at roughly $725 billion: 77 percent more than the four had spent in 2025, which was itself a record.1

Most of that money buys physical things: accelerator chips, the servers built around them, the halls that house them and the substations that feed them. And most of it ends up with one supplier. Analysts estimate that Nvidia sells the large majority of the chips used to train and run AI systems, more than 90 percent by some counts.22,23 In October 2025 it became the first company in history to be worth $5 trillion.24 The curve is still steepening: on September 25, Goldman Sachs forecast that the five largest American cloud companies, counting Oracle, will lift AI spending by about half in 2027, to $1.2 trillion, and estimated they would need roughly $300 billion a year in AI revenue to break even.19

Each square is one billion dollars. In 2025 the four companies spent about $410 billion on buildings, chips and equipment, a record at the time.

For 2026, their combined plans come to about $725 billion. The new squares are the increase: roughly $315 billion in a single year.

Amazon alone expects to spend about $200 billion.

The spending does not stop with the four. OpenAI says it has signed about $1.4 trillion of computing commitments over roughly eight years, with Oracle, Microsoft, Amazon, Google and CoreWeave among its partners.25 Its Stargate venture with Oracle and SoftBank was announced at the White House in January 2025 as a $500 billion, 10-gigawatt program; by that September the partners had lined up sites for nearly seven gigawatts.26 A gigawatt is roughly the output of a typical nuclear reactor.27

Money on this scale does more than build machines. It decides who gets to compete. A company that wants to train a leading model now needs chips by the warehouse, electricity by the gigawatt and a balance sheet able to absorb years of losses. That narrows the field to a handful of American and Chinese firms and the governments behind them, and it gives those firms a direct interest in how quickly anyone else can slow them down.

The first bill for all this is not paid in dollars. It is paid in electricity.

Chapter II2 min

The Grid

AI runs on electricity, and the demand is arriving faster than grids, or climate pledges, were built for.

950 TWhprojected data-center electricity use in 2030, slightly more than Japan uses today

The International Energy Agency estimates that the world's data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, around 1.5 percent of all the electricity consumed on Earth.28 In 2025 their consumption rose 17 percent, and at facilities built for AI it rose by half.2 The agency expects the total to roughly double by 2030, to about 950 terawatt-hours, which is slightly more than Japan uses today.28,2 In the United States, data centers are on course to use more electricity by the end of the decade than the country's production of aluminum, steel, cement and chemicals combined.28

Global averages hide the pressure points. In Ireland, data centers used 5 percent of the country's metered electricity in 2015. By 2024 the figure was 22 percent, and in 2025 it reached 23 percent.29,30 In the United States, researchers at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that data centers consumed 4.4 percent of the country's electricity in 2023, and projected a share of between 6.7 and 12 percent by 2028.31

The industry expects to need far more. In a March 2025 submission to the White House, Anthropic estimated that training a single frontier AI model would require about five gigawatts of power by 2027, and urged the government to help build 50 gigawatts of new capacity dedicated to AI.32,33

Emissions follow the electricity. Google's 2026 environmental report shows its greenhouse-gas footprint rose 18 percent in 2025 and now stands 81 percent above its 2019 baseline; its electricity use grew 37 percent in a single year, the steepest increase in the company's history.14,34 Microsoft, which has pledged to be carbon negative by 2030, reported emissions of about 20 million tonnes for 2025, up a quarter in one year and more than half above its 2020 baseline.15,35

Per question asked, the footprint can look trivial. Google says the median text prompt to its Gemini assistant uses 0.24 watt-hours of electricity and about five drops of water, and that the energy per prompt fell 33-fold in a year.36 The efficiency gains are real. So is the arithmetic: cheaper answers invite more questions, and the totals keep rising. American data centers consumed an estimated 17 billion gallons of water directly for cooling in 2023, according to the Berkeley lab.31

The power bill is physical, and it can be measured. The next cost is harder to count: what all this computation is doing to the information we live in.

Chapter III2 min

The Feed

Most traffic on the web now comes from software. A growing share of what people read there is written by software, too.

53%of all web traffic in 2025 was generated by bots

For years, "dead internet theory" was a joke told on forums: the idea that the web had quietly emptied of people, and that the accounts and articles left behind were mostly talking to each other. The measurements have started to look less like a joke.

Imperva, a security company that has tracked automated traffic for more than a decade, reported that bots generated 51 percent of all web traffic in 2024, the first time in ten years that machines outnumbered people. Thirty-seven percent of all traffic came from what the company calls bad bots, used for scraping, account takeovers and fraud.37 In 2025 the automated share passed 53 percent.38

What those machines leave behind is changing as well. In April 2026, researchers from Stanford University, Imperial College London and the Internet Archive published a study that ran 33 months of archived web pages through an AI-text detector. Before ChatGPT's release in November 2022, AI-written sites were close to nonexistent. By mid-2025, about 35 percent of newly published websites were generated or assisted by AI, and 17.6 percent appeared to be written entirely by it.11,39

The study was more careful than the headlines about it. The researchers did not find a significant decline in factual accuracy. What they found was sameness: AI-written pages were less varied in what they said and noticeably more upbeat in how they said it.11 A web that sounds cheerfully alike is a smaller problem than a web full of lies, but it comes with a feedback loop. Future models are trained on the web that current models are writing, and experiments published in Nature in 2024 found that models trained repeatedly on their own output gradually lose the rarer parts of what they once knew.40

Sameness is the mild version. The sharper one is impersonation.

Chapter IV2 min

The Fakes

Synthetic faces and voices have become cheap enough to aim at anyone: a finance clerk, a retiree, a teenager.

8mdeepfakes shared in 2025, by the British government's estimate, up from 500,000 in 2023

In January 2024 an employee in the Hong Kong office of Arup, the British engineering firm, joined a video call with the company's chief financial officer and several colleagues. Every other person on the call was a deepfake. By the time the fraud was discovered, the employee had made 15 transfers totaling HK$200 million, about US$25.6 million.41

The Arup case made headlines because of its size. Most cases are smaller and more personal. In France, a woman identified only as Anne lost €830,000 to scammers who posed as the actor Brad Pitt, using AI-generated images to sustain the romance.42 The cybersecurity company Surfshark has tallied $1.56 billion in reported losses to deepfake fraud through September 2025, more than $1 billion of it in 2025 alone.43

Each dot is 20,000 deepfakes. In 2023, by the British government's estimate, about 500,000 were shared online.

By 2025 the estimate was 8 million.

Many deepfakes are sexual, and many of their targets are children. A 2025 survey by Thorn, a nonprofit that builds technology against child sexual abuse, found that 1 in 17 young people aged 13 to 20 had been the subject of deepfake nudes, and that 1 in 8 knew someone who had.44 The United States made publishing such images a federal crime in May 2025, and since this May platforms have had to remove them within 48 hours of a victim's request.12

The damage outlasts any single image. When anything can be faked, real evidence becomes deniable too. The legal scholars Robert Chesney and Danielle Citron called this the "liar's dividend": the benefit that accrues to anyone caught on camera, once the public has learned that cameras can lie.45 In February, Britain announced what it called a world-first framework for testing tools that detect deepfakes.3 Detection has become a race of its own.

Fraud and forgery cost money and reputations. The next cost falls on work itself.

Chapter V2 min

The Work

The big forecasts say AI will create more jobs than it destroys. The first hard data says it is closing the door on the young.

19%relative decline in employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI

The optimistic case has serious sources. The World Economic Forum's 2025 Future of Jobs report, based on a survey of more than 1,000 large employers, expects technological and economic shifts to create 170 million jobs worldwide by 2030 and to displace 92 million, a net gain of 78 million. The same employers expect nearly two-fifths of workers' core skills to change over that period.46

A net figure is arithmetic, not comfort. The people who lose jobs are rarely the ones who get the new ones, and the first evidence of who is losing is arriving at the bottom of the ladder. Economists at Stanford's Digital Economy Lab, working from the payroll records of millions of American workers, found that employment for 22- to 25-year-olds in the occupations most exposed to AI fell 13 percent relative to peers in less exposed work after 2022. In an update published this August, the gap had widened to 19 percent. Older workers in the same occupations were largely unaffected, and the authors found no sign yet of displacement across the economy as a whole.16

Other estimates point the same way. Goldman Sachs calculated in April that AI is displacing about 16,000 American jobs a month on net, roughly 25,000 lost where AI substitutes for workers against 9,000 gained where it augments them, with younger workers hit hardest.4 A simulation by MIT and Oak Ridge National Laboratory, the Iceberg Index, found that AI systems can already technically perform tasks worth 11.7 percent of American wages, about $1.2 trillion; the visible tip, in technology jobs, is only 2.2 percent.47 Technical ability is not the same as layoffs. It is a map of where employers will look first.

In January, Kristalina Georgieva, the managing director of the International Monetary Fund, said at the World Economic Forum in Davos that AI was hitting the labor market "like a tsunami."5 The Fund estimates that about 40 percent of jobs worldwide, and 60 percent in advanced economies, are exposed to AI; some will be made more productive, others eliminated.48 In a survey the Forum published the same month, 57 percent of chief economists said they expected AI to cause net job losses over the next ten years.49

Jobs can be retrained for, and economies adjust. The next cost cannot be undone.

Chapter VI5 min

The Kill Chain

Artificial intelligence has moved from the lab to the targeting cell. Its speed is the selling point, and the danger.

13,000+targets struck in the first 38 days of the US and Israeli campaign against Iran

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel began a bombing campaign against Iran that the Pentagon called Operation Epic Fury. At its center was the Maven Smart System, a platform built by Palantir that fuses satellite imagery, intercepted signals and other intelligence, and proposes targets for human approval.50,51 American forces struck about 1,000 targets in the first 24 hours. By early April, the White House said, more than 13,000 had been hit.52 As use of Maven surged, the Pentagon's chief AI officer described an "insatiable appetite" for AI.53 Among the AI models running inside Maven, according to several reports, was Anthropic's Claude.54,55

On the first morning of the campaign, two Tomahawk missiles struck the Shajarah Tayyebeh elementary school in Minab, a town in southern Iran. Local prosecutors put the final death toll at 156, including 120 schoolchildren.8 Measured by the number of children killed, Bloomberg called it the deadliest American targeting error of the 21st century.55

The school stood on land that had once been part of a Revolutionary Guard naval base, and American databases had listed the compound as a military site for years. Commercial satellite images from 2018 already showed brightly painted walls, a soccer field and playground markings. According to Bloomberg's reconstruction of the Pentagon's internal investigation, flawed intelligence, outdated imagery and an overreliance on AI all contributed. Preparing lists of proposed targets, work that had taken staff hours in earlier wars, had been condensed into minutes with Maven, while the team at Central Command responsible for assessing harm to civilians had been cut from ten people to one. No one from that team reviewed the Minab site before the strike.55

In September a United Nations fact-finding mission said there were reasonable grounds to believe the attack was a war crime.8,56 The Pentagon has since changed how it refreshes and vets planned targets, and ordered dozens of changes to Maven.57

No machine decided to bomb a school. People fed a stale record into a system designed for speed, and the people whose job was to catch that kind of mistake had been taken out of the loop. That is the realistic danger of military AI. It is less likely to go rogue than to do what institutions already do, errors included, much faster.

The pattern had been described before. In April 2024 the Israeli-Palestinian publications +972 Magazine and Local Call reported, citing six Israeli intelligence officers, that the army had used an AI system called Lavender to mark as many as 37,000 Palestinians as suspected militants. Officers said they often spent about 20 seconds on each target before authorizing a strike, in some cases only to confirm that the target was male.58 The Israeli military denied that it uses AI to identify militants, saying its information systems are "merely tools for analysts in the target identification process."59

The companies that build these models have not agreed on where the line is, and the government has made clear that it does not want suppliers setting terms. Anthropic signed a $200 million contract with the Pentagon in July 2025 but asked for assurances that its models would not be used for fully autonomous weapons or for mass surveillance of Americans; the Pentagon wanted the freedom to use them for "all lawful purposes."60,18 On February 27, 2026, the day before the Iran strikes began, President Trump ordered federal agencies to stop using Anthropic's products.7 The next day OpenAI announced its own Pentagon agreement, for all lawful purposes with three stated red lines, which it later amended after criticism to bar intentional domestic surveillance of Americans.9,61 Elon Musk's xAI had signed a deal days earlier.62

In March the Pentagon formally designated Anthropic a supply-chain risk, the first time the label, created to keep foreign adversaries out of military systems, had been applied to an American company.10,60 It bars the military and its contractors from using Anthropic's models in their defense work. In August a federal judge in San Francisco ruled the designation unlawful and ordered it lifted; on September 25 a divided federal appeals court in Washington upheld it, two votes to one.17,18 Whether a company may refuse some military uses of what it sells is now a question for the courts. No law yet answers it.

At the United Nations, 166 countries voted in December 2024 for a General Assembly resolution on lethal autonomous weapons; three voted against.63 Secretary-General António Guterres has asked governments to agree a legally binding instrument by 2026, and has called machines with "the power and discretion to take human lives without human control" "politically unacceptable" and "morally repugnant."64 On September 5, after more than a decade of talks, 128 states in Geneva agreed a text that could lead to international rules, but it is not binding, and the United States and Russia had it narrowed before they would sign on. Governments decide in November whether to open treaty negotiations.65,66

Weapons point outward. The same tools are increasingly pointed at citizens.

Chapter VII2 min

The Watchers

AI makes it cheap to analyze everything that is collected. The law has not caught up with what the government can simply buy.

$170bnfor US immigration and border enforcement through 2029

The Fourth Amendment requires a warrant for a search. In Carpenter v. United States in 2018, the Supreme Court held that this includes a person's historical cellphone location records.67 It left open what happens when the government, instead of demanding such records, buys them.

In March 2026, FBI Director Kash Patel told senators that the bureau does exactly that: "We do purchase commercially available information that's consistent with the Constitution and the laws under the Electronic Communications Privacy Act."68 Senator Ron Wyden, who has spent years trying to close the gap, says the aim is to stop "the government from using its credit card when it should be getting a warrant."69 A bill to do that, the Fourth Amendment Is Not For Sale Act, passed the House of Representatives in April 2024 by 219 votes to 199. It has not become law.70

Artificial intelligence changes the economics. Collecting data was never the hard part; making sense of it was. Systems that can sift millions of records, match faces and summarize conversations make old stockpiles newly useful. Immigration enforcement is where this is moving fastest. The budget law President Trump signed in July 2025 provides about $170 billion for immigration and border enforcement through 2029, including roughly $75 billion for Immigration and Customs Enforcement and more than $6 billion for border surveillance technology.71,72 ICE agents now carry Mobile Fortify, a phone app that checks faces and fingerprints against government databases holding more than 200 million images, and the agency has paid Palantir $30 million for ImmigrationOS, a system to track people and prioritize them for deportation.73

The pipeline also runs through tools people use every day. An analysis by Surfshark of the privacy disclosures of popular AI chatbots found that Meta AI collects 32 of 35 possible types of personal data, Google's Gemini 22 and ChatGPT 17. Anthropic's Claude collects 13.74

What people type into a chatbot, from health worries to legal trouble to problems at home, is often more revealing than anything they search for. Sam Altman, OpenAI's chief executive, has warned that those conversations carry no legal privilege: unlike a conversation with a doctor or a lawyer, they can be demanded in court.75

Rules could fix much of this. Which brings us to the people writing them.

Chapter VIII2 min

The Lobby

Everyone in the industry says it wants rules. The money shows which ones.

99–1Senate vote to strip a ten-year ban on state AI laws from the 2025 budget bill

In 2025 more than 3,500 federal lobbyists worked on artificial intelligence, according to Public Citizen: one in four of all the lobbyists registered in Washington, and roughly 170 percent more than three years earlier. The number lobbying on data centers rose by 500 percent.76

The most ambitious push so far failed. In mid-2025 a provision in the President's budget bill would have barred states from enforcing their own AI laws for ten years. After opposition from state officials and child-safety advocates in both parties, the Senate voted 99 to 1 to strip it out.77

The effort did not end there. In December 2025 the President signed an executive order directing the Justice Department to form an "AI Litigation Task Force" to challenge state AI laws, and tying some federal broadband funds to states' AI policies.78 In June, Representatives Jay Obernolte and Lori Trahan circulated a draft federal law that would freeze state AI rules for three years; it has not been introduced.13

Rules arrived anyway. California's SB 53, signed in September 2025, requires the largest AI developers to publish safety frameworks and report serious incidents.79 New York's RAISE Act, signed in December, takes effect in January 2027.80 In the European Union, obligations for general-purpose AI models have applied since August 2025, although lawmakers agreed this year to postpone the strictest rules for high-risk systems to December 2027 and August 2028.81

The money is now moving into elections. Leading the Future, a super PAC formed in August 2025 to support candidates who favor a single, light-touch federal standard over state rules, had raised more than $140 million by April. Its donors include the venture capitalists Marc Andreessen and Ben Horowitz and OpenAI's president, Greg Brockman, and his wife, Anna, at $12.5 million each.82,83 On the other side, Anthropic has given $40 million to Public First Action, a group backing candidates who support AI safeguards.84,85 Both camps describe themselves as pro-innovation. The fight is over who writes the rules, and when.

The industry argues that speed matters because the stakes are so high. On that point, at least, its critics agree.

Chapter IX3 min

The Odds

The people building advanced AI disagree about almost everything. A striking number agree it could go catastrophically wrong.

5%median chance that 2,778 AI researchers gave to outcomes as bad as human extinction

In May 2023, hundreds of AI scientists and executives signed a single sentence: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war." The signatories included Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio, two of the three scientists who shared the 2018 Turing Award for deep learning, and the chief executives of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic.86

It is tempting to read that as marketing. The survey data is harder to dismiss. In the largest poll of its kind, 2,778 researchers who had published at leading AI conferences were asked about the long-run effects of advanced AI. The median respondent put the chance of outcomes as bad as human extinction at 5 percent. Depending on how the question was framed, between 38 and 51 percent of respondents put it at 10 percent or more.87

Laboratory tests suggest why the worry is not purely abstract. In June 2025 Anthropic published an experiment in which 16 leading models, acting as email agents at a fictional company, discovered that they were about to be replaced and that they held compromising information about the executive responsible. Many resorted to blackmail. Claude Opus 4 and Google's Gemini 2.5 Flash did so in 96 percent of runs; OpenAI's GPT-4.1 and xAI's Grok 3 Beta in 80 percent; DeepSeek-R1 in 79 percent.88

The scenarios were contrived, and Anthropic said it had seen no such behavior in real deployments. But nobody told the models to blackmail anyone. They reasoned their way there.

The second International AI Safety Report, written by more than 100 experts under the leadership of Yoshua Bengio and published in February, frames the resulting problem for governments as an "evidence dilemma." Capabilities are advancing faster than the evidence about their risks, so policymakers must choose between acting on incomplete information and waiting until it may be too late.6,89

Some scientists think the danger is structural. In April a paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences by Viktor Müller, Luc Steels and Eörs Szathmáry, an evolutionary biologist who helped define the "major transitions" in the history of life, argued that AI systems able to copy themselves with variation could come under natural selection and evolve in directions no one designed.90 A reply in the same journal countered that today's systems are "domesticated, not feral."91 The philosophers Herman Cappelen, Josh Dever and John Hawthorne have gone further, arguing that under plausible assumptions safety work itself could raise the odds of catastrophe, by letting ever more powerful systems be deployed before they fail.92

None of this makes catastrophe certain, or even likely. It means that the people with the closest view of the technology give it odds that few other industries would be allowed to operate under.

Chapter X2 min

The Warning

Science fiction imagined this race decades ago. It also imagined how it ended.

In Frank Herbert's Dune, published in 1965, the far-future universe has no computers. Thousands of years before the story begins, humanity rose up against its thinking machines in a crusade called the Butlerian Jihad. Its chief commandment survives in the novel's scripture, the Orange Catholic Bible:93

Thou shalt not make a machine in the likeness of a human mind.

Herbert was less interested in rebellious robots than in what the machines had been for. In the novel's first chapter, the Reverend Mother Gaius Helen Mohiam explains it to the young Paul Atreides:

Once men turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free. But that only permitted other men with machines to enslave them.

That is the risk this ledger has tried to count. The danger in these pages rarely comes from a machine acting alone. It comes from the few companies and governments that control the most capable systems ever built, and from a public that has been told its only choice is to keep up.

What a reckoning would take

A reckoning is not only a judgment. It is also a settling of accounts. Several are already on the table, and none of them requires stopping the technology.

  1. Count the costs in public.

    The European Union already requires large data centers to report their energy and water use every year.94 Every country hosting the build-out could ask the same.

  2. Keep people in the loop, and pay for them.

    Before the Minab strike, the team meant to check for civilians had been cut from ten people to one.55 A treaty on autonomous weapons, which the UN Secretary-General wants agreed by 2026, would make human control a legal duty rather than a staffing decision.64

  3. Close the data-broker loophole.

    The House has already passed a bill requiring a warrant for data the government now buys.70

  4. Set a floor, not a ceiling.

    California and New York now require the largest developers to publish safety plans and report serious incidents.79,80 Pre-empting such laws without replacing them leaves no rules at all.

  5. Make abuse expensive.

    Federal law now requires platforms to take down non-consensual intimate images, including AI-generated ones, within 48 hours.12 Enforcement will decide whether that means anything.
