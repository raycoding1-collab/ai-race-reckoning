IV第四

無為

Doing Nothing

The teachings: water, emptiness, softness and the uncarved block.

The Daodejing is short, rhymed in places and often cryptic. It gives advice more than arguments, and much of the advice is for rulers. Its teachings fit on a handful of leaves. Each one lists the chapters where it appears; the numbers open the book at the end of this page.

無為

wúwéi Non-action

Acting without forcing. Letting things take their course, and doing only what the situation itself asks. It is the book’s most characteristic idea, and it does not mean idleness: The Way is ever without action, yet nothing is left undone.

Daodejing 2 3 37 43 48 57 63 64

自然

zìrán So of itself

What happens by its own nature, unforced. Even the Way follows it. When the best rulers have finished their work, the people all say, ‘It happened by itself.’

Daodejing 17 23 25 51 64

水

shuǐ Water

The book’s favorite image of virtue. Water is soft, seeks the lowest place and serves everything, yet in the end it wears down stone. Rivers and seas rule the hundred valleys because they stay below them.

Daodejing 8 32 66 78

樸

pǔ The uncarved block

Unworked wood, before it is cut into shapes and uses: simplicity before names and distinctions. When the block is split, it becomes vessels, and the sage would rather keep the wood whole.

Daodejing 15 19 28 32 37 57

虛

xū Emptiness

The hole in the hub lets the wheel turn; the space inside a pot makes it a pot. The mind, too, works best empty: Reach the utmost emptiness; hold fast to deep stillness.

Daodejing 4 5 11 16

反

fǎn Return

Opposites make each other and turn into each other. Whatever goes out comes back, and whatever reaches its peak begins to decline. Returning is the motion of the Way.

Daodejing 2 16 25 40 58 65

柔弱

róuruò Soft and weak

When people are born they are soft and weak; when they die they are hard and stiff. Suppleness is a sign of life, and the soft and weak overcome the hard and strong.

Daodejing 36 43 76 78

知足

zhīzú Knowing enough

Knowing you have enough is wealth. Disaster comes from wanting more, not from having little.

Daodejing 33 44 46

三寶

sānbǎo Three treasures

The first is compassion, the second is restraint, the third is not daring to be first in the world.

Daodejing 67

德

dé Virtue

What a thing receives from the Way: its power, its character, its particular good. The highest kind does not look like virtue at all. The highest virtue does not act virtuous, and so it has virtue.

Daodejing 38 51 55 65

玄

xuán Dark

The color of deep water and the night sky, used for whatever is too deep to see: dark virtue, the dark female, and dark, and darker still, the gate of all wonders.

Daodejing 1 6 10 56 65

不言

bù yán Without words

Those who know do not speak; those who speak do not know. The sage teaches by example. The Tang poet Bai Juyi (772–846) saw the difficulty: 若道老君是知者，緣何自著五千文？ “If Lord Lao was one who knew, why did he write five thousand words himself?”

Daodejing 2 43 56 81

Advice to a ruler

The book’s politics are radical in their mildness: fewer laws, lighter taxes, no glory in war, less cleverness all round. The more laws and orders are posted, the more thieves and robbers there are. Of the best rulers, those below know only that they are there. And, in the line most often quoted by politicians, Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish. The usual reading is that a small fish falls apart if you keep turning it. Ronald Reagan quoted the line in his State of the Union address of January 1988: “Govern a great nation as you would cook a small fish; do not overdo it.”{{fn:reagan}}

Another line has become a proverb in many languages: a journey of a thousand li begins beneath your feet. A li is a Chinese mile, about half a kilometer today.
