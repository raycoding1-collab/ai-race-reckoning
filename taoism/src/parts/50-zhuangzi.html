Zhuangzi, the tradition’s great storyteller and its most cheerful skeptic.

Master Zhuang

Zhuangzi, Master Zhuang, was Zhuang Zhou, a man of Meng in the state of Song who lived in the fourth century BCE; the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives his dates as 369–298.{{fn:iep-zhuangzi}} The book that carries his name is funnier, stranger and more argumentative than the Daodejing. It is full of stories: talking trees and skulls, a cook who carves like a dancer, a fish the size of a country that turns into a bird.

The Zhuangzi we read has 33 chapters. It is the edition of Guo Xiang (252–312), who cut an older version of 52 chapters down to size. The first seven, the Inner Chapters, have long been taken as closest to Zhuang Zhou himself. The Outer Chapters (8–22) and Miscellaneous Chapters (23–33) come from followers and other hands.{{fn:zhuangzi-book}}

Where the Daodejing advises rulers, Zhuangzi declines to rule. When the king of Chu sent two officials to hand him the government of the state, he was fishing, and did not put down his rod:

Eight stories

The themes run through the stories rather than arguments: wandering free and easy, the equality of all things, transformation, the uses of uselessness, suspicion of words and the calm acceptance of death. The painting behind this chapter shows the useless tree of the fourth story, and someone asleep beneath it.
