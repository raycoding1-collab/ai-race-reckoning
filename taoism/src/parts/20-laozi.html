II第二

老子

The Old Master

A keeper of archives, a pass in the west, and a book of five thousand characters.

A life in one short biography

Nearly everything told about Laozi comes from one brief biography by the Han court historian Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BCE), written some four centuries after Laozi was supposed to have lived.{{fn:sep-laozi,shiji63}} His surname was Li, his given name Er, and he was also called Dan. He came from the southern state of Chu and kept the archives at the court of Zhou. Confucius came to ask him about ritual and left shaken:

Seeing the Zhou dynasty in decline, Laozi left for the west. At the border pass, the keeper of the pass, Yin Xi, stopped him.

The painting behind this chapter shows that moment: an old man on a water buffalo, a road climbing to a gate. Sima Qian himself was not sure of his story. He reports that some identified Laozi with another man of Chu, Lao Laizi; that others said he was the Grand Historian Dan of Zhou, who lived more than a century after Confucius; that Laozi was said to have lived to 160, or over 200, by cultivating the Way. He ends with a shrug: 世莫知其然否, “no one in the world knows whether this is so.”{{fn:shiji63}}

Laozi means “Old Master”, which may be a title more than a name. Most historians now read the Daodejing as an anthology: sayings, rhymed verses and comments gathered over generations before they settled into one book.{{fn:sep-laozi}}

Three tombs

For two thousand years the text was known only through later copies. Then the ground began to give up older ones.

{{witnesses}}

The bamboo slips from Guodian, in Hubei, were found in October 1993 in a tomb dated to about 300 BCE: 71 slips in three bundles, with passages matching 31 of the 81 chapters of the received text, in a different order.{{fn:guodian}} The two silk manuscripts from Mawangdui, in Hunan, were excavated in December 1973 from a tomb sealed in 168 BCE. Both put the part on virtue before the part on the Way.{{fn:mawangdui}} A third early copy, on bamboo from the Western Han, came to Peking University in 2009 and was published in 2012, divided into 77 chapters.{{fn:beida,beida-77}}

The text most readers know, in 81 chapters, was transmitted with the commentary of Wang Bi (226–249), who died at twenty-three. Even that is not quite his: Rudolf Wagner showed that the text printed with Wang Bi’s commentary was later partly replaced with readings from other editions.{{fn:wagner}} The Chinese on this site is that received text.

Five thousand characters, two thousand translations

The received text runs to {{ddj_chars}} characters. Few books have been translated more often. Misha Tadd’s bibliography counted 2,052 translations in 97 languages by 2022.{{fn:tadd}} The earliest complete foreign version is a Latin manuscript made by Jesuit missionaries in China in the early eighteenth century.{{fn:jesuit-latin}} The first complete translation published in a Western language was Stanislas Julien’s French, in 1842,{{fn:julien}} and the first in English was John Chalmers’s, in 1868.{{fn:chalmers}}
