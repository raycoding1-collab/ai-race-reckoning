陰陽

Shade and Sun

How one thing becomes two, and two become everything.

Two sides of a hill

Look at the mountain in the painting. The sun stands to the left, so the mountain’s left face is pale with light and its right face dense with shadow. That is the oldest meaning of the two words. Both characters carry the radical 阝, a hill: 陰 yīn was the shady, northern side of a hill and 陽 yáng the sunny, southern side.{{fn:yinyang}} The pair grew to name every complementary contrast: cool and warm, dark and bright, receiving and giving, rest and motion, female and male, night and day.

Yin and yang are not good and evil. They are two phases of one process, and each turns into the other: Misfortune: good fortune leans on it. Good fortune: misfortune hides in it. Nor did the Taoists invent them. Yin and yang, qi and the five phases were the shared science of early China, which Taoist writers absorbed and made their own. The Huainanzi, a compendium presented to Emperor Wu of Han in 139 BCE, joins the Way of the Daodejing to yin-yang and five-phase cosmology.{{fn:huainanzi}}

The Supreme Ultimate

The Book of Changes, a classic shared by every Chinese school, says: 易有太極，是生兩儀, “in change there is the Supreme Ultimate, and it gives birth to the two modes.”{{fn:yijing}} In the eleventh century the philosopher Zhou Dunyi (1017–1073) drew a Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate, a column of circles that shows the limitless becoming the ultimate, dividing into yin and yang, then into the five phases, then into the ten thousand things.{{fn:zhou-dunyi}} The familiar swirl of dark and light, each holding a seed of the other, is a later simplification credited to the Ming scholar Lai Zhide (1525–1604). English speakers have called it the yin-yang symbol only since the 1960s.{{fn:taijitu}}

Five phases

Wood, fire, earth, metal and water are usually called the five elements, but 五行 wǔxíng means five movements or phases. They are stages of change, each giving rise to the next and each checking another. Wood feeds fire; fire leaves earth in its ashes; earth bears metal; metal gathers water, as dew on cold bronze; water nourishes wood. And wood parts the earth, earth dams water, water quenches fire, fire melts metal, metal cuts wood. Each phase was matched to a direction, a season and a color.{{fn:wuxing}}
