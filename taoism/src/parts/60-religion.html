VI第六

道教

The Religion

Gods, priests, scriptures and emperors: two thousand years of organized Taoism.

The old master becomes a god

By the second century CE, Laozi was worshipped. In 165 and 166 the Han emperor Huan had sacrifices offered to him at his supposed birthplace, and in 166 the emperor performed a ceremony for Laozi inside the palace itself, the only time a Chinese emperor did so. An inscription written for the occasion, the Laozi ming, treats him as an embodiment of the Dao.{{fn:laojun}} As Taishang Laojun, Lord Lao the Most High, he became one of the Three Pure Ones, the highest gods of the Taoist heavens, beside the Celestial Worthy of Primordial Beginning and the Celestial Worthy of Numinous Treasure.{{fn:three-pure}}

In 142, according to the tradition, Lord Lao appeared to Zhang Daoling on Mount Heming in Sichuan and made him the first Celestial Master. The community he founded was organized into parishes and asked each household for five pecks of rice, hence its other name, the Way of the Five Pecks of Rice. It ruled a small theocratic state until 215, when Zhang’s grandson Zhang Lu submitted to the warlord Cao Cao and the faithful were resettled across China, carrying the religion with them.{{fn:five-pecks,tianshi}}

Revelations

New scriptures kept arriving from the heavens. Between 364 and 370 a medium named Yang Xi received visits from the Perfected of the Heaven of Highest Clarity, Shangqing, on Mount Mao, near Nanjing. The scholar Tao Hongjing (456–536) later gathered Yang’s manuscripts into the Declarations of the Perfected.{{fn:yang-xi,tao-hongjing}} Around 400 Ge Chaofu issued the Lingbao, Numinous Treasure, scriptures, which reshaped Taoist ritual; the master Lu Xiujing catalogued them in 437, and in 471 presented the throne with a catalogue of all Taoist scripture arranged in Three Caverns, the division that still organizes the Taoist canon.{{fn:lingbao,lu-xiujing}}

Emperors and the Way

Taoism became a state religion more than once. In 442 the Northern Wei emperor Taiwu received Taoist registers in a ceremony led by the reformer Kou Qianzhi.{{fn:kou-qianzhi}} The Tang imperial family, surnamed Li, claimed Laozi, born Li Er, as its ancestor; in 666 Emperor Gaozong gave him the title Supremely Mysterious and Primordial Emperor.{{fn:gaozong}} Emperor Xuanzong wrote his own commentary on the Daodejing and put the book into the civil service examinations.{{fn:ddj-guide}} Imperial favor had a price. Court alchemists made their elixirs of cinnabar and mercury, and five Tang emperors are said to have died after taking them, four of them in the ninth century.{{fn:elixir}}

In the twelfth century, under the Jurchen Jin dynasty, Wang Chongyang founded Quanzhen, Complete Perfection, a monastic order of celibate masters devoted to inner alchemy; among his seven great disciples was a woman, Sun Bu’er.{{fn:wang-chongyang}} In 1222 one of them, the aged Qiu Chuji, reached the camp of Chinggis Khan south of the Hindu Kush after a journey of two years, and won the Khan’s favor for his order.{{fn:qiu-chuji}} The favor did not last. After court debates in the 1250s, which the Buddhists won, Khubilai Khan ordered in 1281 that all Taoist books except the Daodejing be burned.{{fn:phagpa}}

Under the Ming, Taoism regained imperial favor. Emperor Zhu Di turned Mount Wudang into an imperial temple complex for the martial god Zhenwu, today a World Heritage site,{{fn:wudang}} and in 1445 the court printed the Taoist canon, the Daozang: 1,426 texts in 5,305 volumes, with a supplement of 50 more texts in 1607.{{fn:daozang}}

Taoism today

Taoist clergy today belong to two broad orders. Quanzhen is monastic: its priests and nuns are celibate and vegetarian and live in temples such as the White Cloud Temple in Beijing. Zhengyi, Orthodox Unity, descends from the Celestial Masters: its priests may marry, live at home and serve local communities, performing rites for the living and the dead.{{fn:schools,quanzhen}}

Counting Taoists is hard, because most people who burn incense at a Taoist temple would not call themselves Taoist. China’s government reported about 9,000 Taoist temples and more than 40,000 Taoist clergy in 2018.{{fn:whitepaper}} In survey data analyzed by the Pew Research Center, 18 percent of Chinese adults say they believe in Taoist deities.{{fn:pew-china}} In Taiwan about a quarter of adults identify as Taoist, roughly as many as identify as Buddhist.{{fn:pew-taiwan}}
