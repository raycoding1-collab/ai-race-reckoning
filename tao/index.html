I第一

道

The Way

A word for a road that came to name the source of everything.

A road that speaks

The character 道 dào sets the sign for walking, 辶, beside 首, a head: someone going along a road, or leading the way.1 In Chinese it still means a road, a path, a method, and also to say. The first line of the Daodejing uses both senses at once. The Way that can be way-ed, walked or put into words, is not the constant Way.

Every school had a way

In the centuries before the first empire, every school of thought claimed to know the dao. For Confucius and his followers it was the way of the ancient sage kings: ritual, duty and good government. The Daodejing reaches further back. Its Way comes before heaven and earth, cannot be named, and pays no attention to human purposes.

Straw dogs were ritual effigies. The Zhuangzi describes how they were kept in boxes and wrapped in embroidered cloth before a sacrifice, then trampled by passers-by and burned for kindling once it was over.5 Heaven and earth are not cruel. They simply do not hold on.

Taoism, Daoism, and the two words behind them

Tao and Dao are the same word, 道, spelled in two systems of romanization: the older Wade–Giles and pinyin, the system used in China today. This site uses pinyin for Chinese names and terms (Dao, Laozi, Zhuangzi) and keeps the tradition’s most familiar English name, Taoism.

English uses one word where Chinese sources use two. Daojia 道家, the house or lineage of the Way, began as a label in a survey of the schools of thought by the Han historian Sima Tan, who died in 110 BCE.6 Daojiao 道教, the teaching of the Way, names the organized religion with its priests, rites and scriptures. For a long time Western scholars split these into a pure early philosophy and a later, corrupted religion. Most specialists now reject the split. The Taoist canon never made it, and Taoists recited, practiced and commented on the old books for two thousand years.7,8

II第二

老子

The Old Master

A keeper of archives, a pass in the west, and a book of five thousand characters.

A life in one short biography

Nearly everything told about Laozi comes from one brief biography by the Han court historian Sima Qian (c. 145–86 BCE), written some four centuries after Laozi was supposed to have lived.9,10 His surname was Li, his given name Er, and he was also called Dan. He came from the southern state of Chu and kept the archives at the court of Zhou. Confucius came to ask him about ritual and left shaken:

Seeing the Zhou dynasty in decline, Laozi left for the west. At the border pass, the keeper of the pass, Yin Xi, stopped him.

The painting behind this chapter shows that moment: an old man on a water buffalo, a road climbing to a gate. Sima Qian himself was not sure of his story. He reports that some identified Laozi with another man of Chu, Lao Laizi; that others said he was the Grand Historian Dan of Zhou, who lived more than a century after Confucius; that Laozi was said to have lived to 160, or over 200, by cultivating the Way. He ends with a shrug: 世莫知其然否, “no one in the world knows whether this is so.”10

Laozi means “Old Master”, which may be a title more than a name. Most historians now read the Daodejing as an anthology: sayings, rhymed verses and comments gathered over generations before they settled into one book.9

Three tombs

For two thousand years the text was known only through later copies. Then the ground began to give up older ones.

The bamboo slips from Guodian, in Hubei, were found in October 1993 in a tomb dated to about 300 BCE: 71 slips in three bundles, with passages matching 31 of the 81 chapters of the received text, in a different order.11 The two silk manuscripts from Mawangdui, in Hunan, were excavated in December 1973 from a tomb sealed in 168 BCE. Both put the part on virtue before the part on the Way.3 A third early copy, on bamboo from the Western Han, came to Peking University in 2009 and was published in 2012, divided into 77 chapters.12,14

The text most readers know, in 81 chapters, was transmitted with the commentary of Wang Bi (226–249), who died at twenty-three. Even that is not quite his: Rudolf Wagner showed that the text printed with Wang Bi’s commentary was later partly replaced with readings from other editions.13 The Chinese on this site is that received text.

Five thousand characters, two thousand translations

The received text runs to 5,290 characters. No work of philosophy has been translated more often. Misha Tadd’s bibliography counted 2,052 translations in 97 languages by 2022.15 The earliest complete foreign version is a Latin manuscript made by Jesuit missionaries in China in the early eighteenth century.16 The first complete translation published in a Western language was Stanislas Julien’s French, in 1842,17 and the first in English was John Chalmers’s, in 1868.18

III第三

陰陽

Shade and Sun

How one thing becomes two, and two become everything.

Two sides of a hill

Look at the mountain in the painting. The sun stands to the left, so the mountain’s left face is pale with light and its right face dense with shadow. That is the oldest meaning of the two words. Both characters carry the radical 阝, a hill: 陰 yīn was the shady, northern side of a hill and 陽 yáng the sunny, southern side.19 The pair grew to name every complementary contrast: cool and warm, dark and bright, receiving and giving, rest and motion, female and male, night and day.

Yin and yang are not good and evil. They are two phases of one process, and each turns into the other: Misfortune: good fortune leans on it. Good fortune: misfortune hides in it. Nor did the Taoists invent them. Yin and yang, qi and the five phases were the shared science of early China, which Taoist writers absorbed and made their own. The Huainanzi, a compendium presented to Emperor Wu of Han in 139 BCE, joins the Way of the Daodejing to yin-yang and five-phase cosmology.20

One, two, three

Chapter 42 of the Daodejing compresses the birth of the world into a count:

Breath

The character 氣 qì shows vapor, 气, rising over rice, 米. It means breath, steam, air, and the stuff everything is made of. When qi gathers, things take form; when it disperses, they dissolve. The Zhuangzi says it of people too:

The Supreme Ultimate

The Book of Changes, a classic shared by every Chinese school, says: 易有太極，是生兩儀, “in change there is the Supreme Ultimate, and it gives birth to the two modes.”21 In the eleventh century the philosopher Zhou Dunyi (1017–1073) drew a Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate, a column of circles that shows the limitless becoming the ultimate, dividing into yin and yang, then into the five phases, then into the ten thousand things.22 The familiar swirl of dark and light, each holding a seed of the other, is a later simplification credited to the Ming scholar Lai Zhide (1525–1604). English speakers have called it the yin-yang symbol only since the 1960s.23

Five phases

Wood, fire, earth, metal and water are usually called the five elements, but 五行 wǔxíng means five movements or phases. They are stages of change, each giving rise to the next and each checking another. Wood feeds fire; fire leaves earth in its ashes; earth bears metal; metal gathers water, as dew on cold bronze; water nourishes wood. And wood parts the earth, earth dams water, water quenches fire, fire melts metal, metal cuts wood. Each phase was matched to a direction, a season and a color.24

IV第四

無為

Doing Nothing

The teachings: water, emptiness, softness and the uncarved block.

The Daodejing is short, rhymed in places and often cryptic. It gives advice more than arguments, and much of the advice is for rulers. Its teachings fit on a handful of leaves. Each one lists the chapters where it appears; the numbers open the book at the end of this page.

無為

wúwéi Non-action

Acting without forcing. Letting things take their course, and doing only what the situation itself asks. It is the book’s most characteristic idea, and it does not mean idleness: The Way is ever without action, yet nothing is left undone.

Daodejing 2 3 37 43 48 57 63 64

自然

zìrán So of itself

What happens by its own nature, unforced. Even the Way follows it. When the best rulers have finished their work, the people all say, ‘It happened by itself.’

Daodejing 17 23 25 51 64

水

shuǐ Water

The book’s favorite image of virtue. Water is soft, seeks the lowest place and serves everything, yet in the end it wears down stone. Rivers and seas rule the hundred valleys because they stay below them.

Daodejing 8 32 66 78

樸

pǔ The uncarved block

Unworked wood, before it is cut into shapes and uses: simplicity before names and distinctions. When the block is split, it becomes vessels, and the sage would rather keep the wood whole.

Daodejing 15 19 28 32 37 57

虛

xū Emptiness

The hole in the hub lets the wheel turn; the space inside a pot makes it a pot. The mind, too, works best empty: Reach the utmost emptiness; hold fast to deep stillness.

Daodejing 4 5 11 16

反

fǎn Return

Opposites make each other and turn into each other. Whatever goes out comes back, and whatever reaches its peak begins to decline. Returning is the motion of the Way.

Daodejing 2 16 25 40 58 65

柔弱

róuruò Soft and weak

When people are born they are soft and weak; when they die they are hard and stiff. Suppleness is a sign of life, and the soft and weak overcome the hard and strong.

Daodejing 36 43 76 78

知足

zhīzú Knowing enough

Knowing you have enough is wealth. Disaster comes from wanting more, not from having little.

Daodejing 33 44 46

三寶

sānbǎo Three treasures

The first is compassion, the second is restraint, the third is not daring to be first in the world.

Daodejing 67

德

dé Virtue

What a thing receives from the Way: its power, its character, its particular good. The highest kind does not look like virtue at all. The highest virtue does not act virtuous, and so it has virtue.

Daodejing 38 51 55 65

玄

xuán Dark

The color of deep water and the night sky, used for whatever is too deep to see: dark virtue, the dark female, and dark, and darker still, the gate of all wonders.

Daodejing 1 6 10 56 65

不言

bù yán Without words

Those who know do not speak; those who speak do not know. The sage teaches by example. The Tang poet Bai Juyi (772–846) saw the difficulty: 若道老君是知者，緣何自著五千文？ “If Lord Lao was one who knew, why did he write five thousand words himself?”

Daodejing 2 43 56 81

Advice to a ruler

The book’s politics are radical in their mildness: fewer laws, lighter taxes, no glory in war, less cleverness all round. The more laws and orders are posted, the more thieves and robbers there are. Of the best rulers, those below know only that they are there. And, in the line most often quoted by politicians, Govern a great state as you would cook a small fish. The usual reading is that a small fish falls apart if you keep turning it. Ronald Reagan quoted the line in his State of the Union address of January 1988: “Govern a great nation as you would cook a small fish; do not overdo it.”25

Another line has become a proverb in many languages: a journey of a thousand li begins beneath your feet. A li is a Chinese mile, about half a kilometer today.

V第五

莊子

The Butterfly

Zhuangzi, the tradition’s great storyteller and its most cheerful skeptic.

Master Zhuang

Zhuangzi, Master Zhuang, was Zhuang Zhou, a man of Meng in the state of Song who lived in the fourth century BCE; the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives his dates as 369–298.27 The book that carries his name is funnier, stranger and more argumentative than the Daodejing. It is full of stories: talking trees and skulls, a cook who carves like a dancer, a fish the size of a country that turns into a bird.

The Zhuangzi we read has 33 chapters. It is the edition of Guo Xiang (252–312), who cut an older version of 52 chapters down to size. The first seven, the Inner Chapters, have long been taken as closest to Zhuang Zhou himself. The Outer Chapters (8–22) and Miscellaneous Chapters (23–33) come from followers and other hands.28

Where the Daodejing advises rulers, Zhuangzi declines to rule. When the king of Chu sent two officials to hand him the government of the state, he was fishing, and did not put down his rod:

Eight stories

The themes run through the stories rather than arguments: wandering free and easy, the equality of all things, transformation, the uses of uselessness, suspicion of words and the calm acceptance of death. The painting behind this chapter shows the useless tree of the fourth story, and someone asleep beneath it.

北冥有魚，其名為鯤。鯤之大，不知其幾千里也；化而為鳥，其名為鵬。

鵬之背，不知其幾千里也；怒而飛，其翼若垂天之雲。

The great fish and the great bird

In the dark sea of the north there is a fish called Kun. Kun is so big that no one knows how many thousand li it measures. It changes into a bird called Peng. Peng's back is so broad that no one knows how many thousand li it spans. When Peng rises in flight, its wings are like clouds hanging from the sky.

Zhuangzi 1, 逍遙遊30

昔者莊周夢為胡蝶，栩栩然胡蝶也，自喻適志與！不知周也。

俄然覺，則蘧蘧然周也。

不知周之夢為胡蝶與，胡蝶之夢為周與？

周與胡蝶，則必有分矣。此之謂物化。

The butterfly dream

Once Zhuang Zhou dreamed he was a butterfly, a butterfly fluttering about, happy with itself and doing as it pleased. It did not know it was Zhou.

Suddenly he woke, and there he was, solidly Zhou.

He did not know whether he was Zhou who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming of being Zhou.

Between Zhou and the butterfly there must be some distinction. This is called the transformation of things.

Zhuangzi 2, 齊物論26

庖丁為文惠君解牛，手之所觸，肩之所倚，足之所履，膝之所踦，砉然嚮然，奏刀騞然，莫不中音。合於桑林之舞，乃中經首之會。

文惠君曰：「譆，善哉！技蓋至此乎？」

庖丁釋刀對曰：「臣之所好者道也，進乎技矣。始臣之解牛之時，所見無非牛者。三年之後，未嘗見全牛也。方今之時，臣以神遇而不以目視，官知止而神欲行。依乎天理，批大郤，導大窾，因其固然。……良庖歲更刀，割也；族庖月更刀，折也。今臣之刀十九年矣，所解數千牛矣，而刀刃若新發於硎。彼節者有閒，而刀刃者無厚；以無厚入有閒，恢恢乎其於遊刃必有餘地矣。……」

文惠君曰：「善哉！吾聞庖丁之言，得養生焉。」

Cook Ding carves an ox

Cook Ding was carving an ox for Lord Wenhui. Wherever his hand touched, wherever his shoulder leaned, wherever his foot stepped, wherever his knee pressed, the flesh fell away with a hiss, and the knife swished through it, never missing a beat, as if dancing the Mulberry Grove dance or keeping time to the Jingshou music.

"Ah, wonderful!" said Lord Wenhui. "Can skill really go this far?"

Cook Ding put down his knife and answered, "What I care about is the Way, which goes beyond skill. When I first began carving oxen, all I could see was the ox. After three years I no longer saw the whole ox. Now I meet it with my spirit and do not look with my eyes. My senses stop, and my spirit moves as it wishes. I follow the natural grain, cut into the great hollows, guide the blade through the great openings, going by how the thing actually is. … A good cook changes his knife once a year, because he cuts. An ordinary cook changes his knife once a month, because he hacks. I have had this knife for nineteen years and carved thousands of oxen, and its edge is as fresh as if it had just come from the whetstone. There are spaces between the joints, and the blade has no thickness. Put what has no thickness into what has space, and there is room, more than enough, for the blade to play. …"

"Wonderful!" said Lord Wenhui. "I have heard the words of Cook Ding, and learned how to nourish life."

Zhuangzi 3, 養生主31

惠子謂莊子曰：「吾有大樹，人謂之樗。其大本擁腫而不中繩墨，其小枝卷曲而不中規矩，立之塗，匠者不顧。今子之言，大而無用，眾所同去也。」

莊子曰：「……今子有大樹，患其無用，何不樹之於無何有之鄉，廣莫之野，彷徨乎無為其側，逍遙乎寢臥其下？不夭斤斧，物無害者，無所可用，安所困苦哉！」

The useless tree

Huizi said to Zhuangzi, "I have a big tree that people call a stinktree. Its trunk is too knotted and swollen for a carpenter's line; its branches are too twisted for a square or a compass. It stands by the road and no carpenter looks at it twice. Your words are the same: big and useless, and everyone turns away from them."

Zhuangzi said, "… Now, you have a big tree and you are worried that it is useless. Why not plant it in the village of Nothing-at-All, in the wide and empty wilds, where you could wander idly beside it doing nothing, and lie down to sleep beneath it, free and easy? No axe will cut its life short, nothing will harm it. If it is of no use, how can it come to grief?"

Zhuangzi 1, 逍遙遊30

南海之帝為儵，北海之帝為忽，中央之帝為渾沌。

儵與忽時相與遇於渾沌之地，渾沌待之甚善。

儵與忽謀報渾沌之德，曰：「人皆有七竅以視聽食息，此獨無有，嘗試鑿之。」

日鑿一竅，七日而渾沌死。

The death of Hundun

The emperor of the southern sea was called Shu, Brief. The emperor of the northern sea was called Hu, Sudden. The emperor of the center was called Hundun, Chaos.

Shu and Hu often met in the land of Hundun, and Hundun treated them very well.

Shu and Hu talked over how to repay Hundun's kindness. "Everyone has seven openings, to see, to hear, to eat and to breathe," they said. "This one alone has none. Let's try boring some."

Every day they bored one opening. On the seventh day, Hundun died.

Zhuangzi 7, 應帝王32

莊子與惠子遊於濠梁之上。

莊子曰：「儵魚出遊從容，是魚之樂也。」

惠子曰：「子非魚，安知魚之樂？」

莊子曰：「子非我，安知我不知魚之樂？」

惠子曰：「我非子，固不知子矣；子固非魚也，子之不知魚之樂，全矣。」

莊子曰：「請循其本。子曰『汝安知魚樂』云者，既已知吾知之而問我，我知之濠上也。」

The happiness of fish

Zhuangzi and Huizi were strolling on the bridge over the Hao river.

Zhuangzi said, "Out swim the minnows, so free and easy. That is the happiness of fish."

Huizi said, "You are not a fish. How do you know the happiness of fish?"

Zhuangzi said, "You are not me. How do you know I don't know the happiness of fish?"

Huizi said, "I am not you, so I certainly don't know about you. And you are certainly not a fish, so that settles it: you don't know the happiness of fish."

Zhuangzi said, "Let's go back to where we started. When you said, 'How do you know the happiness of fish?' you asked because you already knew that I knew it. I know it here, above the Hao."

Zhuangzi 17, 秋水29

莊子妻死，惠子弔之，莊子則方箕踞鼓盆而歌。

惠子曰：「與人居，長子老身，死不哭亦足矣，又鼓盆而歌，不亦甚乎！」

莊子曰：「不然。是其始死也，我獨何能無概然！察其始而本無生，非徒無生也而本無形，非徒無形也而本無氣。雜乎芒芴之間，變而有氣，氣變而有形，形變而有生，今又變而之死，是相與為春秋冬夏四時行也。人且偃然寢於巨室，而我噭噭然隨而哭之，自以為不通乎命，故止也。」

Drumming on a basin

When Zhuangzi's wife died, Huizi came to offer his condolences and found Zhuangzi sitting with his legs sprawled out, drumming on a basin and singing.

"She lived with you," said Huizi. "She raised your children and grew old with you. Not to weep at her death would be enough. But drumming on a basin and singing: isn't that going too far?"

"Not so," said Zhuangzi. "When she first died, how could I not grieve? But then I looked back to her beginning. Before she was born there was no life; not only no life, but no form; not only no form, but no breath. Somewhere in the blur and the haze there was a change and there was breath; the breath changed and there was form; the form changed and there was life. Now there has been another change, and she is dead. It is like the round of the four seasons, spring and autumn, winter and summer. She is lying at rest in the great chamber, and if I followed her sobbing and wailing, I would show that I understood nothing of fate. So I stopped."

Zhuangzi 18, 至樂33

荃者所以在魚，得魚而忘荃；蹄者所以在兔，得兔而忘蹄；言者所以在意，得意而忘言。

吾安得夫忘言之人而與之言哉！

The fish trap

The fish trap is there for the fish; once you have the fish, you can forget the trap. The snare is there for the rabbit; once you have the rabbit, you can forget the snare. Words are there for the meaning; once you have the meaning, you can forget the words.

Where can I find someone who has forgotten words, so that I can have a word with them?

VI第六

道教

The Religion

Gods, priests, scriptures and emperors: two thousand years of organized Taoism.

The old master becomes a god

By the second century CE, Laozi was worshipped. In 165 and 166 the Han emperor Huan had sacrifices offered to him at his supposed birthplace, and in 166 the emperor performed a ceremony for Laozi inside the palace itself, the only time a Chinese emperor did so. An inscription written for the occasion, the Laozi ming, treats him as an embodiment of the Dao.34 As Taishang Laojun, Lord Lao the Most High, he became one of the Three Pure Ones, the highest gods of the Taoist heavens, beside the Celestial Worthy of Primordial Beginning and the Celestial Worthy of Numinous Treasure.35

In 142, according to the tradition, Lord Lao appeared to Zhang Daoling on Mount Heming in Sichuan and made him the first Celestial Master. The community he founded was organized into parishes and asked each household for five pecks of rice, hence its other name, the Way of the Five Pecks of Rice. It ruled a small theocratic state until 215, when Zhang’s grandson Zhang Lu submitted to the warlord Cao Cao and the faithful were resettled across China, carrying the religion with them.36,37

Revelations

New scriptures kept arriving from the heavens. Between 364 and 370 a medium named Yang Xi received visits from the Perfected of the Heaven of Highest Clarity, Shangqing, on Mount Mao, near Nanjing. The scholar Tao Hongjing (456–536) later gathered Yang’s manuscripts into the Declarations of the Perfected.38,39 Around 400 Ge Chaofu issued the Lingbao, Numinous Treasure, scriptures, which reshaped Taoist ritual; in 471 the master Lu Xiujing catalogued them, and the division of scripture into Three Caverns that he used still organizes the Taoist canon.40,41

Emperors and the Way

Taoism became a state religion more than once. In 442 the Northern Wei emperor Taiwu received Taoist registers in a ceremony led by the reformer Kou Qianzhi.42 The Tang imperial family, surnamed Li, claimed Laozi, born Li Er, as its ancestor; in 666 Emperor Gaozong gave him the title Supremely Mysterious and Primordial Emperor.43 Emperor Xuanzong wrote his own commentary on the Daodejing and put the book into the civil service examinations.44 Imperial favor had a price. Many court alchemists made their elixirs of cinnabar and mercury, and in the ninth century five Tang emperors died after taking them.45

In the twelfth century, under the Jurchen Jin dynasty, Wang Chongyang founded Quanzhen, Complete Perfection, a monastic order of celibate masters devoted to inner alchemy; among his seven great disciples was a woman, Sun Bu’er.46 In 1222 one of them, the aged Qiu Chuji, reached the camp of Chinggis Khan south of the Hindu Kush after a journey of two years, and won the Khan’s favor for his order.47 The favor did not last. After court debates in the 1250s, which the Buddhists won, Khubilai Khan ordered in 1281 that all Taoist books except the Daodejing be burned.48

Under the Ming, Taoism regained imperial favor. Emperor Zhu Di turned Mount Wudang into an imperial temple complex for the martial god Zhenwu, today a World Heritage site,49 and in 1445 the court printed the Taoist canon, the Daozang: 1,426 texts in 5,305 volumes, with a supplement of 50 more texts in 1607.50

Taoism today

Taoist clergy today belong to two broad orders. Quanzhen is monastic: its priests and nuns are celibate and vegetarian and live in temples such as the White Cloud Temple in Beijing. Zhengyi, Orthodox Unity, descends from the Celestial Masters: its priests may marry, live at home and serve local communities, performing rites for the living and the dead.61,62

Counting Taoists is hard, because most people who burn incense at a Taoist temple would not call themselves Taoist. China’s government reported about 9,000 Taoist temples and more than 40,000 Taoist clergy in 2018.63 In survey data analyzed by the Pew Research Center, 18 percent of Chinese adults say they believe in Taoist deities.64 In Taiwan about a quarter of adults identify as Taoist, roughly as many as identify as Buddhist.65

VII第七

修煉

Cultivation

Breath, stillness, the alchemist’s furnace and the sacred mountains.

The character for an immortal, 仙 xiān, sets a person, 亻, beside a mountain, 山. Taoist practice aims at health and long life, and beyond them at transformation: a body and mind refined until they return to the Way.

Guiding and pulling

When archaeologists opened tomb 3 at Mawangdui in 1973 they found, beside the silk Laozi, a painted chart of 44 figures doing exercises of daoyin, guiding and pulling: stretching, bending and breathing to move qi through the body. It is the ancestor of today’s qigong.51 The Zhuangzi already knew the type, and was not wholly impressed:

Taijiquan, the slow, circling exercise known across the world as tai chi, joined UNESCO’s list of intangible heritage in 2020. UNESCO traces it to the mid-seventeenth century in Wenxian county, Henan.60 A later legend credits the Wudang immortal Zhang Sanfeng with inventing it.66

The furnace

Alchemists looked for immortality in the crucible. Ge Hong (283–343) gave recipes for an elixir of gold and cinnabar in his Baopuzi.53 Such elixirs were made of minerals like cinnabar, mercury sulfide, and they could kill; the emperors of the ninth century learned this the hard way.45 The same experiments led to one of history’s great accidents. A Taoist text of the ninth or tenth century warns that some alchemists who heated sulfur, realgar and saltpeter with honey burned their hands and faces, and even their houses: an early description of gunpowder. The first formula written down for use in war appears in the military manual Wujing zongyao of 1044.68,69

In time the furnace moved inside the body. Inner alchemy, neidan, treats the body as the laboratory and three of its own substances, essence (精 jīng), breath (氣 qì) and spirit (神 shén), as the ingredients. Essence is refined into breath, breath into spirit, and spirit returned to emptiness.70 Zhang Boduan’s Awakening to Reality, 81 poems written in 1075, became its classic.54 In 1929 Richard Wilhelm and Carl Jung introduced a later inner-alchemy manual, The Secret of the Golden Flower, to Western readers.71

Mountains

Taoists found paradise inside the landscape. Tang masters mapped ten great and thirty-six lesser grotto-heavens, hollow worlds inside mountains where immortals live, and seventy-two blessed lands.72 Three Taoist mountains are now World Heritage sites: Wudang, rebuilt as an imperial complex under the Ming emperor Zhu Di (inscribed 1994); Qingcheng in Sichuan, counted among the birthplaces of the religion (2000); and Sanqingshan, whose three peaks are named for the Three Pure Ones (2008).49,58,59

VIII第八

流

Downstream

How the Way flowed into painting and poetry, and west along the old road.

Mountains and water

In Chinese, landscape painting is shanshui, mountains and water. Zong Bing (375–443) wrote the earliest surviving essay on it, and saw in the painted landscape a way to the Dao.73 Its most quoted line says of mountains and rivers 質有而趣靈: their substance is real, and they reach toward the spirit. A century later Xie He put first among the principles of painting qiyun shengdong, “spirit resonance, life’s motion”, the living qi a picture must carry.74 Guo Xi’s Early Spring of 1072, now in the National Palace Museum in Taipei, is the great example of the Northern Song mountain style, and the essay attributed to him teaches the three distances: looking up at a peak, looking deep behind it, and looking level across to far hills.75

Painters left much of the silk or paper bare, and the emptiness became mist, water and sky. The paintings on this site keep the same rule: every patch of mist is untouched paper.

Poets

Li Bai (701–762), perhaps the most loved of Chinese poets, went through life with a nickname given him by the older poet He Zhizhang: the Banished Immortal, an immortal exiled from heaven for a spell among mortals.76 Retreat to the mountains, wine, moonlight and freedom from office are constant themes of Chinese poetry, and Taoist ones.

1道經 · The Way

道可道，非常道；名可名，非常名。

無名天地之始；有名萬物之母。

故常無，欲以觀其妙；常有，欲以觀其徼。

此兩者，同出而異名，同謂之玄。

玄之又玄，眾妙之門。

The Way that can be spoken of
is not the constant Way.
The name that can be named
is not the constant name.
Nameless, it is the beginning of heaven and earth;
named, it is the mother of the ten thousand things.
So, ever without desire, one sees its subtlety;
ever with desire, one sees only its edges.
These two come out together but differ in name.
Together they are called dark:
dark, and darker still,
the gate of all wonders.

The silk manuscripts from Mawangdui read 恆 héng where this text has 常 cháng, “constant”. See chapter I.

2道經 · The Way

天下皆知美之為美，斯惡已；皆知善之為善，斯不善已。

故有無相生，難易相成，長短相較，高下相傾，音聲相和，前後相隨。

是以聖人處無為之事，行不言之教；

萬物作焉而不辭，生而不有，為而不恃，功成而弗居。

夫唯弗居，是以不去。

When all under heaven know beauty as beauty,
there is already ugliness;
when all know good as good,
there is already the not-good.
So being and nonbeing give birth to each other,
hard and easy complete each other,
long and short measure each other,
high and low lean on each other,
note and voice harmonize with each other,
before and after follow each other.
Therefore the sage abides in the work of non-action
and practices the teaching without words.
The ten thousand things arise and are not turned away;
the sage gives them life without owning them,
acts without relying on it,
completes the work without dwelling in it.
Only by not dwelling in it
does the sage never lose it.

3道經 · The Way

不尚賢，使民不爭；不貴難得之貨，使民不為盜；不見可欲，使民心不亂。

是以聖人之治，虛其心，實其腹，弱其志，強其骨。

常使民無知無欲，使夫智者不敢為也。

為無為，則無不治。

Do not honor the worthy,
and the people will not compete.
Do not prize goods that are hard to get,
and the people will not steal.
Do not display what stirs desire,
and the people's hearts will not be troubled.
So the sage governs
by emptying hearts and filling bellies,
softening ambitions and strengthening bones,
always keeping the people without cunning and without craving,
so that the clever dare not act.
Do by not doing,
and nothing is left unordered.

4道經 · The Way

道沖而用之或不盈，淵兮似萬物之宗。

挫其銳，解其紛，和其光，同其塵，湛兮似或存。

吾不知誰之子，象帝之先。

The Way is an empty vessel;
use it, and it is never filled.
Fathomless, like the ancestor of the ten thousand things.
It blunts the sharp,
unties the tangled,
softens the glare,
becomes one with the dust.
Deep and clear, it seems perhaps to be there.
I do not know whose child it is;
it seems to come before the Lord on high.

5道經 · The Way

天地不仁，以萬物為芻狗；聖人不仁，以百姓為芻狗。

天地之間，其猶橐籥乎？虛而不屈，動而愈出。

多言數窮，不如守中。

Heaven and earth are not humane;
they treat the ten thousand things as straw dogs.
The sage is not humane;
the sage treats the people as straw dogs.
The space between heaven and earth,
is it not like a bellows?
Empty, yet never exhausted;
the more it moves, the more comes out.
Many words are soon used up;
better to hold to the center.

Straw dogs were ritual effigies, honored during a sacrifice and thrown away after it.

6道經 · The Way

谷神不死，是謂玄牝。

玄牝之門，是謂天地根。

綿綿若存，用之不勤。

The spirit of the valley never dies.
It is called the dark female.
The gate of the dark female
is called the root of heaven and earth.
Faint and unbroken, it seems to endure;
draw on it, and it is never used up.

7道經 · The Way

天長地久。

天地所以能長且久者，以其不自生，故能長生。

是以聖人後其身而身先，外其身而身存。

非以其無私邪？故能成其私。

Heaven is lasting, earth endures.
Heaven and earth can last and endure
because they do not live for themselves;
so they can live long.
So the sage steps back and ends up in front,
stays outside and is kept safe.
Is it not because the sage has no self to serve
that the self is fulfilled?

8道經 · The Way

上善若水。水善利萬物而不爭，處眾人之所惡，故幾於道。

居善地，心善淵，與善仁，言善信，正善治，事善能，動善時。

夫唯不爭，故無尤。

The highest good is like water.
Water benefits the ten thousand things and does not contend.
It settles where the many disdain to be,
and so it is close to the Way.
In a dwelling, the good is the ground;
in a heart, the good is depth;
in giving, the good is kindness;
in speech, the good is trust;
in governing, the good is order;
in affairs, the good is ability;
in action, the good is timing.
Because it does not contend,
it is beyond reproach.

9道經 · The Way

持而盈之，不如其已；揣而梲之，不可長保。

金玉滿堂，莫之能守；富貴而驕，自遺其咎。

功遂身退，天之道。

To hold a vessel and fill it to the brim
is not as good as stopping in time.
Hone a blade to its sharpest
and it will not keep its edge for long.
A hall full of gold and jade
cannot be guarded.
Wealth and rank with arrogance
bring their own ruin.
When the work is done, withdraw:
this is the Way of heaven.

10道經 · The Way

載營魄抱一，能無離乎？專氣致柔，能嬰兒乎？

滌除玄覽，能無疵乎？愛民治國，能無知乎？

天門開闔，能為雌乎？明白四達，能無為乎？

生之、畜之，生而不有，為而不恃，長而不宰，是謂玄德。

Carrying the soul and embracing the One,
can you keep them from parting?
Gathering the breath until it is supple,
can you be like a newborn child?
Cleansing the dark mirror,
can you leave it without a flaw?
Loving the people and ruling the state,
can you do without cleverness?
As the gates of heaven open and close,
can you play the part of the female?
Seeing clearly in all four directions,
can you do without doing?
To give birth and to nourish;
to give birth without owning,
to act without relying on it,
to lead without ruling:
this is called dark virtue.

11道經 · The Way

三十輻，共一轂，當其無，有車之用。

埏埴以為器，當其無，有器之用。

鑿戶牖以為室，當其無，有室之用。

故有之以為利，無之以為用。

Thirty spokes share one hub;
where the hub is empty, the cart is useful.
Clay is shaped into a pot;
where the pot is empty, it is useful.
Doors and windows are cut to make a room;
where the room is empty, it is useful.
So what is there gives benefit;
what is not there gives use.

The hub is the center of the wheel, where the axle passes through.

12道經 · The Way

五色令人目盲；五音令人耳聾；五味令人口爽；

馳騁畋獵，令人心發狂；難得之貨，令人行妨。

是以聖人為腹不為目，故去彼取此。

The five colors blind the eye;
the five notes deafen the ear;
the five flavors dull the palate;
racing and hunting madden the heart;
goods hard to come by lead one astray.
So the sage is for the belly and not for the eye,
lets go of that and takes this.

13道經 · The Way

寵辱若驚，貴大患若身。

何謂寵辱若驚？寵為下，得之若驚，失之若驚，是謂寵辱若驚。

何謂貴大患若身？吾所以有大患者，為吾有身，及吾無身，吾有何患？

故貴以身為天下，若可寄天下；愛以身為天下，若可託天下。

Favor and disgrace alike are alarming;
great trouble is prized like one's own body.
What does it mean that favor and disgrace are alarming?
Favor is lowly:
gaining it is alarming, losing it is alarming.
That is what it means.
What does it mean that great trouble is prized like one's body?
I have great trouble
because I have a body.
If I had no body,
what trouble could I have?
So whoever prizes their body as they would the world
can be trusted with the world;
whoever loves their body as they would the world
can be given the world to keep.

14道經 · The Way

視之不見，名曰夷；聽之不聞，名曰希；搏之不得，名曰微。

此三者不可致詰，故混而為一。

其上不皦，其下不昧，繩繩不可名，復歸於無物。

是謂無狀之狀，無物之象，是謂惚恍。

迎之不見其首，隨之不見其後。

執古之道，以御今之有。能知古始，是謂道紀。

Look, and it cannot be seen: it is called the invisible.
Listen, and it cannot be heard: it is called the inaudible.
Grasp, and it cannot be held: it is called the intangible.
These three cannot be fathomed,
and so they merge into one.
Its top is not bright;
its bottom is not dark.
Endless, unbroken, it cannot be named;
it returns again to no-thing.
This is called the shape without shape,
the image without a thing.
This is called the elusive and evanescent.
Meet it, and you do not see its head;
follow it, and you do not see its back.
Hold to the Way of old
to master the things of today.
To know the ancient beginning
is called the thread of the Way.

15道經 · The Way

古之善為士者，微妙玄通，深不可識。

夫唯不可識，故強為之容：

豫焉若冬涉川；猶兮若畏四鄰；儼兮其若客；渙兮若冰之將釋；

敦兮其若樸；曠兮其若谷；混兮其若濁。

孰能濁以靜之徐清？孰能安以久動之徐生？

保此道者，不欲盈。夫唯不盈，故能蔽不新成。

The good officers of old
were subtle, wondrous, dark and penetrating,
too deep to be known.
Because they cannot be known,
I can only try to describe them:
hesitant, as if crossing a river in winter;
wary, as if afraid of the neighbors on all sides;
formal, like a guest;
yielding, like ice about to melt;
simple, like the uncarved block;
open, like a valley;
murky, like muddy water.
Who can take muddy water and, by stillness, slowly make it clear?
Who can take what is at rest and, by long motion, slowly bring it to life?
Whoever keeps this Way does not wish to be full.
Because they are not full,
they can grow worn and need no renewing.

Some printings of the received text read 容 here; this text reads 客, “guest”, with the Heshang Gong text and the silk manuscripts.

16道經 · The Way

致虛極，守靜篤。萬物並作，吾以觀復。

夫物芸芸，各復歸其根。

歸根曰靜，是謂復命。復命曰常，知常曰明。

不知常，妄作凶。

知常容，容乃公，公乃王，王乃天，天乃道，道乃久，沒身不殆。

Reach the utmost emptiness;
hold fast to deep stillness.
The ten thousand things rise together,
and I watch them return.
Things flourish, each one,
and each returns to its root.
Returning to the root is called stillness;
this is called returning to one's destiny.
Returning to destiny is called the constant;
knowing the constant is called clarity.
Not knowing the constant, one acts blindly and meets disaster.
Knowing the constant, one embraces all;
embracing all, one is impartial;
impartial, one is kingly;
kingly, one is like heaven;
like heaven, one is with the Way;
with the Way, one endures,
and to the end of one's life is never in danger.

17道經 · The Way

太上，下知有之；其次，親而譽之；其次，畏之；其次，侮之。

信不足焉，有不信焉。

悠兮其貴言。功成事遂，百姓皆謂我自然。

Of the best rulers, those below know only that they are there.
Next come those who are loved and praised;
next, those who are feared;
next, those who are scorned.
When trust is lacking,
there is distrust.
Unhurried, the best rulers value their words.
When the work is done and all goes well,
the people all say, "It happened by itself."

18道經 · The Way

大道廢，有仁義；慧智出，有大偽；

六親不和，有孝慈；國家昏亂，有忠臣。

When the great Way is abandoned,
there are humaneness and righteousness.
When cleverness and knowledge appear,
there is great hypocrisy.
When the six relations are out of harmony,
there are filial children and devoted parents.
When the state is in confusion and disorder,
there are loyal ministers.

19道經 · The Way

絕聖棄智，民利百倍；絕仁棄義，民復孝慈；絕巧棄利，盜賊無有。

此三者，以為文不足，故令有所屬：

見素抱樸，少私寡欲。

Cut off sagehood, discard cleverness,
and the people will benefit a hundredfold.
Cut off humaneness, discard righteousness,
and the people will return to filial love.
Cut off craft, discard profit,
and there will be no thieves or robbers.
These three are ornament and not enough,
so let the people have something to hold to:
show plainness, embrace the uncarved block,
lessen self-interest, have few desires.

20道經 · The Way

絕學無憂。唯之與阿，相去幾何？善之與惡，相去若何？

人之所畏，不可不畏。荒兮其未央哉！

眾人熙熙，如享太牢，如春登臺。

我獨泊兮其未兆，如嬰兒之未孩；儽儽兮若無所歸。

眾人皆有餘，而我獨若遺。我愚人之心也哉！沌沌兮！

俗人昭昭，我獨昏昏；俗人察察，我獨悶悶。

澹兮其若海，飂兮若無止。

眾人皆有以，而我獨頑似鄙。

我獨異於人，而貴食母。

Cut off learning, and have no worries.
"Yes" and "yeah": how far apart are they?
Good and evil: how far apart are they?
What others fear, one cannot help fearing too.
How vast it is, and without end!
The crowd is merry, merry,
as if feasting at the great sacrifice,
as if climbing a terrace in spring.
I alone am placid, giving no sign,
like a baby that has not yet learned to smile,
drifting, as if with nowhere to return to.
The crowd all have more than enough;
I alone seem to have lost everything.
Mine is the heart of a fool,
so muddled!
Ordinary people are bright and clear;
I alone am dim.
Ordinary people are sharp and exacting;
I alone am closed and quiet,
calm, like the sea,
blown about, as if never to rest.
The crowd all have their uses;
I alone am stubborn and uncouth.
I alone am unlike others:
I prize being fed by the mother.

唯 wéi and 阿 ē are a polite and a curt way of saying yes.

21道經 · The Way

孔德之容，惟道是從。

道之為物，惟恍惟惚。

惚兮恍兮，其中有象；恍兮惚兮，其中有物。

窈兮冥兮，其中有精；其精甚真，其中有信。

自古及今，其名不去，以閱眾甫。

吾何以知眾甫之狀哉？以此。

The bearing of great virtue
follows the Way alone.
The Way, as a thing,
is elusive and evanescent.
Evanescent, elusive,
within it there is an image;
elusive, evanescent,
within it there is a thing.
Shadowy and dim,
within it there is an essence;
the essence is utterly real,
and within it there is trust.
From ancient times to now
its name has never gone,
and through it we see the fathers of all things.
How do I know the fathers of all things are so?
By this.

22道經 · The Way

曲則全，枉則直，窪則盈，敝則新，少則得，多則惑。

是以聖人抱一為天下式。

不自見，故明；不自是，故彰；不自伐，故有功；不自矜，故長。

夫唯不爭，故天下莫能與之爭。

古之所謂曲則全者，豈虛言哉！誠全而歸之。

Bent, and then whole;
crooked, and then straight;
hollow, and then full;
worn, and then new;
little, and then gaining;
much, and then confused.
So the sage embraces the One
and becomes a pattern for the world;
does not show off, and so is seen;
does not insist on being right, and so is recognized;
does not boast, and so has merit;
does not brag, and so endures.
Only because the sage does not contend
can no one in the world contend with the sage.
The old saying, "Bent, and then whole":
were these empty words?
Truly, wholeness returns.

23道經 · The Way

希言自然。故飄風不終朝，驟雨不終日。

孰為此者？天地。天地尚不能久，而況於人乎？

故從事於道者，道者同於道，德者同於德，失者同於失。

同於道者，道亦樂得之；同於德者，德亦樂得之；同於失者，失亦樂得之。

信不足焉，有不信焉。

To speak little is natural.
A whirlwind does not last all morning;
a downpour does not last all day.
Who makes them? Heaven and earth.
If even heaven and earth cannot make things last,
how much less can people?
So whoever follows the Way
becomes one with the Way;
whoever follows virtue becomes one with virtue;
whoever follows loss becomes one with loss.
One with the Way, the Way gladly receives you;
one with virtue, virtue gladly receives you;
one with loss, loss gladly receives you.
When trust is lacking, there is distrust.

24道經 · The Way

企者不立，跨者不行；

自見者不明，自是者不彰，自伐者無功，自矜者不長。

其在道也，曰餘食贅行。

物或惡之，故有道者不處。

On tiptoe, you do not stand firm;
striding, you do not go far.
Showing off, you are not seen;
insisting on being right, you are not recognized;
boasting, you have no merit;
bragging, you do not endure.
In the eyes of the Way these are called
leftover food and useless growths.
All things may well detest them,
so one who has the Way does not dwell in them.

25道經 · The Way

有物混成，先天地生。

寂兮寥兮，獨立不改，周行而不殆，可以為天下母。

吾不知其名，字之曰道，強為之名曰大。

大曰逝，逝曰遠，遠曰反。

故道大，天大，地大，王亦大。

域中有四大，而王居其一焉。

人法地，地法天，天法道，道法自然。

There was something formed in chaos,
born before heaven and earth.
Silent and empty,
it stands alone and does not change,
goes round and round and does not tire.
It could be the mother of the world.
I do not know its name;
I call it "the Way."
Forced to name it, I would call it "great."
Great means passing on;
passing on means going far;
going far means returning.
So the Way is great,
heaven is great,
earth is great,
and the king too is great.
In the realm there are four great things,
and the king is one of them.
People follow earth,
earth follows heaven,
heaven follows the Way,
the Way follows what is so of itself.

26道經 · The Way

重為輕根，靜為躁君。

是以聖人終日行不離輜重。雖有榮觀，燕處超然。

奈何萬乘之主，而以身輕天下？

輕則失本，躁則失君。

The heavy is the root of the light;
stillness is the master of restlessness.
So the sage travels all day
and never leaves the baggage cart.
Though there are splendid sights,
the sage stays at ease, above them.
How can the lord of ten thousand chariots
treat their own person lightly before the world?
Too light, and the root is lost;
too restless, and the master is lost.

27道經 · The Way

善行無轍迹，善言無瑕讁；善數不用籌策；

善閉無關楗而不可開，善結無繩約而不可解。

是以聖人常善救人，故無棄人；常善救物，故無棄物。是謂襲明。

故善人者，不善人之師；不善人者，善人之資。

不貴其師，不愛其資，雖智大迷，是謂要妙。

A good traveler leaves no tracks;
a good speaker makes no slips;
a good counter needs no counting sticks;
a good door is shut without bolts, yet cannot be opened;
a good knot is tied without rope, yet cannot be undone.
So the sage is always good at saving people
and casts no one aside;
always good at saving things
and casts nothing aside.
This is called cloaked light.
So the good are teachers of the not-good,
and the not-good are raw material for the good.
Not to value the teacher,
not to love the material:
however clever, this is a great confusion.
This is called the essential mystery.

襲明, “cloaked light”: 襲 means to put on a garment.

28道經 · The Way

知其雄，守其雌，為天下谿。為天下谿，常德不離，復歸於嬰兒。

知其白，守其黑，為天下式。為天下式，常德不忒，復歸於無極。

知其榮，守其辱，為天下谷。為天下谷，常德乃足，復歸於樸。

樸散則為器，聖人用之，則為官長，故大制不割。

Know the male, keep to the female,
and be a ravine for the world.
Be a ravine for the world,
and constant virtue will not leave you;
you return to being a newborn child.
Know the white, keep to the black,
and be a pattern for the world.
Be a pattern for the world,
and constant virtue will not err;
you return to the limitless.
Know glory, keep to disgrace,
and be a valley for the world.
Be a valley for the world,
and constant virtue will be enough;
you return to the uncarved block.
When the block is split, it becomes vessels;
the sage uses them and becomes chief of officials.
So the great cutting does not cut.

29道經 · The Way

將欲取天下而為之，吾見其不得已。

天下神器，不可為也。為者敗之，執者失之。

故物或行或隨，或歔或吹，或強或羸，或挫或隳。

是以聖人去甚，去奢，去泰。

Whoever would take the world and work on it,
I see will not succeed.
The world is a sacred vessel;
it cannot be worked on.
Work on it and you ruin it;
grasp it and you lose it.
Of things, some lead and some follow;
some breathe softly, some blow hard;
some are strong, some are frail;
some are crushed, some fall.
So the sage gives up extremes,
gives up extravagance,
gives up excess.

30道經 · The Way

以道佐人主者，不以兵強天下。其事好還：

師之所處，荊棘生焉；大軍之後，必有凶年。

善有果而已，不敢以取強。

果而勿矜，果而勿伐，果而勿驕，果而不得已，果而勿強。

物壯則老，是謂不道，不道早已。

Whoever helps a ruler with the Way
does not force the world with arms.
Such things tend to come back.
Where armies camp, thorns and brambles grow;
after great wars, bad harvests surely follow.
The good commander gets the result and stops,
and dares not use it to seize power.
Gets the result, without bragging;
gets the result, without boasting;
gets the result, without pride;
gets the result, because there was no other way;
gets the result, without force.
Things at their peak begin to age;
this is called going against the Way,
and what goes against the Way soon ends.

31道經 · The Way

夫佳兵者，不祥之器，物或惡之，故有道者不處。

君子居則貴左，用兵則貴右。

兵者不祥之器，非君子之器，不得已而用之，恬淡為上。

勝而不美，而美之者，是樂殺人。

夫樂殺人者，則不可以得志於天下矣。

吉事尚左，凶事尚右。偏將軍居左，上將軍居右，言以喪禮處之。

殺人之眾，以哀悲泣之；戰勝，以喪禮處之。

Fine weapons are instruments of ill omen;
all things may well detest them,
so one who has the Way does not dwell with them.
At home, the gentleman honors the left;
at war, the right.
Weapons are instruments of ill omen,
not the instruments of a gentleman,
used only when there is no other way,
and then with calm detachment.
There is no beauty in victory;
to find it beautiful is to delight in killing,
and whoever delights in killing
cannot have their way in the world.
In happy affairs the left is honored;
in sad affairs, the right.
The lieutenant general stands on the left,
the commander on the right:
they take their places as at a funeral.
When many have been killed,
mourn them with grief and tears;
after victory in war,
keep the rites of mourning.

32道經 · The Way

道常無名。樸雖小，天下莫能臣也。

侯王若能守之，萬物將自賓。

天地相合，以降甘露，民莫之令而自均。

始制有名，名亦既有，夫亦將知止，知止可以不殆。

譬道之在天下，猶川谷之於江海。

The Way is ever nameless.
The uncarved block, though small,
cannot be made a servant by anyone in the world.
If lords and kings could hold to it,
the ten thousand things would come to them as guests.
Heaven and earth would join
and send down sweet dew,
and the people, with no one commanding them,
would share it evenly of themselves.
When cutting begins, there are names.
Once there are names,
one should know where to stop;
knowing where to stop, one is not in danger.
The Way in the world
is like streams and valleys flowing to the rivers and the sea.

33道經 · The Way

知人者智，自知者明。勝人者有力，自勝者強。

知足者富，強行者有志。

不失其所者久，死而不亡者壽。

Knowing others is cleverness;
knowing yourself is clarity.
Overcoming others takes force;
overcoming yourself is strength.
Knowing you have enough is wealth;
pressing on is will.
Not losing your place is lasting;
dying and not perishing is long life.

34道經 · The Way

大道氾兮，其可左右。

萬物恃之而生而不辭，功成不名有，衣養萬物而不為主。

常無欲，可名於小；萬物歸焉而不為主，可名為大。

以其終不自為大，故能成其大。

The great Way flows everywhere;
it can go left or right.
The ten thousand things rely on it for life, and it refuses none.
It completes its work and takes no name for it;
it clothes and feeds the ten thousand things and does not act as their master.
Ever without desire, it may be called small;
the ten thousand things return to it and it does not act as their master:
it may be called great.
Because it never makes itself great,
it can achieve greatness.

35道經 · The Way

執大象，天下往。往而不害，安平太。

樂與餌，過客止。

道之出口，淡乎其無味，視之不足見，聽之不足聞，用之不足既。

Hold to the great image,
and the world will come to you;
come, and meet no harm,
but peace, calm and ease.
Music and good food
make passing travelers stop.
But the Way, when it passes the lips,
is bland, without flavor.
Look, and it is not enough to see;
listen, and it is not enough to hear;
use it, and it cannot be used up.

36道經 · The Way

將欲歙之，必固張之；將欲弱之，必固強之；

將欲廢之，必固興之；將欲奪之，必固與之。

是謂微明。柔弱勝剛強。

魚不可脫於淵，國之利器不可以示人。

What is to be shrunk
must first be stretched.
What is to be weakened
must first be strengthened.
What is to be cast down
must first be raised up.
What is to be taken
must first be given.
This is called subtle clarity.
The soft and weak overcome the hard and strong.
Fish should not leave the deep;
the sharp tools of the state should not be shown to others.

37道經 · The Way

道常無為而無不為。

侯王若能守之，萬物將自化。

化而欲作，吾將鎮之以無名之樸。

無名之樸，夫亦將無欲。不欲以靜，天下將自定。

The Way is ever without action,
yet nothing is left undone.
If lords and kings could hold to it,
the ten thousand things would transform of themselves.
If, transforming, desire should stir,
I would calm it with the nameless uncarved block.
The nameless uncarved block
brings freedom from desire.
Without desire there is stillness,
and the world will settle of itself.

38德經 · Virtue

上德不德，是以有德；下德不失德，是以無德。

上德無為而無以為；下德為之而有以為。

上仁為之而無以為；上義為之而有以為。

上禮為之而莫之應，則攘臂而扔之。

故失道而後德，失德而後仁，失仁而後義，失義而後禮。

夫禮者，忠信之薄，而亂之首。

前識者，道之華，而愚之始。

是以大丈夫處其厚，不居其薄；處其實，不居其華。故去彼取此。

The highest virtue does not act virtuous,
and so it has virtue.
The lowest virtue never lets go of virtue,
and so it has none.
The highest virtue does nothing
and has no need to do.
The lowest virtue does things
and has reasons for doing.
The highest humaneness acts
but has no reasons for acting.
The highest righteousness acts
and has reasons for acting.
The highest propriety acts, and when no one responds,
rolls up its sleeves and drags them to it.
So when the Way is lost, there is virtue;
when virtue is lost, there is humaneness;
when humaneness is lost, there is righteousness;
when righteousness is lost, there is propriety.
Propriety is the thinning of loyalty and trust
and the beginning of disorder.
Foreknowledge is the flower of the Way
and the beginning of folly.
So the great person dwells in the thick, not the thin,
in the fruit, not the flower;
lets go of that and takes this.

39德經 · Virtue

昔之得一者：天得一以清；地得一以寧；神得一以靈；

谷得一以盈；萬物得一以生；侯王得一以為天下貞。其致之。

天無以清，將恐裂；地無以寧，將恐發；神無以靈，將恐歇；

谷無以盈，將恐竭；萬物無以生，將恐滅；侯王無以貴高，將恐蹶。

故貴以賤為本，高以下為基。

是以侯王自謂孤、寡、不穀。此非以賤為本邪？非乎？

故致數輿無輿。不欲琭琭如玉，珞珞如石。

Of old, these attained the One:
heaven attained the One and became clear;
earth attained the One and became settled;
the spirits attained the One and became numinous;
the valley attained the One and became full;
the ten thousand things attained the One and came to life;
lords and kings attained the One and set the world right.
All this the One brought about.
Without its clarity, heaven would split;
without its settling, earth would quake;
without the numinous, the spirits would fade;
without its fullness, the valley would run dry;
without life, the ten thousand things would perish;
without their high rank, lords and kings would fall.
So the noble has the humble as its root;
the high has the low as its foundation.
That is why lords and kings call themselves
"the orphan," "the widowed," "the unworthy."
Is this not taking the humble as the root? Is it not?
So the highest praise is no praise.
Do not wish to tinkle like jade;
be plain as a stone.

輿, “carriage”, is usually read here as 譽, “praise”, and the translation follows that reading.

40德經 · Virtue

反者道之動；弱者道之用。

天下萬物生於有，有生於無。

Returning is the motion of the Way;
weakness is the use of the Way.
The ten thousand things under heaven are born of being;
being is born of nonbeing.

41德經 · Virtue

上士聞道，勤而行之；中士聞道，若存若亡；下士聞道，大笑之。

不笑不足以為道。故建言有之：

明道若昧；進道若退；夷道若纇；

上德若谷；大白若辱；廣德若不足；建德若偷；質真若渝；

大方無隅；大器晚成；大音希聲；大象無形；道隱無名。

夫唯道，善貸且成。

When the best students hear of the Way,
they practice it with all their might.
When middling students hear of the Way,
they keep it half the time.
When the worst students hear of the Way,
they laugh out loud;
if they did not laugh, it would not be the Way.
So the proverbs say:
the bright Way seems dark;
the Way forward seems like retreat;
the level Way seems rough;
the highest virtue seems like a valley;
the purest white seems soiled;
ample virtue seems lacking;
steadfast virtue seems slack;
true substance seems to fade;
the great square has no corners;
the great vessel is finished late;
the great sound is faint;
the great image has no shape.
The Way is hidden and nameless.
Only the Way is good at giving, and at bringing to completion.

42德經 · Virtue

道生一，一生二，二生三，三生萬物。

萬物負陰而抱陽，沖氣以為和。

人之所惡，唯孤、寡、不穀，而王公以為稱。

故物或損之而益，或益之而損。

人之所教，我亦教之。強梁者不得其死，吾將以為教父。

The Way gives birth to one,
one gives birth to two,
two gives birth to three,
three gives birth to the ten thousand things.
The ten thousand things carry yin on their backs and hold yang in their arms;
their breaths blend, and they are in harmony.
What people hate most
is to be orphaned, widowed, unworthy,
yet kings and lords take these as their titles.
So things are sometimes increased by being diminished,
and diminished by being increased.
What others teach, I teach too:
"The violent do not die a natural death."
I will make this the father of my teaching.

One common reading of the numbers: one is undivided breath, two is yin and yang, three is yin and yang with the breath that blends them.

43德經 · Virtue

天下之至柔，馳騁天下之至堅。

無有入無間，吾是以知無為之有益。

不言之教，無為之益，天下希及之。

The softest thing in the world
rides roughshod over the hardest.
What has no substance enters where there is no gap.
By this I know the benefit of non-action.
The teaching without words,
the benefit of non-action:
few in the world attain them.

44德經 · Virtue

名與身孰親？身與貨孰多？得與亡孰病？

是故甚愛必大費，多藏必厚亡。

知足不辱，知止不殆，可以長久。

Your name or your life: which is dearer?
Your life or your goods: which is worth more?
Gain or loss: which is the affliction?
Great love brings great cost;
much hoarding brings heavy loss.
Know what is enough, and you will not be disgraced;
know when to stop, and you will not be in danger;
you can long endure.

45德經 · Virtue

大成若缺，其用不弊。大盈若沖，其用不窮。

大直若屈，大巧若拙，大辯若訥。

躁勝寒，靜勝熱。清靜為天下正。

Great perfection seems flawed,
yet its use never wears out.
Great fullness seems empty,
yet its use is never exhausted.
Great straightness seems bent;
great skill seems clumsy;
great eloquence seems to stammer.
Movement overcomes cold;
stillness overcomes heat.
Clear and still, one sets the world right.

46德經 · Virtue

天下有道，卻走馬以糞；天下無道，戎馬生於郊。

禍莫大於不知足；咎莫大於欲得。

故知足之足，常足矣。

When the world has the Way,
galloping horses are turned back to haul manure.
When the world has lost the Way,
warhorses foal in the borderlands.
No disaster is greater than not knowing what is enough;
no fault is greater than wanting to gain.
So the contentment of knowing what is enough
is lasting contentment.

47德經 · Virtue

不出戶，知天下；不闚牖，見天道。

其出彌遠，其知彌少。

是以聖人不行而知，不見而名，不為而成。

Without going out the door,
know the world.
Without looking out the window,
see the Way of heaven.
The farther one goes,
the less one knows.
So the sage knows without going,
names without seeing,
accomplishes without doing.

George Harrison set this chapter to music in 1968 as “The Inner Light”.

48德經 · Virtue

為學日益，為道日損。

損之又損，以至於無為。無為而無不為。

取天下常以無事，及其有事，不足以取天下。

Pursue learning, and every day something is gained.
Pursue the Way, and every day something is lost.
Lose, and lose again,
until you arrive at non-action.
Do nothing, and nothing is left undone.
The world is won by having no business;
once one has business,
one is not fit to win the world.

49德經 · Virtue

聖人無常心，以百姓心為心。

善者，吾善之；不善者，吾亦善之；德善。

信者，吾信之；不信者，吾亦信之；德信。

聖人在天下，歙歙焉，為天下渾其心。

百姓皆注其耳目，聖人皆孩之。

The sage has no fixed heart
and takes the people's heart for a heart.
The good I treat as good;
the not-good I also treat as good,
and so goodness is gained.
The trustworthy I trust;
the untrustworthy I also trust,
and so trust is gained.
In the world, the sage draws in,
and for the world's sake keeps the heart blurred and whole.
The people all strain their eyes and ears;
the sage treats them all as children.

德 is read here as 得, “to gain”, a common reading.

50德經 · Virtue

出生入死。

生之徒，十有三；死之徒，十有三；人之生，動之死地，亦十有三。

夫何故？以其生生之厚。

蓋聞善攝生者，陸行不遇兕虎，入軍不被甲兵；

兕無所投其角，虎無所措其爪，兵無所容其刃。

夫何故？以其無死地。

We come out into life and go into death.
Companions of life, three in ten;
companions of death, three in ten;
and people who, living, move toward the places of death,
also three in ten.
Why is this?
Because they hold on to life too hard.
I have heard that one good at guarding life
travels by land without meeting rhinoceros or tiger,
goes into battle without armor or weapons.
The rhinoceros finds nowhere to drive its horn,
the tiger nowhere to set its claws,
the weapon nowhere to lodge its blade.
Why is this?
Because there is no place of death in them.

十有三 can mean “three in ten” or “thirteen”; the translation follows the first reading.

51德經 · Virtue

道生之，德畜之，物形之，勢成之。

是以萬物莫不尊道而貴德。

道之尊，德之貴，夫莫之命而常自然。

故道生之，德畜之；長之育之；亭之毒之；養之覆之。

生而不有，為而不恃，長而不宰，是謂玄德。

The Way gives them life,
virtue nourishes them,
matter gives them shape,
circumstance completes them.
So the ten thousand things all honor the Way and prize virtue.
The Way is honored and virtue prized
not because anyone decrees it,
but always, of itself.
So the Way gives them life and virtue nourishes them,
raises them and rears them,
settles them and ripens them,
feeds them and shelters them.
To give life without owning,
to act without relying on it,
to lead without ruling:
this is called dark virtue.

52德經 · Virtue

天下有始，以為天下母。

既得其母，以知其子；既知其子，復守其母，沒身不殆。

塞其兌，閉其門，終身不勤。開其兌，濟其事，終身不救。

見小曰明，守柔曰強。

用其光，復歸其明，無遺身殃；是為習常。

The world has a beginning,
and it is the mother of the world.
Once you have found the mother,
you know the children.
Once you know the children,
go back and hold to the mother,
and to the end of your life you will be in no danger.
Block the openings,
shut the doors,
and to the end of your life you will not be worn out.
Open the openings,
add to your affairs,
and to the end of your life you will be past saving.
To see the small is called clarity;
to keep to the soft is called strength.
Use the light,
return to clarity,
bring no harm upon yourself:
this is called practicing the constant.

53德經 · Virtue

使我介然有知，行於大道，唯施是畏。

大道甚夷，而民好徑。

朝甚除，田甚蕪，倉甚虛；

服文綵，帶利劍，厭飲食，財貨有餘；

是為盜夸。非道也哉！

If I had the least bit of knowledge,
I would walk the great Way
and fear only straying from it.
The great Way is very level,
yet people love side paths.
The court is spotless,
the fields are overgrown,
the granaries are empty,
yet they wear embroidered silks,
carry sharp swords,
gorge on food and drink,
and have wealth to spare.
This is called the robber's pomp.
It is surely not the Way!

54德經 · Virtue

善建者不拔，善抱者不脫，子孫以祭祀不輟。

修之於身，其德乃真；修之於家，其德乃餘；修之於鄉，其德乃長；

修之於國，其德乃豐；修之於天下，其德乃普。

故以身觀身，以家觀家，以鄉觀鄉，以國觀國，以天下觀天下。

吾何以知天下然哉？以此。

What is well planted cannot be uprooted;
what is well held cannot slip away,
and children's children will keep up the offerings.
Cultivate it in yourself, and virtue will be real;
cultivate it in the family, and virtue will overflow;
cultivate it in the village, and virtue will last;
cultivate it in the state, and virtue will flourish;
cultivate it in the world, and virtue will be everywhere.
So see the self through the self,
the family through the family,
the village through the village,
the state through the state,
the world through the world.
How do I know the world is so?
By this.

55德經 · Virtue

含德之厚，比於赤子。

蜂蠆虺蛇不螫，猛獸不據，攫鳥不搏。

骨弱筋柔而握固。未知牝牡之合而全作，精之至也。

終日號而不嗄，和之至也。

知和曰常，知常曰明，益生曰祥，心使氣曰強。

物壯則老，謂之不道，不道早已。

One who holds virtue in abundance
is like a newborn child.
Wasps and scorpions, vipers and snakes do not sting it;
wild beasts do not seize it;
birds of prey do not strike it.
Its bones are weak, its sinews soft, yet its grip is firm.
It knows nothing of the union of male and female,
yet its whole body stirs:
its vital essence is at its height.
It cries all day and never grows hoarse:
its harmony is at its height.
To know harmony is called constant;
to know the constant is called clarity.
To force life is called ill-omened;
for the mind to drive the breath is called forcing.
Things at their peak begin to age;
this is called going against the Way,
and what goes against the Way soon ends.

56德經 · Virtue

知者不言，言者不知。

塞其兌，閉其門，挫其銳，解其分，和其光，同其塵，是謂玄同。

故不可得而親，不可得而疏；不可得而利，不可得而害；

不可得而貴，不可得而賤。故為天下貴。

Those who know do not speak;
those who speak do not know.
Block the openings,
shut the doors,
blunt the sharp,
untie the tangled,
soften the glare,
become one with the dust:
this is called dark sameness.
It cannot be drawn close
or held at a distance;
it cannot be benefited
or harmed;
it cannot be honored
or humbled.
So it is honored by all the world.

57德經 · Virtue

以正治國，以奇用兵，以無事取天下。

吾何以知其然哉？以此：

天下多忌諱，而民彌貧；民多利器，國家滋昏；

人多伎巧，奇物滋起；法令滋彰，盜賊多有。

故聖人云：我無為，而民自化；我好靜，而民自正；

我無事，而民自富；我無欲，而民自樸。

Govern the state by the straightforward;
wage war by surprise;
win the world by having no business.
How do I know it is so?
By this:
the more taboos and prohibitions in the world,
the poorer the people;
the more sharp tools the people have,
the more the state is in turmoil;
the more cunning and skill people have,
the more strange things arise;
the more laws and orders are posted,
the more thieves and robbers there are.
So the sage says:
I do nothing, and the people transform themselves;
I love stillness, and the people set themselves right;
I have no business, and the people grow rich by themselves;
I have no desires, and the people become simple by themselves.

58德經 · Virtue

其政悶悶，其民淳淳；其政察察，其民缺缺。

禍兮福之所倚，福兮禍之所伏。

孰知其極？其無正。正復為奇，善復為妖。

人之迷，其日固久。

是以聖人方而不割，廉而不劌，直而不肆，光而不燿。

When the government is dull and quiet,
the people are pure and simple.
When the government is sharp and exacting,
the people are wanting and restless.
Misfortune: good fortune leans on it.
Good fortune: misfortune hides in it.
Who knows where it ends?
There is nothing fixed.
The straight turns strange again;
the good turns uncanny again.
People have been lost
for a very long time.
So the sage is square but does not cut,
has edges but does not wound,
is straight but not unbending,
shines but does not dazzle.

59德經 · Virtue

治人事天，莫若嗇。

夫唯嗇，是謂早服；早服謂之重積德；

重積德則無不克；無不克則莫知其極；

莫知其極，可以有國；有國之母，可以長久。

是謂深根固柢，長生久視之道。

In governing people and serving heaven,
nothing is better than restraint.
Only through restraint
does one yield early.
Yielding early is called storing up virtue.
Store up virtue, and there is nothing you cannot overcome;
when there is nothing you cannot overcome,
no one knows your limit;
when no one knows your limit,
you can hold a state;
holding the mother of the state,
you can long endure.
This is called deep roots and a firm stem,
the Way of long life and lasting sight.

60德經 · Virtue

治大國，若烹小鮮。

以道蒞天下，其鬼不神；

非其鬼不神，其神不傷人；非其神不傷人，聖人亦不傷人。

夫兩不相傷，故德交歸焉。

Govern a great state
as you would cook a small fish.
When the world is ruled by the Way,
the ghosts lose their power.
Not that the ghosts lose their power,
but their power does not harm people.
Not that their power does not harm people,
but the sage does not harm people either.
When neither harms the other,
their virtue meets and returns to the people.

Quoted by Ronald Reagan in his State of the Union address of 1988.

61德經 · Virtue

大國者下流，天下之交，天下之牝。

牝常以靜勝牡，以靜為下。

故大國以下小國，則取小國；小國以下大國，則取大國。

故或下以取，或下而取。

大國不過欲兼畜人，小國不過欲入事人。

夫兩者各得其所欲，大者宜為下。

A great state is the low ground where the rivers run,
the meeting place of the world,
the female of the world.
The female always overcomes the male by stillness,
and in stillness takes the lower place.
So a great state, by placing itself below a small state,
wins the small state;
a small state, by placing itself below a great state,
wins over the great state.
One lowers itself to win;
the other is low and so wins.
A great state wants no more than to gather and keep people;
a small state wants no more than to join and serve others.
If both are to get what they want,
the great one should take the lower place.

62德經 · Virtue

道者萬物之奧。善人之寶，不善人之所保。

美言可以市尊，美行可以加人。

人之不善，何棄之有？

故立天子，置三公，雖有拱璧以先駟馬，不如坐進此道。

古之所以貴此道者何？不曰以求得，有罪以免邪？故為天下貴。

The Way is the hidden sanctuary of the ten thousand things,
treasure of the good,
refuge of the not-good.
Fine words can buy honor;
fine deeds can raise a person above others.
Even when people are not good,
why cast them away?
So when the Son of Heaven is enthroned
and the three ministers installed,
though there be discs of jade to present, followed by teams of four horses,
it is better to sit still and offer this Way.
Why did the ancients prize this Way?
Did they not say, "Seek, and you will find;
do wrong, and you will be spared"?
So it is honored by all the world.

63德經 · Virtue

為無為，事無事，味無味。

大小多少，報怨以德。

圖難於其易，為大於其細；

天下難事，必作於易；天下大事，必作於細。

是以聖人終不為大，故能成其大。

夫輕諾必寡信，多易必多難。是以聖人猶難之，故終無難矣。

Act without acting;
work without working;
taste the tasteless.
Treat the small as great and the few as many;
repay resentment with virtue.
Plan for the hard while it is easy;
do the great while it is small.
The hardest things in the world begin as easy ones;
the greatest things in the world begin as small ones.
So the sage never attempts great things,
and so achieves greatness.
Light promises inspire little trust;
the more things seem easy, the harder they become.
So the sage treats things as hard
and in the end finds nothing hard.

64德經 · Virtue

其安易持，其未兆易謀；其脆易泮，其微易散。

為之於未有，治之於未亂。

合抱之木，生於毫末；九層之臺，起於累土；千里之行，始於足下。

為者敗之，執者失之。是以聖人無為故無敗，無執故無失。

民之從事，常於幾成而敗之。慎終如始，則無敗事。

是以聖人欲不欲，不貴難得之貨；

學不學，復眾人之所過，以輔萬物之自然而不敢為。

What is at rest is easy to hold;
what has not yet shown itself is easy to plan for;
what is brittle is easy to break;
what is tiny is easy to scatter.
Deal with things before they arise;
set them in order before disorder begins.
A tree you can barely put your arms around
grows from a tiny shoot;
a tower of nine stories
rises from a heap of earth;
a journey of a thousand li
begins beneath your feet.
Work on it and you ruin it;
grasp it and you lose it.
So the sage does not act, and so does not ruin;
does not grasp, and so does not lose.
People in their affairs
often fail on the verge of success.
Be as careful at the end as at the beginning,
and nothing will fail.
So the sage desires not to desire
and does not prize goods that are hard to get;
learns not to learn
and goes back to what the crowd has passed by,
to help the ten thousand things be as they are,
and does not dare to act.

The source of the proverb about a journey of a thousand miles. A li is a Chinese mile.

65德經 · Virtue

古之善為道者，非以明民，將以愚之。

民之難治，以其智多。

故以智治國，國之賊；不以智治國，國之福。

知此兩者亦稽式。常知稽式，是謂玄德。

玄德深矣，遠矣，與物反矣，然後乃至大順。

The good practitioners of the Way in ancient times
did not use it to make the people clever,
but to keep them simple.
People are hard to govern
because they know too much.
So to govern a state by cleverness
is to rob the state;
to govern it without cleverness
is to bless the state.
Knowing these two is knowing the standard.
To know the standard always
is called dark virtue.
Dark virtue is deep and far-reaching;
it turns back with things,
and so arrives at the great harmony.

66德經 · Virtue

江海所以能為百谷王者，以其善下之，故能為百谷王。

是以欲上民，必以言下之；欲先民，必以身後之。

是以聖人處上而民不重，處前而民不害。

是以天下樂推而不厭。

以其不爭，故天下莫能與之爭。

Rivers and seas can be kings of the hundred valleys
because they are good at staying below them;
so they can be kings of the hundred valleys.
So whoever wishes to be above the people
must speak as one below them;
whoever wishes to lead the people
must follow behind them.
So the sage is above and the people do not feel the weight,
is in front and the people do not feel threatened,
and the whole world gladly pushes the sage forward and never tires.
Because the sage does not contend,
no one in the world can contend with the sage.

67德經 · Virtue

天下皆謂我道大，似不肖。

夫唯大，故似不肖。若肖，久矣其細也夫！

我有三寶，持而保之：一曰慈，二曰儉，三曰不敢為天下先。

慈故能勇；儉故能廣；不敢為天下先，故能成器長。

今舍慈且勇，舍儉且廣，舍後且先，死矣！

夫慈，以戰則勝，以守則固。天將救之，以慈衛之。

All the world says my Way is great,
yet it seems like nothing at all.
It is because it is great
that it seems like nothing.
If it seemed like something,
it would long ago have grown small.
I have three treasures;
I hold them and keep them.
The first is compassion,
the second is restraint,
the third is not daring to be first in the world.
Compassionate, one can be brave;
restrained, one can be generous;
not daring to be first in the world,
one can become the chief of all vessels.
Now, to give up compassion yet be brave,
to give up restraint yet be generous,
to give up the last place yet be first:
this is death!
Compassion wins in attack and holds firm in defense.
When heaven would save someone,
it guards them with compassion.

The three treasures: 慈 cí, compassion; 儉 jiǎn, restraint or frugality; and not daring to be first.

68德經 · Virtue

善為士者不武，善戰者不怒，善勝敵者不與，善用人者為之下。

是謂不爭之德，是謂用人之力，是謂配天古之極。

A good officer is not warlike;
a good fighter is not angry;
one good at defeating enemies does not engage them;
one good at leading people places themselves below them.
This is called the virtue of not contending,
the power to use others,
matching heaven: the utmost of old.

69德經 · Virtue

用兵有言：吾不敢為主而為客，不敢進寸而退尺。

是謂行無行，攘無臂，扔無敵，執無兵。

禍莫大於輕敵，輕敵幾喪吾寶。

故抗兵相加，哀者勝矣。

Those who use arms have a saying:
"I dare not be the host, but rather the guest;
I dare not advance an inch, but rather retreat a foot."
This is called marching without marching,
rolling up one's sleeves without bared arms,
driving back an enemy that is not there,
holding weapons that are not there.
No disaster is greater than taking the enemy lightly;
taking the enemy lightly nearly costs me my treasures.
So when opposing armies meet,
the side that grieves will win.

70德經 · Virtue

吾言甚易知，甚易行。天下莫能知，莫能行。

言有宗，事有君。

夫唯無知，是以不我知。

知我者希，則我者貴。是以聖人被褐懷玉。

My words are very easy to understand
and very easy to practice,
yet no one in the world can understand them,
and no one can practice them.
Words have an ancestor;
deeds have a master.
Because people do not understand this,
they do not understand me.
Those who understand me are few;
those who follow me are rare.
So the sage wears coarse cloth
and carries jade inside.

71德經 · Virtue

知不知，上；不知知，病。

夫唯病病，是以不病。

聖人不病，以其病病，是以不病。

To know that you do not know is highest;
not to know, yet think you know, is a sickness.
Only by treating the sickness as sickness
can one be free of it.
The sage is not sick,
treating the sickness as sickness,
and so is not sick.

72德經 · Virtue

民不畏威，則大威至。

無狎其所居，無厭其所生。

夫唯不厭，是以不厭。

是以聖人自知不自見，自愛不自貴。故去彼取此。

When the people do not fear power,
a greater terror arrives.
Do not cramp their dwellings;
do not crush their livelihood.
Only if you do not crush them
will they not reject you.
So the sage has self-knowledge but no self-display,
self-regard but no self-exaltation,
lets go of that and takes this.

73德經 · Virtue

勇於敢則殺，勇於不敢則活。此兩者，或利或害。

天之所惡，孰知其故？是以聖人猶難之。

天之道，不爭而善勝，不言而善應，不召而自來，繟然而善謀。

天網恢恢，疏而不失。

Courage in daring leads to death;
courage in not daring leads to life.
Of these two, one brings benefit, one brings harm.
What heaven hates,
who knows why?
Even the sage finds it hard.
The Way of heaven
does not contend, yet is good at winning;
does not speak, yet is good at answering;
is not summoned, yet comes of itself;
is unhurried, yet is good at planning.
Heaven's net is vast, so vast;
its mesh is wide, yet nothing slips through.

74德經 · Virtue

民不畏死，奈何以死懼之？

若使民常畏死，而為奇者，吾得執而殺之，孰敢？

常有司殺者殺。夫代司殺者殺，是謂代大匠斲。

夫代大匠斲者，希有不傷其手矣。

If the people do not fear death,
why threaten them with death?
If the people always feared death,
and I could seize and kill those who do wrong,
who would dare?
There is always the appointed executioner to kill.
To kill in the executioner's place
is to cut wood in the master carpenter's place.
Of those who cut wood in the master carpenter's place,
few do not hurt their hands.

75德經 · Virtue

民之飢，以其上食稅之多，是以飢。

民之難治，以其上之有為，是以難治。

民之輕死，以其求生之厚，是以輕死。

夫唯無以生為者，是賢於貴生。

The people are starving
because those above them eat up too much in taxes;
that is why they starve.
The people are hard to govern
because those above them are always doing things;
that is why they are hard to govern.
The people take death lightly
because they chase life too hard;
that is why they take death lightly.
Only those who do not make living their business
are wiser than those who prize life.

76德經 · Virtue

人之生也柔弱，其死也堅強。

萬物草木之生也柔脆，其死也枯槁。

故堅強者死之徒，柔弱者生之徒。

是以兵強則不勝，木強則兵。

強大處下，柔弱處上。

When people are born they are soft and weak;
when they die they are hard and stiff.
When plants and trees are born they are soft and tender;
when they die they are withered and dry.
So the hard and stiff are companions of death;
the soft and weak are companions of life.
So an army that is too strong will not win;
a tree that is too strong will be felled.
The hard and great take the lower place;
the soft and weak take the higher.

77德經 · Virtue

天之道，其猶張弓與？

高者抑之，下者舉之；有餘者損之，不足者補之。

天之道，損有餘而補不足。

人之道，則不然，損不足以奉有餘。

孰能有餘以奉天下？唯有道者。

是以聖人為而不恃，功成而不處，其不欲見賢。

The Way of heaven:
is it not like drawing a bow?
What is high is pulled down;
what is low is raised up;
what has too much is reduced;
what has too little is given more.
The Way of heaven
takes from what has too much and gives to what has too little.
The way of people is not so:
it takes from those who have too little to serve those who have too much.
Who can take what they have in excess and give it to the world?
Only one who has the Way.
So the sage acts without relying on it,
completes the work without dwelling on it,
and has no wish to show off their worth.

78德經 · Virtue

天下莫柔弱於水，而攻堅強者莫之能勝，以其無以易之。

弱之勝強，柔之勝剛，天下莫不知，莫能行。

是以聖人云：受國之垢，是謂社稷主；受國不祥，是為天下王。

正言若反。

Nothing in the world is softer or weaker than water,
yet for attacking the hard and strong nothing can surpass it,
for nothing can take its place.
That the weak overcomes the strong,
that the soft overcomes the hard,
everyone in the world knows,
yet no one can put it into practice.
So the sage says:
whoever takes on the filth of the state
is called master of its altars;
whoever takes on the misfortunes of the state
is called king of the world.
True words sound like their opposite.

79德經 · Virtue

和大怨，必有餘怨，安可以為善？

是以聖人執左契，而不責於人。

有德司契，無德司徹。

天道無親，常與善人。

When a great resentment is settled,
some resentment surely remains;
how can this be called good?
So the sage holds the left half of the tally
and makes no demands on others.
Those with virtue keep to the tally;
those without virtue collect the tax.
The Way of heaven has no favorites;
it is always with the good.

80德經 · Virtue

小國寡民。使有什伯之器而不用，使民重死而不遠徙。

雖有舟輿，無所乘之；雖有甲兵，無所陳之。

使人復結繩而用之。

甘其食，美其服，安其居，樂其俗。

鄰國相望，雞犬之聲相聞，民至老死，不相往來。

Let the state be small and its people few.
Though they have tools that do the work of ten or a hundred, let them go unused.
Let the people take death seriously and not travel far.
Though they have boats and carriages, let there be no call to ride them;
though they have armor and weapons, let there be no call to display them.
Let the people go back to knotting cords to keep records.
Let their food be sweet to them,
their clothes beautiful,
their homes at peace,
their customs a joy.
Neighboring states may be within sight of each other,
the sounds of their dogs and chickens heard across the way,
yet the people will grow old and die
without ever going back and forth.

Knotted cords were a way of keeping records before writing.

81德經 · Virtue

信言不美，美言不信。

善者不辯，辯者不善。

知者不博，博者不知。

聖人不積，既以為人己愈有，既以與人己愈多。

天之道，利而不害；聖人之道，為而不爭。

True words are not beautiful;
beautiful words are not true.
The good do not argue;
those who argue are not good.
Those who know are not widely learned;
the widely learned do not know.
The sage does not hoard.
The more the sage does for others, the more the sage has;
the more the sage gives to others, the more the sage possesses.
The Way of heaven benefits and does not harm.
The Way of the sage acts and does not contend.

About the translation

道 is the Way; 德 dé is virtue, in the old sense of a thing’s power; 無為 is non-action; 萬物, literally “ten thousand things”, stays as the ten thousand things. 聖人, the sage, has no gender in Chinese and none here. The translation stays close to the Chinese and keeps its compression where English allows. Where a line can be read more than one way, a note gives the other reading.

The paintings

The eight ink paintings behind this page were made for it, stroke by stroke, by a small program written for the purpose. Each exists in a day version, ink on paper, and a night version by moonlight; the taijitu button at the top of the page switches between them.
