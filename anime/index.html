How this history works

Ten reels, four questions

Anime is not a genre. It is a medium and an industry that has held every kind of story, from boxing melodrama to Shinto ecology to cyberpunk philosophy. Its history is told here in ten reels, from medieval picture scrolls to the global box office of 2025. Each reel asks the same four questions, so you can compare one era with the next.

Featured works are laid out like the rows of an 絵コンテ e-konte, the storyboard sheet every anime is planned on. Each has a cut number, a picture, its content and its time. Dotted words such as open a short definition.

Reel 00 · Prologue

c. 1100 — 1916

Before the Frame

Long before cinema reached Japan, its artists were telling stories across time in pictures: on scrolls unrolled at the pace of the reader's hands, in lantern shows, in woodblock sketchbooks.

前史

12th–13th century

At Kōzan-ji temple in Kyoto, four ink scrolls show frogs wrestling rabbits, monkeys dressed as monks and a frog seated as a Buddha. The best-known scroll has no text at all. The brush line carries the story on its own.

How a scroll moves

A picture scroll is read right to left, about an arm's width at a time. You set the pace, and each new stretch of paper replaces the last. It works something like a frame, and something like a cut.

1814

Hokusai publishes the first volume of his Manga: sketchbooks of people, animals and spirits caught in the middle of moving. The word meant something like “whimsical pictures”.

c. 1907

Someone stencils fifty frames onto a strip of celluloid and joins it into a loop. A boy writes 活動写真, “moving pictures”, and bows. It lasts three seconds.

背景

The world around it

Anime is a twentieth-century art built on a technology invented elsewhere. Asking where it “comes from” in older Japanese art is a fair question, but an easy one to overstate. What can be said with confidence is that Japanese artists and audiences had long habits that animation later drew on: reading pictures in sequence, telling stories through line rather than modelled volume, and stacking flat planes one behind another to make space.

The great age of the picture scroll, , was the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The Tale of Genji scrolls lift the roofs off rooms so that we look down into them, a convention called fukinuki yatai, “blown-away roof”. The Legends of Mount Shigi follow a rice granary as it flies through the sky while a crowd runs below. The Ban Dainagon scroll stages a palace fire with dozens of figures fleeing in every direction. Some scrolls draw the same person several times in one scene so that a single picture shows a sequence of moments, a device called iji-dōzu, “different times in one picture”.

In 1999 the director Isao Takahata published a book whose title translates as Twelfth-Century Animation. Going through national-treasure scrolls passage by passage, he argued that they already contained “cinematic and animation-like” devices: something like cuts, pans and close-ups, and above all a sense of time. He did not claim the scrolls were anime. He showed that the problem animators solve, how to make pictures carry time, is very old.

In the Edo period (1603–1868), woodblock printing made pictures cheap. Comic picture books called kibyōshi combined words and images on every page, and performers staged lantern shows, moving wooden projectors behind a paper screen so that ghosts drifted and faces changed. Katsushika Hokusai's Manga, fifteen volumes published from 1814, filled page after page with bodies in action: wrestlers, acrobats, fishermen, ghosts.

Moving pictures reached Japan in 1896 and 1897, first Edison's Kinetoscope and then the Lumière brothers' Cinématographe. Silent films were shown with , narrators who stood beside the screen and voiced the action, and who were often a bigger draw than the film. Foreign cartoons followed. The first one known to have been screened was by the French animator Émile Cohl, shown in Tokyo's Asakusa district in April 1912.

Then, in 2005, a collection of old films and projectors in Kyoto turned up a strip of fifty frames, stencilled in red and black directly onto celluloid and joined into a loop. A boy writes 活動写真 from right to left, turns, takes off his cap and bows. It was probably made between 1905 and 1912, for home projectors, by someone whose name is lost. If the dating is right it is the oldest Japanese animation we have. Because it was never shown in a cinema, anime's history as an industry still begins a few years later, in 1917.

意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. None of these artists were trying to invent anime. The scroll painters were working out how to make time visible on a surface you hold in your hands. The lantern performers wanted to frighten and delight a room. Hokusai wanted a teaching manual and a record of everything that moves. What links them is an interest in movement held still: a body caught mid-stride, a crowd mid-panic, a granary mid-flight. Animation would take that frozen instant and set it going again.

Interiority. A scroll is a private experience. One or two people unroll it at a table, at the speed of their own hands, deciding when to linger. The lantern show and, later, were the opposite: crowded, noisy, shared. Anime inherited both. Manga and home video made it intimate, something you take in alone at your own pace; cinema and television made it communal. The boy in the stencilled loop, bowing to whoever owned the projector, sits right between the two.

波紋

Ripples

In Japan

Picture storytelling never stopped. In the 1930s street performers gathered crowds of children for serialised paper-theatre stories, and several postwar manga artists, among them Shigeru Mizuki and Sanpei Shirato, started out drawing for kamishibai.

Around the world

Japanese images had already crossed the world once. From the 1860s, prints by Hokusai, Hiroshige and others shaped Japonisme in Europe. Van Gogh painted copies of Hiroshige, and Hokusai's Great Wave became one of the most reproduced images ever made. Anime would be the second wave.

One story

Isao Takahata, at sixty-three

In 1999 the director of Grave of the Fireflies published a close study of medieval picture scrolls. His argument was that a twelfth-century painter and a modern animator face the same problem: how to make a still picture carry the feeling of time.

Reel 01

1917 — 1945

Paper, Sound and War

In 1917 three young artists put the first Japanese cartoons on cinema screens. Within three decades animation in Japan had learned to talk and to sing, and to serve an empire at war.

黎明

1917

A cartoonist, a watercolour painter and a political caricaturist, each hired by a different film company, release the first Japanese animated films. Only one of their 1917 films is known to survive.

1 September 1923

The Great Kantō earthquake and the fires that follow destroy much of Tokyo, its film studios and their prints.

1933

Kenzō Masaoka gives Japanese animation a voice with Chikara to Onna no Yo no Naka, the first anime with recorded dialogue.

12 April 1945

The Navy's Momotarō: Sacred Sailors, Japan's first animated feature, opens in a country being burned from the air. In Osaka a sixteen-year-old named Osamu Tezuka watches it and cries.

背景

The world around it

By the 1910s American and European cartoons were drawing crowds in Japanese cinemas, and film companies wanted their own. In 1917 three men, working separately, answered. Ōten Shimokawa, a cartoonist for the humour magazine Tokyo Puck, made shorts for the Tenkatsu company; his first reached screens in January. Seitarō Kitayama, a watercolourist, made Saru Kani Gassen (The Monkey and the Crab) for Nikkatsu, released in May. Jun'ichi Kōuchi, a political cartoonist, made Namakura Gatana (The Dull Sword) for Kobayashi Shōkai, released on 30 June.

None of Shimokawa's five films of 1917 survives. Namakura Gatana was thought lost too, until a copy turned up at an antique dealer's in Osaka in 2008. Two minutes long, it shows a swaggering samurai who buys a blunt sword, tries it out on passers-by, and is beaten by the ordinary townspeople he means to cut down.

The pioneers worked with whatever was cheap. Celluloid was imported and expensive, so many early films were made with paper cut-outs, or drawn and redrawn in chalk. Kitayama opened Japan's first studio devoted to animation in 1921 and made educational and advertising films. Two years later the Great Kantō earthquake levelled much of Tokyo, and with it studios, equipment and prints. A great deal of the decade's work is gone.

What survives is often extraordinary. Noburō Ōfuji cut his figures from chiyogami, patterned Japanese paper, and later from coloured cellophane, so that his films look like moving paper craft. Yasuji Murata built comic fables from jointed cut-outs. Kenzō Masaoka, trained as a painter, pushed for the two things that would make animation modern: celluloid, and sound. His Chikara to Onna no Yo no Naka (1933) was the first anime with recorded dialogue. No copy is known to survive.

From the 1930s the state took a growing interest in film. The Film Law of 1939 put production under government licensing, and animators were commissioned by ministries and the armed forces. Japanese audiences had also seen what animation could do at feature length: Princess Iron Fan, made in wartime Shanghai by the Wan brothers in 1941, was shown in Japan in 1942. It prompted the Navy to order an animated feature of its own.

Mitsuyo Seo made two. Momotarō's Sea Eagles (1943) retold the attack on Pearl Harbor with the folk hero Momotarō leading a carrier crew of animals. Momotarō: Sacred Sailors (1945), seventy-four minutes long, was Japan's first feature-length animation. It shows animal paratroopers taking an island from the British, who are drawn as cowards. It opened on 12 April 1945 to a country under bombing, and few children saw it. The film was believed destroyed after the surrender until a negative was found in a Shochiku warehouse in 1983.

意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. The first generation wanted to prove that Japanese artists could make the new art, and they reached for what audiences already knew: folk tales such as Momotarō and The Monkey and the Crab, and the jokes of the political cartoon. Namakura Gatana is a cartoonist's satire, and its target is a member of the warrior class humiliated by commoners. Ōfuji wanted animation that looked unmistakably Japanese, and found it in paper. Masaoka wanted it to be an art. In 1943, in the middle of the war, he made Spider and the Tulip, a nearly plotless film in which a ladybird hides inside a tulip from a singing spider until a storm sweeps the spider away. Some critics have read its gentleness as a quiet refusal of the war film. Its villain, though, is drawn in the style of American blackface minstrel caricature: a reminder that even the gentlest wartime art carried the racism of its time.

Interiority. For the first audiences the feeling was novelty and laughter: drawings that moved, and sometimes talked. War changed the feeling. The Momotarō films put round-eyed, cuddly animals into combat and asked children to see sacrifice for the emperor as sweet. That pairing of the cute and the violent would haunt Japanese animation for decades, and later artists turned it inside out: Grave of the Fireflies, In This Corner of the World and The Wind Rises all return to these years to ask what such images cost.

波紋

Ripples

In Japan

Wartime commissions built skills, teams and equipment that animation had never had. In January 1948 veterans including Masaoka and Sanae Yamamoto founded Nihon Dōga in the ruins of Tokyo. Within a decade it would become Tōei Dōga, the studio that trained the next generation.

Around the world

The flow ran almost entirely one way: into Japan. Disney, the Fleischers and Cohl shaped Japanese ambitions, and Shanghai's Princess Iron Fan showed that an Asian studio could make a feature. Disney's Snow White, held back by the war, did not open in Japan until September 1950.

One story

Osamu Tezuka, sixteen, Osaka, April 1945

A teenager in a city being firebombed watched Momotarō: Sacred Sailors and was moved to tears. He later said the film made him want to be an animator. Eighteen years later his own studio would put Astro Boy on television.

背景

The world around it

The surrender of August 1945 left the animators who had worked on propaganda with no clients and, under the American Occupation, a new set of rules. Censors discouraged feudal and militaristic themes, and animation survived on advertising, educational films and short commissions. In January 1948 a group of veterans, among them Kenzō Masaoka and Sanae Yamamoto, founded Nihon Dōga in the ruins of Tokyo. It was renamed Nichidō Eiga in 1952. Meanwhile Hollywood's backlog arrived: Disney's Snow White, held back by the war, opened in Japan in 1950, and Japanese artists studied its fluid, fully drawn motion with envy.

In 1956 the film company Toei bought Nichidō and renamed it Tōei Dōga. Its president, Hiroshi Ōkawa, gave it an explicit ambition: to be “the Disney of the East”, Japan's first company built to make full-length theatrical animation, with features that could be sold in Asia and the West. Toei built a new studio at Ōizumi in western Tokyo and organised it like an animation factory, with key animators, in-betweeners and departments of tracers and painters, most of them young women.

The first feature, Hakujaden (The Tale of the White Serpent), came out on 22 October 1958. It adapts the Chinese legend of a white snake spirit who becomes a woman for love of a young man. Directed by Taiji Yabushita, with key animation led by Yasuji Mori and Akira Daikubara, it was Japan's first animated feature in colour. In 1961 it reached American cinemas as Panda and the Magic Serpent. Toei followed with roughly one feature a year: Magic Boy (1959), Saiyūki (1960), adapted from a manga by Osamu Tezuka and sold in the US as Alakazam the Great, and in 1963 The Little Prince and the Eight-Headed Dragon, whose flat, graphic designs and climactic dragon battle, scored by the Godzilla composer Akira Ifukube, are still studied by animators.

The studio's real product turned out to be people. Isao Takahata joined in 1959 and Hayao Miyazaki in 1963. Reiko Okuyama, who applied in 1957 thinking Toei published children's books, was hired as an in-betweener on Hakujaden and went on to become one of the first women in Japan to serve as an animation director. Animators at Toei also organised. Its labour union was active and sometimes fierce, and in 1964 Miyazaki served as its chief secretary, with Takahata beside him. The politics of that union would shape the films they made together.

Elsewhere, the paper animator Noburō Ōfuji remade his silent Kujira (The Whale) in 1952 in coloured cellophane, and it was screened at the Cannes Film Festival in 1953. After his death in 1961 the Mainichi Film Awards created a prize in his name. The Ōfuji Noburō Award is still one of Japan's main honours for animation.

意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. Toei's founders measured themselves against Disney: full animation, lush colour, music, a story any family could follow. But they chose Asian legends rather than European fairy tales, partly to stand apart and partly to sell in Asia. The animators, led by Yasuji Mori, cared above all about character acting, the small gestures that make a drawn person seem to think. By the early 1960s, with The Little Prince and the Eight-Headed Dragon, the studio was moving away from Disney's round shapes towards flat, stylised design. The search for a Japanese way of animating had begun.

Interiority. Hakujaden is a story of longing across a boundary that should not be crossed: a spirit who gives up her power to be with a human. For an audience that had lived through grey years of war and hunger it offered colour and tenderness on a scale they had not seen in a Japanese film. For one teenager it did more. Miyazaki later wrote that after the film he stumbled home in the snow, ashamed of the cynical manga he had been drawing because it was fashionable, and cried all night. The heroine's sincerity made him want to make something as earnest. It is the feeling this whole history is named after: 感動, being moved, turning into a vocation.

Ripples

In Japan

Toei became the training ground for the industry. Takahata, Miyazaki, Yasuo Ōtsuka, Yōichi Kotabe and Reiko Okuyama all learned there, and its TV division would later make Mazinger Z, Sailor Moon, Dragon Ball and One Piece. On 31 July 2026 the studio marked seventy years.

Around the world

Export was the plan from the start. Hakujaden, Magic Boy and Alakazam the Great all reached American cinemas by 1961, the first Japanese animated features released there. In June 2026 Toei marked its seventieth year at the Annecy animation festival in France, the industry's biggest international meeting.

背景

The world around it

Japanese television began in 1953. The Crown Prince's wedding in 1959 and the Tokyo Olympics in 1964 sent sales of sets soaring, and the new channels needed hours of programmes to fill. Animation was the most expensive thing they could buy. A half-hour of cartoons in the Toei style, drawn on ones and twos, cost several times what a live-action children's show did.

Osamu Tezuka, born in 1928 and raised in Takarazuka, had trained as a doctor and then become the most popular cartoonist in Japan, with cinematic manga such as New Treasure Island (1947), Kimba and Astro Boy. He adored Disney and the Fleischer brothers, and he wanted to animate. In 1961 he set up his own studio, Mushi Production, and in 1963 sold Fuji TV a weekly Tetsuwan Atom at ¥550,000 an episode. It was far below the real cost, and Tezuka covered the gap with his manga income and with licensing: the confectioner Meiji Seika put Atom stickers in boxes of Marble chocolate, an early case of what Japan now calls the .

To make a cartoon every week for that price, Mushi stripped animation down. Drawings were held for three frames instead of one or two, so a second of film might use eight drawings instead of twenty-four. Shots were reused across episodes from a of stock footage. Faces stayed still while separate mouth cels flapped, a trick called . Camera moves across a single stood in for action. None of these devices was new; American studios such as UPA and Hanna-Barbera used many of them. But Mushi turned them into a system, and then into a style.

It worked. Astro Boy ran for 193 episodes and opened the floodgates. The same year brought Tetsujin 28-gō and Eightman; Toei answered with Ken the Wolf Boy. In 1965 Mushi's Jungle Emperor (Kimba the White Lion) became the first full-colour series made for Japanese TV. Toei's Sally the Witch (1966) began the genre; Tatsunoko's Mach GoGoGo (1967) became Speed Racer abroad; Star of the Giants (1968) led the boom on television; and Sazae-san (1969) started a run that has never ended.

Not everyone accepted the TV model. At Toei, Isao Takahata directed Hols: Prince of the Sun (1968), an ambitious feature whose budget was frozen mid-production, forcing scenes into still frames. It failed at the box office and Takahata was demoted. Mushi made a trilogy of erotic “Animerama” features for adults, ending with the watercolour Belladonna of Sadness (1973). Gō Nagai's Mazinger Z (1972) put a boy in the cockpit of a giant robot for the first time, and its die-cast metal toys turned robot shows into a merchandising business. In 1973 Mushi went bankrupt. Its people founded or filled Sunrise, Madhouse, Group TAC and Tezuka Productions: the industry's next generation of studios.

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. Tezuka had lived through the bombing of Osaka and trained in medicine, and his stories return again and again to the value of life. Astro Boy is a robot boy built by a grieving scientist to replace his dead son, then rejected because he cannot grow up. Named after the atom in the decade after Hiroshima, he is a gentle superweapon who wants to be human and defends a world that does not quite accept him. Osamu Dezaki, who directed Ashita no Joe, wanted stillness to carry emotion. At the peak of a scene he froze the action into a painted , or split the screen, or repeated a blow three times from three angles. Takahata, in Hols, wanted a hero who wins only when a whole village acts together, and a heroine, Hilda, torn between the evil that controls her and the people she has come to love. She is often called the first psychologically complex heroine in Japanese animation.

Interiority. Television made anime part of family life. Children watched the same heroes at the same hour every week in the living room and grew up alongside them. The dramas spoke to the work ethic of the economic miracle, all effort and endurance. Ashita no Joe went further. Joe Yabuki is a slum kid who boxes because it is the only thing that makes him feel alive, and the story ends, in the manga's last panel, with him slumped in his corner, burned out to “pure white ash”. When his rival Rikiishi died in the manga in 1970, the poet Shūji Terayama organised a real funeral and about 800 people came. When student radicals hijacked a plane that month, they named themselves after Joe. For a generation he stood for burning yourself out against a system you cannot beat.

Drawing less, meaning more

Film runs at 24 frames a second. Animate “on ones” and you draw a new picture for every frame. “On twos” holds each drawing for two frames, “on threes” for three. Switch between them and watch the ball. The background is a camera pan, which costs no extra drawings, so it stays smooth at every setting. That was the trick Mushi Production built a weekly show on.

The hold 止め

One drawing stays on screen while something else moves: the camera, an effect, a mouth. Directors such as Osamu Dezaki turned the held frame from a shortcut into a moment of emphasis.

The bank バンク

Finished sequences were filed and reused: the robot's launch, the special attack, the magical-girl transformation. Repetition saved money, and turned those sequences into rituals audiences waited for every week.

The lip flap 口パク

Only the mouth moves, swapped between a few shapes over a still face. Japanese studios usually record voices after animating, so lips are timed loosely. Akira was a rare exception: it recorded the dialogue first.

背景

The world around it

The 1973 oil shock ended Japan's years of double-digit growth, and the collapse of Mushi Production scattered its talent across new studios: Sunrise, Madhouse, Nippon Animation, alongside Toei, Tatsunoko and Tokyo Movie Shinsha. Toy companies became the main sponsors of TV animation, and robot shows became toy catalogues. Yet the audience was changing. The first generation raised on TV anime was now in high school and university, and it wanted more.

Space Battleship Yamato (1974) gave it to them. Produced by Yoshinobu Nishizaki with designs by the manga artist Leiji Matsumoto, it raises the wreck of the battleship Yamato, sunk in 1945, and rebuilds it as a starship to fetch a cure for an Earth poisoned by radioactive bombardment. On TV it lost to Heidi. In reruns it found teenagers, and in 1977 a compilation film drew lines around cinemas. Magazines for older fans followed, led by Animage in 1978, and fans began organising: fan clubs, cel collecting, and from 1975 the Comic Market.

Meanwhile Isao Takahata, Hayao Miyazaki and the designer Yōichi Kotabe made Heidi, Girl of the Alps (1974) after a research trip to Switzerland, then a rarity for TV animation. It began a tradition of literary adaptations later branded World Masterpiece Theater, carried by Takahata's 3000 Leagues in Search of Mother (1976) and Anne of Green Gables (1979). Miyazaki directed his first series, Future Boy Conan, for NHK in 1978, and his first feature, The Castle of Cagliostro, in 1979.

1979 was the hinge. Sunrise's Mobile Suit Gundam, directed by Yoshiyuki Tomino, told a war story in which giant robots were mass-produced weapons with model numbers, piloted by frightened teenagers. Its toy sponsor wanted brightly coloured super robots; ratings were poor and the show was cut from 52 episodes to 43. Then Bandai's plastic model kits, launched in 1980, and three compilation films turned it into a phenomenon. That same year brought Rintaro's film of Matsumoto's Galaxy Express 999, the long-running Doraemon, and The Rose of Versailles, from Riyoko Ikeda's shōjo manga about a woman raised as a man to command the Queen's guard.

Fans were becoming makers. Students in Osaka produced short opening films for the 1981 and 1983 Japan science-fiction conventions, DAICON III and IV, that astonished professionals. Several of them, including Hideaki Anno, would found the studio Gainax. In 1983 the columnist Akio Nakamori mocked the new fans as , and the label stuck. In December that year Dallos became the first , made to be sold straight to fans on tape. And in 1982 Super Dimension Fortress Macross combined transforming fighter jets, a love triangle and a teenage pop idol whose songs end an interstellar war.

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. Yamato resurrects the symbol of Japan's defeat as a ship that saves the world, and critics have argued ever since whether that is nationalist nostalgia or a story of sacrifice for peace; it can be read both ways. Tomino wanted a war with no simple villains: both sides of Gundam's war have reasons, its heroes do terrible things, and its hope lies in “Newtypes”, people who might come to understand one another without words. Takahata believed daily life was drama enough. In Heidi the events are cheese toasting over a fire, goat milk, weather, and a homesick child in a city flat. Shōji Kawamori and the makers of Macross asked what would happen if culture, not weapons, decided a war.

Interiority. These were the first anime that teenagers felt took them seriously. Amuro Ray is sulky, frightened and resentful, and when he is slapped he cries that not even his father has ever hit him. Many fans recognised themselves in a robot pilot for the first time. In Galaxy Express 999 a boy crosses the galaxy to get a machine body that will let him live forever, and learns that a life which ends is the only kind that means anything. Its last farewell, to the mysterious Maetel, plays as a farewell to boyhood. Heidi's sleepwalking in Frankfurt shows a child's homesickness as an illness, without melodrama. And around these works a community grew whose members found, at conventions and fan markets, that they were not alone.

The Golden Age

Bubble-economy money, home video and a generation of directors at full strength produced the densest run of masterpieces anime has known, culminating in a single year: 1988.

背景

The world around it

In the second half of the 1980s Japan's asset bubble made money easy. Publishers, toy makers, broadcasters, advertising agencies and trading companies all wanted a piece of media, and animation was a place to put it. A new model of financing spread: the , in which several companies share a film's cost and rights. Akira was made by one, the Akira Committee, whose members included the publisher Kodansha, the toy maker Bandai, the studio Toho, the agency Hakuhodo and the trading house Sumitomo.

Home video added a second market. After Dallos in 1983, multiplied: the cyberpunk Bubblegum Crisis (1987), the realist police-robot drama Patlabor (1988), Hideaki Anno's directing debut Gunbuster (1988), and the space opera Legend of the Galactic Heroes, which ran to 110 episodes between 1988 and 1997. Fans would pay for stories too adult, too long or too strange for television. Television itself was enjoying a golden age of manga adaptations from Weekly Shōnen Jump: Fist of the North Star (1984), Dragon Ball (1986), Saint Seiya (1986) and City Hunter (1987).

Above all, it was an age of directors. Hayao Miyazaki had begun Nausicaä as a manga in Animage in 1982, partly because investors would not fund an original film; the film version, released in 1984, was a hit, and in 1985 he, Isao Takahata and producer Toshio Suzuki founded Studio Ghibli. Mamoru Oshii made the dream-logic comedy Urusei Yatsura 2: Beautiful Dreamer (1984) and the near-wordless Angel's Egg (1985). A team of twenty-somethings at the new studio Gainax made Royal Space Force: The Wings of Honnêamise (1987), funded by Bandai with a total cost, including advertising, of about ¥800 million. Yoshiyuki Tomino closed the first Gundam saga with Char's Counterattack (1988).

Then came 1988. On 16 April Ghibli released My Neighbor Totoro and Grave of the Fireflies as a double bill, the second adapted from Akiyuki Nosaka's semi-autobiographical 1967 story of two children in the firebombing of Kobe. On 16 July Katsuhiro Ōtomo's Akira opened: around 160,000 cels and 327 colours, 50 of them mixed for the film; dialogue recorded before animation so that lips could match it; a Tokyo of 2019, rebuilt after a third world war, rendered in more detail than any animated city before.

The decade ended badly. Emperor Hirohito died on 7 January 1989 and Osamu Tezuka a month later. In July the police arrested Tsutomu Miyazaki, who had murdered four young girls. Reporters found 5,763 videotapes in his room, among them anime and horror films, and named him “the otaku murderer”. A moral panic followed, and for years meant something sinister. Six days after the arrest, Ghibli released Kiki's Delivery Service, the studio's first big box-office hit. On the last trading day of the year the Nikkei index peaked. Within months the bubble had begun to burst.

What they reached for, and how it felt

Akira turns adolescent rage into body horror: Tetsuo, always the one rescued by his friend Kaneda, gains godlike power and his body swells out of control. Kiki is about a young woman alone in a new city who suddenly loses her ability to fly, the clearest picture of burnout and creative block Ghibli ever made.

How anime makes space, time and feeling

By the late 1980s Japanese animation had a visual language of its own. Some of it came from economy, some from individual artists, and some from older habits of picture-making. Here are its main working parts, starting with the one scholars argue about most: how anime makes depth.

Layers that slide

Anime usually makes depth by sliding flat layers past each other at different speeds: near things rush, far things drift, and the viewer never travels into the picture. The film theorist Thomas Lamarre called this “animetism”.

A camera that moves in

Disney's multiplane camera, introduced in 1937, did the opposite. It spaced painted layers vertically and moved the camera down through them, so the audience seemed to travel into depth. Lamarre called that “cinematism”.

Why it matters

Sliding layers are cheap, and they produce a particular feeling: watching a world go by, as from a train. It is one reason trains, skies and long lateral journeys recur so often in anime.

背景

The world around it

The bubble burst in 1990 and 1991. Asset prices fell for more than a decade, companies stopped hiring for life, and young people faced a labour market of temporary work. 1995 felt like a hinge: the Great Hanshin earthquake killed more than 6,400 people in January, and in March the cult Aum Shinrikyō released sarin on the Tokyo subway. Windows 95 and the internet arrived the same year. The mood was anxious, and the most influential anime of the decade turned that anxiety into stories about the self.

Neon Genesis Evangelion began on 4 October 1995 in an early-evening slot on TV Tokyo. Its director, Hideaki Anno, had come out of four years of depression after his previous series, and wrote that he had tried to put everything of himself into the show. It starts as a giant-robot story about a fourteen-year-old who must pilot a biomechanical weapon against monstrous “Angels”. By its last episodes, made in a production crisis, it had moved entirely inside its characters' minds. The final two episodes infuriated many fans. Anno answered with a film, The End of Evangelion (1997). The series' success taught producers that adult fans would buy expensive video, and late-night anime aimed at them became a large part of the schedule.

A month later came Mamoru Oshii's Ghost in the Shell, made at Production I.G and co-financed by Kodansha, Bandai Visual and the British label Manga Entertainment, an early sign of foreign money. It underperformed in cinemas, then in August 1996 became the first Japanese animated film to reach No. 1 on Billboard's home video sales chart. The Wachowskis showed it to producer Joel Silver when pitching The Matrix: “We wanna do that for real.”

Ghibli had its strongest decade. Takahata made Only Yesterday (1991) and Pom Poko (1994). Miyazaki's Porco Rosso (1992), first planned as a 45-minute in-flight film for Japan Airlines, grew into a feature. Whisper of the Heart (1995) was directed by Yoshifumi Kondō, seen as the studio's heir until his death in 1998 at the age of 47. And Princess Mononoke (1997) set Japan's box-office record, seen by twelve million people in its first five months.

Meanwhile television sent anime around the world. Sailor Moon (1992) became a global icon for girls. Pokémon, a game from 1996, became an anime in April 1997; in December an episode's flashing lights caused photosensitive seizures, and broadcasters adopted guidelines on flashing images that still apply. In the US, Cartoon Network's Toonami block (1997) showed Dragon Ball Z and Sailor Moon to millions, and Pokémon: The First Movie opened at No. 1 in November 1999. Shinichirō Watanabe's Cowboy Bebop (1998) was judged too adult for its first broadcaster, which aired only twelve episodes; the complete series ran on the satellite channel WOWOW. On 20 October 1999 One Piece began. It is still running.

意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. Anno wanted to tell the truth about depression, about people who hurt each other because they are afraid of being hurt. Oshii asked what remains of a person when her body is manufactured and her memories can be edited: the “ghost” in the shell. Satoshi Kon's Perfect Blue (1997) asked the same question of fame, following a pop idol whose sense of self breaks apart as she becomes an actress and is stalked through an online diary written in her name. Miyazaki built Princess Mononoke around a war between forest gods and an ironworks town led by Lady Eboshi, who shelters lepers and former brothel workers. Nobody in it is simply wrong. Its hero sets out, in his own words, to “see with eyes unclouded” and judge only then. Kunihiko Ikuhara's Revolutionary Girl Utena (1997) took the princess story apart and asked who gets to be the prince.

Interiority. No anime before Evangelion had spent so long inside a frightened mind. Shinji Ikari repeats 「逃げちゃダメだ」, “I mustn't run away”, and climbs into the robot because it is the only way his father will look at him. The show borrows Schopenhauer's hedgehog's dilemma: the closer we get, the more we hurt each other. For many viewers, especially young men in a stagnating Japan, it was the first time a story described exactly what they felt. Cowboy Bebop offered a different interior, stylish and melancholy. Each of its bounty hunters is running from a past, and its hero says he isn't going somewhere to die, but to find out whether he's really alive. Serial Experiments Lain (1998) imagined the internet as a place where identity dissolves, years before most people were online.

Digital tools ended the cel era, and the world's top film prizes went to a hand-drawn Japanese fantasy. Meanwhile one man with a desktop computer, and fans online, showed that anime no longer needed a big studio or a broadcaster to reach the world.

背景

The world around it

Around the turn of the century Japanese studios finished replacing painted cels with digital ink and paint and computer compositing. Lines became cleaner, colour gradients and lighting effects cheaper, and 3D models began to appear among the drawings. The department, once the people who photographed cels, became the place where much of a show's atmosphere was made.

The decade's defining film was hand-drawn. Spirited Away (2001) follows ten-year-old Chihiro, whose parents turn into pigs at a spirit world's banquet and who must work in a bathhouse for gods to save them. In Japan it earned ¥30.8 billion on first release. It shared the Golden Bear at Berlin with Bloody Sunday in 2002, and in 2003 won the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature, the first for a non-English-language film. Miyazaki did not go to the ceremony. Years later he told the Los Angeles Times: “The reason I wasn't here for the Academy Award was because I didn't want to visit a country that was bombing Iraq.” His next film, Howl's Moving Castle (2004), placed a pointless war at the centre of a story that, in the original novel, had little of one.

Directors flourished. Satoshi Kon followed Perfect Blue with Millennium Actress (2001), Tokyo Godfathers (2003), the TV series Paranoia Agent (2004) and Paprika (2006). Mamoru Oshii's Ghost in the Shell 2: Innocence became, in 2004, the first anime to compete for the Palme d'Or at Cannes. Masaaki Yuasa's Mind Game (2004) exploded animation into collage. Mamoru Hosoda made The Girl Who Leapt Through Time (2006) and Summer Wars (2009). And Makoto Shinkai, a former game-company employee, wrote, directed, animated and edited Voices of a Distant Star over seven months on a Power Mac G4, voicing the first version himself with his partner.

Shōnen series built a global audience: Naruto (2002), Bleach (2004), Fullmetal Alchemist (2003) and its remake Brotherhood (2009), Death Note (2006). Late-night television grew crowded. Kyoto Animation's The Melancholy of Haruhi Suzumiya (2006) spread online through fans copying its closing dance, and its Lucky Star (2007) sent fans on to Washinomiya Shrine, whose town in 2008 registered the show's fictional sisters as residents. Gainax's Gurren Lagann (2007) and the meditative Mushishi (2005) showed the range.

The internet changed distribution before the industry did. Fans subtitled episodes within hours and shared them through BitTorrent; sites like Crunchyroll, founded in 2006 on fan uploads, moved to licensed streaming as Japanese companies saw the audience and started selling to it. The government noticed too. In 2008 the foreign ministry named Doraemon Japan's “anime ambassador”, and in 2010 the economy ministry set up an office to promote what was being called Cool Japan.

Behind the fantasy sits a Japan that had lost itself in the bubble: the theme park ruin, the parents gorging, No-Face trying to buy affection with gold. Shinkai wanted to show how ordinary light, a convenience store at night or a train platform at dusk, can break your heart. Imaishi's Gurren Lagann wanted to make you shout.

Interiority. The decade's great films are about holding on to a self. Chihiro forgets her name and almost forgets her parents. The most remembered scene in the film is the quietest: a train ride across a flooded plain, Chihiro and No-Face sitting side by side as stations slide past, a passage of pure . 5 Centimeters per Second (2007) takes its title from the speed at which cherry blossoms fall, and follows a boy whose first love becomes a longing he carries into a numb adulthood. Haruhi's heroine is so bored by ordinary life that she may be rewriting the universe to make it interesting. Fullmetal Alchemist's brothers pay for one act of grief, trying to bring back their dead mother, with their bodies, and spend the series atoning.

After the Wave

The earthquake and tsunami of March 2011 changed what Japanese stories needed to say. Streaming let Japan and the world watch the same episode on the same night. And in 2019 a fire at Kyoto Animation showed how much this art had come to mean to people everywhere.

背景

The world around it

On 11 March 2011 the strongest earthquake ever recorded in Japan sent a tsunami into the coast of Tōhoku, killing or leaving missing more than 18,000 people, and caused meltdowns at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant. In the weeks after, broadcasters practised jishuku, self-restraint. Puella Magi Madoka Magica, a magical-girl series that had turned out to be about despair and sacrifice, had its final two episodes postponed until 21 April. They aired back to back to an audience that had been waiting through a national catastrophe.

The decade's stories kept returning to disaster, memory and the wish to undo the past. Makoto Shinkai said Your Name (2016), in which a comet destroys a lakeside town, came directly from the earthquake. It earned about ¥25 billion in Japan and became, at the time, the highest-grossing anime film worldwide. That autumn also brought Naoko Yamada's A Silent Voice, about a former bully and the deaf girl he tormented, and Sunao Katabuchi's In This Corner of the World, about a young wife's daily life in wartime Kure and Hiroshima. Katabuchi could not raise money for it from the usual investors; 3,374 people funded its pilot through the crowdfunding site Makuake, then a Japanese record.

The masters made their farewells. Miyazaki's The Wind Rises (2013) is a biography of Jiro Horikoshi, designer of the Zero fighter, and an honest look at an artist whose beautiful dream became a weapon. He announced his retirement that September (it did not last). Takahata spent eight years and ¥5.15 billion on The Tale of the Princess Kaguya (2013), drawn in charcoal and watercolour strokes that seem to be still drying. He died in 2018.

The audience went global in real time. Crunchyroll and others began streaming episodes abroad within hours of Japanese broadcast, and Netflix started commissioning anime of its own, such as Masaaki Yuasa's Devilman Crybaby (2018). Attack on Titan (2013–2023) became a worldwide obsession; in 2021 the analytics firm Parrot Analytics named it the most in-demand TV show in the world, the first non-English series to top its list. Light and web novels fed an boom of stories about being reborn in another world. Web-native animators were recruited onto shows such as One-Punch Man (2015) and Mob Psycho 100 (2016). And in 2019 ufotable's Demon Slayer showed what digital compositing could add to hand-drawn action.

On 18 July 2019 a man walked into Kyoto Animation's Studio 1 carrying petrol and set the building alight. Thirty-six people were killed and more than thirty injured, most of them artists. It was one of the deadliest mass killings in Japan since the Second World War. Donations of about ¥3.3 billion came from within Japan and more than US$2 million from abroad, and the studio passed all of it to victims and their families. The attacker was sentenced to death in January 2024; in 2025 he withdrew his appeal and the sentence became final.

意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. Shinkai wanted to imagine a world in which the people of a doomed town could be saved, and gave his audience the ending reality had refused. Katabuchi wanted to show that ordinary life went on under the bombs: cooking with weeds, mending clothes, drawing. He spent six years researching wartime Kure, gathering thousands of photographs and checking the weather for every day of the story. Yamada, whose camera lingers on hands and feet, wanted to show how hard it is for people to reach each other. Miyazaki took the epigraph of The Wind Rises from Paul Valéry, “The wind is rising, we must try to live”, and gave his designer's mentor the line that airplanes are beautiful dreams and cursed dreams at once. Takahata wanted to show the joy of being alive on Earth, and the cost of being kept from it. Hajime Isayama built Attack on Titan on walls, and then made his readers question who was really outside them.

Interiority. These are stories about grief and repair. In Your Name two people search for someone they can no longer remember, a feeling of loss without an object that many viewers recognised after 2011. A Silent Voice covers every face around its anxious hero with an X, and lets them fall away one by one as he learns to look at people again. In In This Corner of the World the heroine loses her right hand, her drawing hand, and the film's style changes with her grief. Violet Evergarden (2018), from Kyoto Animation, follows a former child soldier who writes letters for others while trying to understand the last words her commander said to her: “I love you.” Madoka ends with a girl who saves everyone by becoming a principle of the universe, and so can never be seen by her friends again.

The Whole World Watching

When a pandemic closed the world's doors, anime became its comfort and a common language. By the mid-2020s more of its money came from abroad than from Japan, its films set global box-office records, and one of its symbols flew over protests from Jakarta to Kathmandu.

背景

The world around it

The pandemic shut cinemas and sent the world indoors, onto streaming services with deep anime libraries. Demand surged. Netflix said in 2025 that more than half of its members worldwide watch anime, about 150 million households, that anime viewing on the service had tripled in five years, and that most of those viewers watch it dubbed. Crunchyroll, bought by Sony in 2021 for $1.175 billion, passed 21 million paying subscribers by March 2026. Episodes now launch simultaneously around the world in dozens of languages.

The money followed the audience. The Association of Japanese Animations put the industry's 2024 revenue at a record ¥3.84 trillion, of which ¥2.17 trillion was earned abroad, the third year in which overseas income exceeded domestic. In 2024 Japan's government set a goal of ¥20 trillion a year in overseas sales for its creative industries by 2033. Box-office records fell repeatedly. Demon Slayer: Mugen Train (2020) took more than ¥40 billion in Japan. Its sequel Infinity Castle (2025) opened to $70.6 million in North America, the biggest debut ever for an anime film, and earned about $820 million worldwide, the most for any Japanese film; at home it became only the second film ever to pass ¥40 billion.

The art kept widening. Hideaki Anno finished Evangelion with 3.0+1.0 Thrice Upon a Time (2021). Takehiko Inoue directed his own The First Slam Dunk (2022) in a hybrid of 3D animation and hand-drawn line. Makoto Shinkai's Suzume (2022) faced the 2011 earthquake directly and competed at the Berlin Film Festival. CloverWorks' Bocchi the Rock! (2022) turned social anxiety into visual comedy, and Madhouse's Frieren (2023) took the top spot of MyAnimeList's rankings from Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood. Kiyotaka Oshiyama's Look Back (2024), an hour-long film about two girls who draw manga, won the Japan Academy Prize. Sources grew more varied too: the Korean webtoon Solo Leveling became a hit anime in 2024, and in 2026 the year's biggest film in Japan was Chiikawa, adapted from cartoons first posted on social media.

Strains showed. A 2024 survey by the animators' association NAFCA found that more than 40 percent of animators earned under ¥200,000 a month. Japan's Freelance Act, in force from November 2024, gave freelance staff new protections. In March 2025 a wave of AI-generated images “in the style of Studio Ghibli” swept social media, and a clip of Miyazaki from 2016 calling an AI demonstration “an insult to life itself” spread with it, often without its context: he had been shown a computer-generated figure dragging itself by its head and said it reminded him of a friend with a disability. And anime's influence on the rest of animation became plain. In March 2026 the Oscar for animated feature went to KPop Demon Hunters, an American film whose directors named Sailor Moon and Cowboy Bebop among their models.

How do you live?

Japanese title of Miyazaki's 2023 film, 君たちはどう生きるか, borrowed from Genzaburō Yoshino's 1937 novel for young readers
意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. The decade's elders wrote endings. Miyazaki, in his eighties, made The Boy and the Heron about a boy grieving his mother in wartime who is offered the chance to inherit a crumbling world built by his great-uncle, and chooses instead to go back and live in the flawed one. He gave it the title of a book he had read as a child: How Do You Live? Anno ended Evangelion by letting Shinji grow up, and shot the last scene at Ube-Shinkawa station in his own home town. The younger makers were reaching elsewhere. Demon Slayer's Koyoharu Gotōge built a blockbuster on compassion for the defeated. Frieren's writer Kanehito Yamada began a fantasy where others end, after the hero has won, to ask what the journey meant. Tatsuki Fujimoto's Look Back asks why anyone keeps drawing after loss.

Interiority. This was the era when audiences most visibly used anime to feel together. Japanese cinemas filled with people in masks crying at Mugen Train's ending, and its fallen hero's motto, 「心を燃やせ」, “set your heart ablaze”, became a phrase of the pandemic. Bocchi the Rock! made social anxiety funny without mocking it, and many viewers said they had never seen their own inner life drawn so precisely. Frieren moved millions with grief in slow motion: an elf who, decades after her companion's death, realises she never really tried to know him while he was alive, and sets out to understand people. For a generation of young protesters, the Straw Hat flag held a whole inner world: the loyalty of a found family, and contempt for a corrupt world government.

Glossary

The words of the trade

Japanese terms used in this history, with their characters. Dotted words throughout the page open these definitions in place.

animeアニメ
Japanese shortening of animēshon, from English “animation”. It came into everyday use in the 1970s. Before that people said dōga or manga eiga (cartoon films), and TV anime was often called terebi manga.
bankバンク
A library of finished footage reused across episodes: a robot launching, a weapon firing, a magical girl transforming. Cheap to repeat, and repetition turned it into ritual.
benshi弁士
Live narrators who stood beside the screen during silent films, voicing characters and explaining the story. Often more famous than the films.
celセル
A clear celluloid (later acetate) sheet. Characters were traced and painted on cels, which were laid over painted backgrounds and photographed. Japanese TV stopped using them in 2013.
compositing撮影
Literally “photography”. Once the filming of cels; now the digital department that layers drawings, backgrounds and effects and adds light, blur and atmosphere.
dōga動画
Literally “moving picture”. The older Japanese word for animation (as in Tōei Dōga), and also the studio term for in-between drawings.
emakimono絵巻物
Illustrated hand scrolls, read right to left an arm's width at a time. The golden age of the form was the 12th and 13th centuries.
genga原画
Key animation: the drawings at the important poses of a movement. Key animators are credited individually, and fans follow their work.
harmony celハーモニー
A frame in which the character is painted in the textured style of the background, freezing a key moment like an illustration. Osamu Dezaki made it his signature; fans call these frames “postcard memories”.
henshin変身
Transformation: the sequence in which a hero or magical girl changes into their powered form. Usually bank footage.
hold止め
A single drawing kept on screen for a stretch of time, often with a camera move across it. The basic tool of limited animation.
in-betweens中割り
The drawings that fill the gaps between key drawings. Usually made by junior animators, and often outsourced.
isekai異世界
“Other world”. Stories in which someone is transported or reborn into a fantasy world. A dominant genre of the 2010s, much of it adapted from web novels.
Itano Circus板野サーカス
Swarms of missiles corkscrewing through the frame on smoke trails, as animated by Ichirō Itano for Macross (1982) and much imitated since.
iyashikei癒し系
“Healing type”. Gentle, low-conflict works meant to soothe, such as Mushishi or Aria.
josei女性
Works aimed at adult women.
kamishibai紙芝居
“Paper theatre”: a storyteller slides illustrated boards through a small wooden frame. Popular with children from about 1930 to the 1950s; several manga artists started in it.
kuchipaku口パク
Lip flap. Mouths are drawn on separate layers in a few shapes and swapped over a held face, so dialogue costs very few drawings.
ma間
The interval between things: a pause, an emptiness left on purpose. Hayao Miyazaki used the word for the quiet moments in his films.
magical girl魔法少女
A genre of girls with magical powers, from Sally the Witch (1966) to Sailor Moon (1992) and its deconstruction in Madoka Magica (2011).
manga漫画
Comics, and the source of a large share of anime. The word was popularised by Hokusai's sketchbooks (1814 onward), where it meant something like “whimsical pictures”.
mechaメカ
Robots and machines, and the genre built around them. “Super robots” (Mazinger Z) are near-mythic; “real robots” (Gundam) are mass-produced weapons.
media mixメディアミックス
Telling one story and selling one character across manga, TV, film, toys, games and merchandise at once. Scholar Marc Steinberg traces it to Astro Boy's chocolate-sticker campaign of 1963.
moe萌え
Literally “budding”. A protective, affectionate attachment to fictional characters, and the designs built to provoke it.
mono no aware物の哀れ
A classical Japanese term for a gentle sadness at the passing of things, such as falling cherry blossoms.
mukokuseki無国籍
“Stateless”. Characters and settings without clear ethnic or national markers. Scholar Kōichi Iwabuchi argued that this “culturally odourless” quality helped anime travel.
otakuオタク
A devoted fan. Popularised as a mocking label by the columnist Akio Nakamori in 1983, stigmatised after a 1989 murder case, and slowly reclaimed from the 2000s.
OVAオリジナル・ビデオ・アニメ
Original video animation: made for sale on tape or disc rather than for TV or cinema. The first was Dallos (1983). OVAs gave niche and adult stories a market.
production committee製作委員会
A group of companies (publisher, broadcaster, toy maker, music label, distributor) that jointly fund an anime and split its rights. It spreads risk; the studio that makes the show often owns none of it.
sakuga作画
Drawing, or the quality of the drawing and animation. Among fans it means a standout sequence of animation, celebrated for the individual animator who made it.
seichi junrei聖地巡礼
“Pilgrimage to sacred places”: fans travelling to the real locations behind a work's backgrounds, such as Washinomiya Shrine (Lucky Star) or the stairs in Your Name.
seinen青年
Works aimed at adult men, often from magazines such as Young Magazine (where Akira ran).
seiyū声優
Voice actor. In Japan a recognised profession with its own stars, concerts and fan culture.
sekai-keiセカイ系
“World type”. Stories in which a teenage relationship is tied directly to the fate of the world, with society in between left out. Usually traced to Evangelion.
shōjo少女
“Girls”. A marketing category whose manga, from the 1970s on, developed much of the medium's language of feeling and inner life.
shōnen少年
“Boys”. A marketing category (from manga magazines such as Weekly Shōnen Jump), not a genre: action, sport, comedy and romance all appear in it.
simulcast同時配信
Streaming an episode abroad, subtitled, within hours of its Japanese broadcast. Standard since the 2010s.
spokonスポ根
Sports plus konjō (guts): stories of training, suffering and will, such as Star of the Giants and Tomorrow's Joe.
utsushi-e写し絵
An Edo-period magic lantern show in which performers moved hand-held wooden projectors behind a paper screen, so painted images walked, flew and transformed.

つづく

Tsuzuku: “to be continued”. Generations of Japanese viewers saw the word at the end of an episode. A century after a boy in a sailor suit bowed to the camera, anime is made in Tokyo and Seoul, financed from Riyadh and Los Angeles, and watched by hundreds of millions of people who will never visit Japan. What has not changed is what it does. It makes drawings move, and the people watching them are moved.
