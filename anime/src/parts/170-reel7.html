Reel 07

2000 — 2010

The Digital Spirit

Digital tools ended the cel era, and the world's top film prizes went to a hand-drawn Japanese fantasy. Meanwhile one man with a desktop computer, and fans online, showed that anime no longer needed a big studio or a broadcaster to reach the world.

デジタル

背景

The world around it

Around the turn of the century Japanese studios finished replacing painted cels with digital ink and paint and computer compositing. Lines became cleaner, colour gradients and lighting effects cheaper, and 3D models began to appear among the drawings. The [[satsuei|compositing]] department, once the people who photographed cels, became the place where much of a show's atmosphere was made.

The decade's defining film was hand-drawn. Spirited Away (2001) follows ten-year-old Chihiro, whose parents turn into pigs at a spirit world's banquet and who must work in a bathhouse for gods to save them. In Japan it earned ¥30.8 billion on first release. It shared the Golden Bear at Berlin with Bloody Sunday in 2002, and in 2003 won the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature, the first for a non-English-language film. Miyazaki did not go to the ceremony. Years later he told the Los Angeles Times: “The reason I wasn't here for the Academy Award was because I didn't want to visit a country that was bombing Iraq.” His next film, Howl's Moving Castle (2004), placed a pointless war at the centre of a story that, in the original novel, had little of one.

Directors flourished. Satoshi Kon followed Perfect Blue with Millennium Actress (2001), Tokyo Godfathers (2003), the TV series Paranoia Agent (2004) and Paprika (2006). Mamoru Oshii's Ghost in the Shell 2: Innocence became, in 2004, the first anime to compete for the Palme d'Or at Cannes. Masaaki Yuasa's Mind Game (2004) exploded animation into collage. Mamoru Hosoda made The Girl Who Leapt Through Time (2006) and Summer Wars (2009). And Makoto Shinkai, a former game-company employee, wrote, directed, animated and edited Voices of a Distant Star over seven months on a Power Mac G4, voicing the first version himself with his partner.

Shōnen series built a global audience: Naruto (2002), Bleach (2004), Fullmetal Alchemist (2003) and its remake Brotherhood (2009), Death Note (2006). Late-night television grew crowded. Kyoto Animation's The Melancholy of Haruhi Suzumiya (2006) spread online through fans copying its closing dance, and its Lucky Star (2007) sent fans on [[seichi]] to Washinomiya Shrine, whose town in 2008 registered the show's fictional sisters as residents. Gainax's Gurren Lagann (2007) and the meditative Mushishi (2005) showed the range.

The internet changed distribution before the industry did. Fans subtitled episodes within hours and shared them through BitTorrent; sites like Crunchyroll, founded in 2006 on fan uploads, moved to licensed streaming as Japanese companies saw the audience and started selling to it. The government noticed too. In 2008 the foreign ministry named Doraemon Japan's “anime ambassador”, and in 2010 the economy ministry set up an office to promote what was being called Cool Japan.

意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. Miyazaki wanted to make a film for ten-year-old girls, who he felt were offered little but romance stories. He gave Chihiro no special powers, only the will to work and to remember her name. Behind the fantasy sits a Japan that had lost itself in the bubble: the theme park ruin, the parents gorging, No-Face trying to buy affection with gold. Satoshi Kon wanted to show how memory, film and fantasy overlap, and in Millennium Actress he made a woman's whole life into one chase through the history of Japanese cinema. Shinkai wanted to show how ordinary light, a convenience store at night or a train platform at dusk, can break your heart. Imaishi's Gurren Lagann wanted to make you shout.

Interiority. The decade's great films are about holding on to a self. Chihiro forgets her name and almost forgets her parents. The most remembered scene in the film is the quietest: a train ride across a flooded plain, Chihiro and No-Face sitting side by side as stations slide past, a passage of pure [[ma]]. 5 Centimeters per Second (2007) takes its title from the speed at which cherry blossoms fall, and follows a boy whose first love becomes a longing he carries into a numb adulthood. Haruhi's heroine is so bored by ordinary life that she may be rewriting the universe to make it interesting. Fullmetal Alchemist's brothers pay for one act of grief, trying to bring back their dead mother, with their bodies, and spend the series atoning.

Spirited Away千と千尋の神隠し

Hayao Miyazaki · Studio Ghibli · Golden Bear 2002 · Academy Award 2003

Ten-year-old Chihiro is sulking in the back seat as her family moves to a new town. A wrong turn leads them through a tunnel into what looks like an abandoned theme park. Her parents gorge themselves on food laid out for the spirits and turn into pigs, and to save them Chihiro must take a job in a bathhouse for the gods, run by a witch, Yubaba, who keeps her workers by taking their names.

It says
You keep yourself by remembering your name and doing your work. Greed turns people into pigs; kindness can redeem even a monster.
It feels
Strange and generous: a bathhouse of gods, a river spirit choked with rubbish, a boy who is really a buried river.
It changed
It became Japan's highest-grossing film for nearly two decades and the most decorated anime ever made, and it made Ghibli a household name worldwide.
How it was made

Miyazaki had said that Princess Mononoke (1997) would be his last feature. The idea that brought him back came on holiday at his mountain cabin, with the young daughters of friends: he felt that no film spoke to girls of their age. As usual he began without a finished script, storyboarding as production went on. By now Ghibli painted and composited its drawings on computers, but every movement was still drawn by hand.

Not a story in which the characters grow up, but a story in which they draw on something already inside them, brought out by the particular circumstances.

Hayao Miyazaki, interviewed by Midnight Eye, 2002
Why it lasts

It trusts silence and strangeness. The train ride across the flooded plain moves the plot on by almost nothing, and it is the scene people remember. Nothing is explained: No-Face, the radish spirit and the soot sprites simply exist, the way the world does to a child. And its heroine is, as Miyazaki intended, an ordinary girl, who wins by courage and courtesy rather than by magic.

Look forThe “stink spirit” scene. Miyazaki has said it came from his own experience of cleaning up a river.

Millennium Actress千年女優

Satoshi Kon · Madhouse

A small documentary crew visits Chiyoko Fujiwara, a great film star who vanished from public life decades ago. The interviewer, a lifelong fan, returns a key she lost long ago. As she tells her story, he and his cameraman find themselves inside it, following her from wartime Manchuria to feudal castles to the surface of the moon, as she pursues a man she met only once, as a girl.

It says
What we chase may matter less than the chasing. A retired star tells her life as one long pursuit of a man she met once.
It feels
Joyous and aching: her memories and her film roles blur, and the interviewers keep stumbling into the scenes.
It changed
It showed what animated editing could do that live action could not, cutting from one century to another within a single movement.
How it was made

After Perfect Blue (1997), a thriller about a pop idol losing her grip on reality, Kon turned the same blurring of memory and fiction to warmer ends. With the writer Sadayuki Murai he built the film as a tour of Japanese cinema, through samurai epics, Kurosawa's Throne of Blood, monster movies, postwar melodrama and science fiction, with each of Chiyoko's roles standing for an era. She is loosely modelled on the great stars Setsuko Hara and Hideko Takamine. Susumu Hirasawa's electronic score drives her on.

Why it lasts

It is a love letter to Japanese cinema and a gentle argument about memory: that we remember our lives as stories, with ourselves in the lead. Kon completed only four features before he died, and this is the warmest of them. Its last line, which recasts the whole chase, is famous; don't look it up before you watch.

Look forChiyoko running. Again and again she runs, on foot, on horseback, by bicycle, out of one film and one era into the next, and the cut never stops to tell you the century has changed.

Voices of a Distant Starほしのこえ

Makoto Shinkai, almost single-handed · 2002

2046. Mikako, fifteen, is chosen as a pilot for a UN fleet sent after the aliens who wiped out an expedition to Mars. Noboru, the classmate she likes, stays behind on Earth. Her only link to him is her mobile phone, and as the fleet travels deeper into space each text takes longer to arrive: days, then months, then years.

It says
Love survives distance, but time doesn't wait. Her messages from deep space take longer and longer to reach him.
It feels
Small, aching and luminous: a rainy bus stop, a phone screen, “I'm here.”
It changed
It proved one person with a computer could make anime that moved an audience, and launched the director of Your Name.
How it was made

Shinkai had been designing animated openings for video games at the company Falcom. He made Voices on his own in about seven months on a Power Mac G4, writing, directing, drawing, lighting and editing it with off-the-shelf software; the music was by Tenmon. For the first version he and his partner recorded the voices themselves, at home. It premiered at a small cinema in Shimokitazawa in February 2002 and went on sale on DVD.

Why it lasts

It found an exact image for a modern feeling: waiting for a reply. Years before smartphones, it made a text message the measure of a relationship, and its rain, convenience-store light and empty platforms became the vocabulary Shinkai still uses. It is also a defining example of [[sekaikei]], the story in which a teenage romance and the fate of the world are the same thing.

Look forThe time stamps. Each message shows when it was sent, and the gap between sending and reading grows into the whole story.

Mushishi蟲師

Hiroshi Nagahama · Artland · from Yuki Urushibara's manga

Ginko is a mushishi, a travelling specialist in mushi: the most basic forms of life, invisible to most people, which drift through rivers, rain and human bodies. He walks from village to village in an unnamed, timeless Japan of rice terraces and mountain mist. Each episode is a single case: a swamp that wanders, a light seen only behind closed eyelids, a pillow that brings dreams which come true.

It says
Nature is neither good nor evil, only alive. The “mushi”, primal life forms, cause harm only by being what they are.
It feels
Hushed and seasonal, like a book of folk tales read by lamplight: mist, rain, snow, and a doctor who never hurries.
It changed
It became the standard by which [[iyashikei]], the gentle “healing” genre, is measured.
How it was made

Urushibara drew Mushishi for Kodansha's Afternoon from 1999 to 2008, and Nagahama's adaptation keeps its form: self-contained tales with no running plot and no villain, only Ginko arriving, listening and leaving. Toshio Masuda's spare score leaves room for water, insects and wind. And every episode opens with a sound no one expected from a story set in old Japan: “The Sore Feet Song”, a gentle English-language folk song by the Scottish singer-songwriter Ally Kerr.

Why it lasts

It is patient. Ginko's job is to understand rather than to conquer, so each episode becomes a small essay on living alongside what cannot be controlled: illness, grief, the weather, time. It is best watched as it was made, one story at a time, and it rewards returning to like few series do.

Look forEpisode 1, “The Green Seat”: a boy whose drawings come to life, and a first, unhurried lesson in what a mushishi does.

Paprikaパプリカ

Satoshi Kon · Madhouse · from Yasutaka Tsutsui's novel

Scientists have built the DC Mini, a device that lets a therapist enter a patient's dreams. Dr Atsuko Chiba uses it secretly, at night, as Paprika, a playful alter ego who treats patients from inside their dreams. Then prototypes are stolen, and someone begins pouring dreams into waking minds, starting with a delirious parade that grows with every dreamer it swallows.

It says
Dreams are private for a reason. When a device lets them leak, the city is overrun by everyone's fantasies at once.
It feels
Exhilarating, funny, slightly frightening: a parade of fridges, dolls and frogs marching through Tokyo.
It changed
Kon's last completed film. Critics have often compared it with Christopher Nolan's Inception (2010).
How it was made

Kon and the writer Seishi Minakami cut Tsutsui's sprawling novel down to ninety minutes of transformations, with cuts that match one dream to the next and dreams to waking life. It came after Kon's TV series Paranoia Agent, and reunited him with Susumu Hirasawa, whose electronic march drives the parade. Tsutsui and Kon gave themselves cameos as the two bartenders of a bar that exists only in dreams.

With my heart full of gratitude for everything good in the world, I'll put down my pen. Now excuse me, I have to go.

Satoshi Kon, from “Sayonara”, the farewell he wrote before his death in August 2010 (translated by Makiko Itoh)
Why it lasts

Kon died of pancreatic cancer in 2010, aged 46, leaving a fifth feature, The Dreaming Machine, unfinished. His reputation has only grown since, and Paprika's vision of a city flooded with everyone's private fantasies now looks, to many viewers, like a premonition of life online.

Look forThe opening titles: Paprika crossing night-time Tokyo by jumping into pictures, from a billboard to the side of a truck to a passer-by's T-shirt.

The Melancholy of Haruhi Suzumiya涼宮ハルヒの憂鬱

Kyoto Animation · from Nagaru Tanigawa's light novels

Kyon, a deadpan high-school boy, sits in front of Haruhi Suzumiya, who announces on the first day of class that she is interested only in aliens, time travellers and espers. She founds a club to find them, the SOS Brigade, and drags him in. The other three members, Kyon soon learns, are exactly what Haruhi is looking for, and have been sent to keep an eye on her.

It says
Teenage boredom is cosmic. A girl who wants aliens, time travellers and espers may be unconsciously creating them.
It feels
Playful and self-aware, narrated by a deadpan boy; it was even first broadcast with its episodes deliberately out of order.
It changed
It made Kyoto Animation famous, light novels a major source for anime, and its closing dance, copied by fans and posted online, one of the first viral anime videos.
How it was made

Kyoto Animation had only recently begun making its own series after years of subcontracting for other studios, and Haruhi became its showcase. The first episode broadcast was a deliberately amateurish student film made by the characters, and the rest were shuffled out of order. When the series was rerun in 2009 with new episodes, the studio went further: “Endless Eight” told the same summer-holiday story eight weeks running, each time newly animated, a stunt that infuriated many fans and fascinated others.

Why it lasts

Beneath the gags is a story about the fear of being ordinary. In one episode Haruhi remembers going to a baseball game as a child and realising she was one person in a crowd of fifty thousand, and nothing special. Many viewers found their own teenage restlessness in that speech. The 2010 film, The Disappearance of Haruhi Suzumiya, told from Kyon's side, is often ranked among Kyoto Animation's finest work.

Look for“Live Alive”, in which Haruhi stands in as a rock band's singer at the school festival, and the animators follow the guitarists' fingers chord by chord.

5 Centimeters per Second秒速5センチメートル

Makoto Shinkai · CoMix Wave Films

Three linked stories follow Takaki Tōno from boyhood to adulthood. In “Cherry Blossom” he travels for hours through a snowstorm to see Akari, the friend who moved away. In “Cosmonaut” a classmate on the island of Tanegashima loves him without telling him. In the title story he is an adult in Tokyo, still carrying something he cannot name.

It says
Some loves don't end, they just recede at the speed of falling petals.
It feels
Exquisitely sad: a train delayed by snow, a rocket launch over Tanegashima, a crossing where two people nearly meet.
It changed
Its photographic light and lonely urban beauty became the template for a whole aesthetic of longing.
How it was made

The title comes from a line of Akari's: cherry petals fall at five centimetres per second. Shinkai built the film from three short pieces and from real places, photographed and painted over: Iwafune station in Tochigi, the space centre on Tanegashima, the level crossings of suburban Tokyo. Its final montage is cut to Masayoshi Yamazaki's 1997 song “One More Time, One More Chance”, and the two are now inseparable.

Why it lasts

It does not give its audience the ending that romance usually promises, and that is why people return to it: it is about how distance accumulates in small, undramatic ways, until a life has quietly turned. Its locations became [[seichi|pilgrimage sites]], and in October 2025 it was remade as a live-action film.

Look forThe messages that never arrive. Each part has one: a letter lost to the wind, texts typed to no one, a confession left unsaid.

Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood鋼の錬金術師 FULLMETAL ALCHEMIST

Yasuhiro Irie · Bones · from Hiromu Arakawa's manga

Two brothers, Edward and Alphonse Elric, try to bring their dead mother back with alchemy. It goes horribly wrong: Edward loses a leg, Alphonse his whole body, and Edward gives his right arm to bind his brother's soul to a suit of armour. Years later Edward is the youngest State Alchemist in the army of Amestris, searching for the Philosopher's Stone that could restore them, and finding instead the secret his country was built on.

It says
Nothing is gained without sacrifice, and no one is beyond redemption, not even soldiers who carried out a genocide.
It feels
Propulsive and warm, a war story and a family story at once, with a cast of dozens who all matter.
It changed
For more than a decade it sat at the top of MyAnimeList's user rankings, the default answer to “the best anime”.
How it was made

Arakawa grew up on a dairy farm in Tokachi, Hokkaido, and worked on it for seven years, taking painting classes, before she became a manga artist. Equivalent exchange, the law at the heart of her story, comes from farm life, where nothing is gained without labour. The first anime, in 2003, overtook the unfinished manga and invented its own ending. Brotherhood, made by the same studio six years later, went back to the beginning and followed the manga to its end: the last episode aired on 4 July 2010, three weeks after the final chapter was published.

A lesson without pain is meaningless. That's because no one can gain without sacrificing something. But by enduring that pain and overcoming it, he shall obtain a powerful, unmatched heart... a fullmetal heart.

The closing narration of the final episode, in the English version
Why it lasts

It is the rare long series with almost no wasted motion: early episodes pay off, minor characters get endings, and the story stops when its source does. Under the adventure runs a serious argument about the state, war guilt and the people who carry out orders, worked through characters on opposite sides of the same atrocity: the soldier Roy Mustang, and the vengeful Ishvalan known only as Scar.

Look forEpisode 30, “The Ishvalan War of Extermination”, in which the soldiers Edward admires remember what they did.

2009

波紋

Ripples

In Japan

  • Otaku went from suspect to marketable: the 2005 TV drama Densha Otoko made a fan its romantic hero, and Akihabara became a tourist sight.
  • Anime tourism began in earnest with Lucky Star's shrine.
  • The state adopted anime as soft power: Doraemon as ambassador (2008), Cool Japan (2010).

Around the world

Spirited Away won the West's top prizes. Naruto and Death Note became shared adolescence across continents. Conventions grew from fan meetings into mass events, and fansubbing communities, operating outside the law, built an audience that the industry would soon serve through streaming.

One story

An empty seat at the Oscars

When Spirited Away won in March 2003, days after the invasion of Iraq began, Miyazaki was not in the room. At the time the studio said he was busy. He later said he had refused to visit a country that was bombing Iraq.

FLCLフリクリ

2000 · OVA, 6 episodes · Kazuya Tsurumaki · Gainax, Production I.G

Naota, twelve, lives in a dull factory town under a giant building shaped like an iron, until a woman on a yellow Vespa hits him over the head with a bass guitar and robots start sprouting from his forehead. Kazuya Tsurumaki's six-episode [[ova|OVA]] turns puberty into manga-panel chaos, set to the alt-rock of The Pillows, and became a cult hit on American late-night TV.

Howl's Moving Castleハウルの動く城

2004 · Feature, 119 min · Hayao Miyazaki · Studio Ghibli

Sophie, a shy young hatter, is cursed by a witch into the body of a woman of ninety, and finds work as a cleaner in the walking castle of the vain wizard Howl. Miyazaki adapted Diana Wynne Jones's novel and added a war fought for reasons no one explains, which he has linked to his anger at the invasion of Iraq. It took ¥19.6 billion in Japan.

Ghost in the Shell 2: Innocenceイノセンス

2004 · Feature, 100 min · Mamoru Oshii · Production I.G

Oshii's sequel to his 1995 Ghost in the Shell follows Batou, the cyborg detective left behind when the Major vanished into the network, as he investigates android dolls that are killing their owners. Dense with quotations, from Descartes to the Bible, and with 3D-built cities of overwhelming detail, it was the first anime ever selected for the main competition at Cannes.

Mind Gameマインド・ゲーム

2004 · Feature, 103 min · Masaaki Yuasa · Studio 4°C

Nishi, a failed manga artist, is shot dead by a yakuza early in the film, talks his way back to life, and ends up living inside a whale. Yuasa's first feature, adapted from Robin Nishi's manga, changes style from scene to scene, mixing photographed faces with wild, rubbery drawing. It won the Japan Media Arts Festival's grand prize, and its freedom runs through all of Yuasa's later work.

MonsterMONSTER

2004 · TV, 74 episodes · Masayuki Kojima · Madhouse

In 1986 Kenzō Tenma, a Japanese brain surgeon in Düsseldorf, defies his hospital's director and operates on a boy shot in the head instead of the city's mayor. Nine years later the boy, Johan, has grown into a killer, and Tenma, suspected of his murders, hunts him across Germany and the Czech Republic. Madhouse adapted Naoki Urasawa's manga almost scene for scene over 74 episodes, proof that TV anime could sustain a slow, adult thriller.

The Girl Who Leapt Through Time時をかける少女

2006 · Feature, 98 min · Mamoru Hosoda · Madhouse

Makoto, a cheerful Tokyo schoolgirl, discovers she can leap back through time, and uses it for karaoke, better grades and dodging an awkward conversation, until her small edits start to cost other people. Hosoda's loose sequel to Yasutaka Tsutsui's 1967 novel opened in about twenty cinemas, spread by word of mouth, and won the first Japan Academy Prize for animation.

Gurren Lagann天元突破グレンラガン

2007 · TV, 27 episodes · Hiroyuki Imaishi · Gainax

Simon, a timid boy digging tunnels for an underground village, finds a tiny drill and a robot's face, and his reckless big-brother figure, Kamina, leads them up to the surface and into war. Every arc raises the stakes, from a village to a galaxy, on the belief that willpower can do anything. Imaishi and others from its team left Gainax in 2011 to found Studio Trigger.

Ponyo崖の上のポニョ

2008 · Feature, 101 min · Hayao Miyazaki · Studio Ghibli

A goldfish girl with a human face escapes her father, a sorcerer of the sea, falls in love with five-year-old Sōsuke and wants to become human, bringing a flood with her. Miyazaki had it drawn entirely by hand, in soft crayon colours, with more than 170,000 drawings, a record for one of his films, and its jaunty theme song became a hit across Japan.

Summer Warsサマーウォーズ

2009 · Feature, 114 min · Mamoru Hosoda · Madhouse

Kenji, a shy maths prodigy, agrees to pose as an older girl's fiancé at her huge family's reunion in rural Nagano. That night a rogue AI takes over OZ, the virtual world that runs everything from mobile phones to traffic lights, and the family's ninety-year-old matriarch organises the resistance by telephone. It was the first Japanese animated film in competition at Locarno.
