c. 1100 — 1916

Before the Frame

Long before cinema reached Japan, its artists were telling stories across time in pictures: on scrolls unrolled at the pace of the reader's hands, in lantern shows, in woodblock sketchbooks.

前史

12th–13th century

At Kōzan-ji temple in Kyoto, four ink scrolls show frogs wrestling rabbits, monkeys dressed as monks and a frog seated as a Buddha. The best-known scroll has no text at all. The brush line carries the story on its own.

How a scroll moves

A picture scroll is read right to left, about an arm's width at a time. You set the pace, and each new stretch of paper replaces the last. It works something like a frame, and something like a cut.

1814

Hokusai publishes the first volume of his Manga: sketchbooks of people, animals and spirits caught in the middle of moving. The word meant something like “whimsical pictures”.

c. 1907

Someone stencils fifty frames onto a strip of celluloid and joins it into a loop. A boy writes 活動写真, “moving pictures”, and bows. It lasts three seconds.

The world around it背景

Pictures that moved before film did

Anime is a twentieth-century art built on a technology invented elsewhere. Asking where it “comes from” in older Japanese art is a fair question, but an easy one to overstate. What can be said with confidence is that Japanese artists and audiences had long habits that animation later drew on: reading pictures in sequence, telling stories through line rather than modelled volume, and stacking flat planes one behind another to make space.

The great age of the picture scroll, [[emaki]], was the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The Tale of Genji scrolls lift the roofs off rooms so that we look down into them, a convention called fukinuki yatai, “blown-away roof”. The Legends of Mount Shigi follow a rice granary as it flies through the sky while a crowd runs below. The Ban Dainagon scroll stages a palace fire with dozens of figures fleeing in every direction. Some scrolls draw the same person several times in one scene so that a single picture shows a sequence of moments, a device called iji-dōzu, “different times in one picture”.

In 1999 the director Isao Takahata published a book whose title translates as Twelfth-Century Animation. Going through national-treasure scrolls passage by passage, he argued that they already contained “cinematic and animation-like” devices: something like cuts, pans and close-ups, and above all a sense of time. He did not claim the scrolls were anime. He showed that the problem animators solve, how to make pictures carry time, is very old.

In the Edo period (1603–1868), woodblock printing made pictures cheap. Comic picture books called kibyōshi combined words and images on every page, and performers staged [[utsushie]] lantern shows, moving wooden projectors behind a paper screen so that ghosts drifted and faces changed. Katsushika Hokusai's Manga, fifteen volumes published from 1814, filled page after page with bodies in action: wrestlers, acrobats, fishermen, ghosts.

Moving pictures reached Japan in 1896 and 1897, first Edison's Kinetoscope and then the Lumière brothers' Cinématographe. Silent films were shown with [[benshi]], narrators who stood beside the screen and voiced the action, and who were often a bigger draw than the film. Foreign cartoons followed. The first one known to have been screened was by the French animator Émile Cohl, shown in Tokyo's Asakusa district in April 1912.

Then, in 2005, a collection of old films and projectors in Kyoto turned up a strip of fifty frames, stencilled in red and black directly onto celluloid and joined into a loop. A boy writes 活動写真 from right to left, turns, takes off his cap and bows. It was probably made between 1905 and 1912, for home projectors, by someone whose name is lost. If the dating is right it is the oldest Japanese animation we have. Because it was never shown in a cinema, anime's history as an industry still begins a few years later, in 1917.

What they reached for, and how it felt意図・内面

Nobody was trying to invent anime

Intent. None of these artists were trying to invent anime. The scroll painters were working out how to make time visible on a surface you hold in your hands. The lantern performers wanted to frighten and delight a room. Hokusai wanted a teaching manual and a record of everything that moves. What links them is an interest in movement held still: a body caught mid-stride, a crowd mid-panic, a granary mid-flight. Animation would take that frozen instant and set it going again.

Interiority. A scroll is a private experience. One or two people unroll it at a table, at the speed of their own hands, deciding when to linger. The lantern show and, later, [[kamishibai]] were the opposite: crowded, noisy, shared. Anime inherited both. Manga and home video made it intimate, something you take in alone at your own pace; cinema and television made it communal. The boy in the stencilled loop, bowing to whoever owned the projector, sits right between the two.

Four scrolls · traditionally attributed to the monk Toba Sōjō, doubtfully

It says
Animals act out human life: archery contests, wrestling, worship before a frog Buddha. Whether it mocks the clergy or is simply play is still argued.
It feels
The brush moves at the speed of the action. Eight hundred years on, the rabbits still look mid-sprint, and the frogs still look pleased with themselves.
It changed
It is the most-cited ancestor of manga and anime, and its frogs and rabbits are now on half the souvenirs in Kyoto. Historians warn that the line of descent is not direct.

It says
A mountain monk's begging bowl lifts a stingy rich man's granary and carries it off over the hills. Later the rice bales fly home on their own.

As you unroll it, the granary flies off across the paper and the crowd runs after it, so your own hands do the chasing.

It is one of the scrolls Takahata studied in Twelfth-Century Animation to show how a single long picture can be paced like a scene.

It says
Begun as a model book for students, it grew into an encyclopedia of the visible world: people working, fighting, yawning, falling over.

Every page is crowded with small people caught halfway through doing something, as if Hokusai had stopped them mid-breath.

It spread the word “manga”. When the books reached Europe in the later nineteenth century they helped spark the fashion for Japanese art known as Japonisme.

波紋

Ripples

In Japan

Picture storytelling never stopped. In the 1930s street [[kamishibai]] performers gathered crowds of children for serialised paper-theatre stories, and several postwar manga artists, among them Shigeru Mizuki and Sanpei Shirato, started out drawing for kamishibai.

Around the world

Japanese images had already crossed the world once. From the 1860s, prints by Hokusai, Hiroshige and others shaped Japonisme in Europe. Van Gogh painted copies of Hiroshige, and Hokusai's Great Wave became one of the most reproduced images ever made. Anime would be the second wave.

One story

Isao Takahata, at sixty-three

In 1999 the director of Grave of the Fireflies published a close study of medieval picture scrolls. His argument was that a twelfth-century painter and a modern animator face the same problem: how to make a still picture carry the feeling of time.
