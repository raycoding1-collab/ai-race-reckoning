Reel 08

2011 — 2019

After the Wave

The earthquake and tsunami of March 2011 changed what Japanese stories needed to say. Streaming let Japan and the world watch the same episode on the same night. And in 2019 a fire at Kyoto Animation showed how much this art had come to mean to people everywhere.

The world around it背景

After 11 March 2011

On 11 March 2011 the strongest earthquake ever recorded in Japan sent a tsunami into the coast of Tōhoku, killing or leaving missing more than 18,000 people, and caused meltdowns at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant. In the weeks after, broadcasters practised jishuku, self-restraint. Puella Magi Madoka Magica, a magical-girl series that had turned out to be about despair and sacrifice, had its final two episodes postponed until 21 April. They aired back to back to an audience that had been waiting through a national catastrophe.

The decade's stories kept returning to disaster, memory and the wish to undo the past. Makoto Shinkai said Your Name (2016), in which a comet destroys a lakeside town, came directly from the earthquake. It earned about ¥25 billion in Japan and became, at the time, the highest-grossing anime film worldwide. That autumn also brought Naoko Yamada's A Silent Voice, about a former bully and the deaf girl he tormented, and Sunao Katabuchi's In This Corner of the World, about a young wife's daily life in wartime Kure and Hiroshima. Katabuchi could not raise money for it from the usual investors; 3,374 people funded its pilot through the crowdfunding site Makuake, then a Japanese record.

The masters made their farewells. Miyazaki's The Wind Rises (2013) is a biography of Jiro Horikoshi, designer of the Zero fighter, and an honest look at an artist whose beautiful dream became a weapon. He announced his retirement that September (it did not last). Takahata spent eight years and ¥5.15 billion on The Tale of the Princess Kaguya (2013), drawn in charcoal and watercolour strokes that seem to be still drying. He died in 2018.

The audience went global in real time. Crunchyroll and others began streaming episodes abroad within hours of Japanese broadcast, and Netflix started commissioning anime of its own, such as Masaaki Yuasa's Devilman Crybaby (2018). Attack on Titan (2013–2023) became a worldwide obsession; in 2021 the analytics firm Parrot Analytics named it the most in-demand TV show in the world, the first non-English series to top its list. Light and web novels fed an [[isekai]] boom of stories about being reborn in another world. Web-native animators were recruited onto shows such as One-Punch Man (2015) and Mob Psycho 100 (2016). And in 2019 ufotable's Demon Slayer showed what digital compositing could add to hand-drawn action.

On 18 July 2019 a man walked into Kyoto Animation's Studio 1 carrying petrol and set the building alight. Thirty-six people were killed and more than thirty injured, most of them artists. It was one of the deadliest mass killings in Japan since the Second World War. Donations of about ¥3.3 billion came from within Japan and more than US$2 million from abroad, and the studio passed all of it to victims and their families. The attacker was sentenced to death in January 2024; in 2025 he withdrew his appeal and the sentence became final.

What they reached for, and how it felt意図・内面

The ending reality refused

Intent. Shinkai wanted to imagine a world in which the people of a doomed town could be saved, and gave his audience the ending reality had refused. Katabuchi wanted to show that ordinary life went on under the bombs: cooking with weeds, mending clothes, drawing. He spent six years researching wartime Kure, gathering thousands of photographs and checking the weather for every day of the story. Yamada, whose camera lingers on hands and feet, wanted to show how hard it is for people to reach each other. Miyazaki took the epigraph of The Wind Rises from Paul Valéry, “The wind is rising, we must try to live”, and gave his designer's mentor the line that airplanes are beautiful dreams and cursed dreams at once. Takahata wanted to show the joy of being alive on Earth, and the cost of being kept from it. Hajime Isayama built Attack on Titan on walls, and then made his readers question who was really outside them.

Interiority. These are stories about grief and repair. In Your Name two people search for someone they can no longer remember, a feeling of loss without an object that many viewers recognised after 2011. A Silent Voice covers every face around its anxious hero with an X, and lets them fall away one by one as he learns to look at people again. In In This Corner of the World the heroine loses her right hand, her drawing hand, and the film's style changes with her grief. Violet Evergarden (2018), from Kyoto Animation, follows a former child soldier who writes letters for others while trying to understand the last words her commander said to her: “I love you.” Madoka ends with a girl who saves everyone by becoming a principle of the universe, and so can never be seen by her friends again.

Puella Magi Madoka Magica魔法少女まどか☆マギカ

Akiyuki Shinbo · written by Gen Urobuchi · Shaft

Madoka Kaname is an ordinary, kind fourteen-year-old. A white, cat-like creature called Kyubey offers her a contract: any wish she likes, granted, in return for becoming a [[mahoshojo|magical girl]] who fights witches. A cold transfer student, Homura, seems determined to stop her signing. Madoka hesitates, watching the girls around her accept the bargain, and slowly learning what it really costs.

It says
Kyubey's contract grants any wish and hides the price, and hope can be kept only by a girl willing to give up everything.
It feels
Two episodes of pastel magical-girl sweetness, then the third. The witches' labyrinths, cut-paper collages by Gekidan Inu Curry, look like nothing else on television.
It changed
It made the deconstruction of cute genres a genre of its own, and its delayed finale became part of how Japan lived through spring 2011.
How it was made

It was an original story, not an adaptation, made by an unlikely team. Ume Aoki, creator of the soft, round-faced comedy Hidamari Sketch, designed the girls; Gen Urobuchi, a writer of dark visual novels, wrote the scripts; Shaft's Akiyuki Shinbo directed. The witches' labyrinths went to the art duo Gekidan Inu Curry, who made them in cut-paper collage, so that each witch seems to come from another artist's nightmare. The last two episodes were held back by the earthquake of March 2011, and aired together in April.

Why it lasts

Having shown the price of every wish, the series asks what a truly selfless one would look like, and its answer made the final episodes some of the most argued-over of the decade. On a second viewing, almost every early line turns out to be a clue.

Look forEpisode 10, which goes back over the story from another girl's side and changes the meaning of everything before it.

Attack on Titan進撃の巨人

Tetsurō Araki and others · WIT Studio, then MAPPA · from Hajime Isayama's manga

For a century the last of humanity has lived inside three concentric walls, safe from the Titans: huge, grinning, naked giants who eat people for no reason anyone knows. Then a Titan taller than the walls appears and kicks a hole in the outermost one. The boy Eren Yeager watches his mother devoured and swears to kill every last Titan. He joins the army, and the story begins to ask who the real enemy is.

It says
The walls built out of fear breed enemies, the monsters outside turn out to be people, and freedom pursued without limit becomes atrocity.
It feels
The flights between rooftops on wires give you vertigo. Later seasons trade that rush for years of moral dread.
It changed
It became one of the most watched and argued-over series of the streaming era, and Parrot Analytics' most in-demand show in the world for 2021.
How it was made

Isayama was nineteen when he drew the first version, a 65-page one-shot. When Weekly Shōnen Jump turned the series down he took it to Kodansha, where it began in Bessatsu Shōnen Magazine in September 2009. He has said the Titans began with a drunk customer at the internet café where he worked: a man who spoke his language, but with whom he could not communicate at all. For the anime, the new Wit Studio, founded in 2012, built the action around the 3D manoeuvre gear, wire-and-gas rigs that let soldiers swing between rooftops and strike at a Titan's neck. MAPPA took over for the final season.

Why it lasts

A monster survival story becomes a war story, then a political tragedy about inherited hatred, and the ground shifts under the audience without the momentum ever dropping. Its ending divided fans fiercely, and the argument it started, about freedom, revenge and what one generation owes the next, is part of why it has not faded.

Look forThe title of the very first episode, 二千年後の君へ, “To You, 2,000 Years from Now”. It is not explained for a very long time.

The Wind Rises風立ちぬ

Hayao Miyazaki · Studio Ghibli

Jirō Horikoshi is a short-sighted boy who dreams of flying and can never be a pilot, so he decides to design aircraft instead. The film follows him through the Great Kantō earthquake of 1923, a financial crash, study trips to Germany and a love affair with Nahoko, who has tuberculosis, to the design of the fighter that led to the Mitsubishi Zero. Throughout, he meets his hero, the Italian designer Giovanni Caproni, in dreams.

It says
A gentle engineer designs the most beautiful plane of his age, and it goes to war.
It feels
The Great Kantō earthquake rumbles with sound effects made from human voices, and Nahoko lies dying beside Jirō, holding his hand while he draws.
It changed
It was controversial in Japan and abroad for its subject, and was received as Miyazaki's most personal reckoning with his own love of machines.
How it was made

Miyazaki first drew the story as a manga for a modelling magazine, and made it into a film at the urging of his producer, Toshio Suzuki. He merged the real engineer with the novelist Tatsuo Hori, whose novel gave the film its title, its sanatorium romance and its epigraph from Paul Valéry. For Jirō's voice he chose Hideaki Anno, who as a young animator had drawn the God Warrior in Nausicaä. The film opened in July 2013, in the middle of a national debate about revising Japan's pacifist constitution; in an essay for Ghibli's own magazine, Miyazaki called revision out of the question.

The wind is rising! We must try to live!

Paul Valéry, “The Graveyard by the Sea”: the film's epigraph, and the line Jirō and Nahoko trade, in French, when they first meet
Why it lasts

Miyazaki neither excuses Jirō nor condemns him. The beauty of the planes and the knowledge of what they were used for sit side by side, and the film leaves you holding both. He announced his retirement soon after its release, and for ten years it stood as his farewell.

Look forThe 1923 Kantō earthquake. Many of its sound effects were made with human voices.

The Tale of the Princess Kaguyaかぐや姫の物語

Isao Takahata · Studio Ghibli · eight years in production

A bamboo cutter finds a tiny girl inside a glowing stalk. She grows at astonishing speed into a wild, happy country child, until her father, convinced by the gold he also finds in the bamboo that she is meant to be a princess, moves her to the capital. There she is taught to pluck her eyebrows, blacken her teeth, keep still and be courted, while five noble suitors demand her hand.

It says
Life on Earth, with all its pain, is worth more than the Moon's feelingless heaven, and Kaguya's tragedy is being kept from living it.
It feels
It looks like a picture scroll come alive, until Kaguya flees the palace and the lines break into furious charcoal scratches.
It changed
The most expensive Japanese film ever made, nominated for an Oscar, and the last work of one of the medium's greatest directors.
How it was made

In a 1999 book, Twelfth-Century Animation, Takahata had argued that Japan's picture scrolls were already a kind of cinema, and Kaguya was his attempt to make a film that looked like one: charcoal lines left unfinished, watercolour washes, the white of the paper showing through. The style was so laborious that the film took eight years. Its backer, Nippon TV's chairman Seiichirō Ujiie, an admirer of Takahata's work, died in 2011 before it was finished; he is credited as its planner.

Why it lasts

It turns a thousand-year-old tale that every Japanese child knows into a furious protest on behalf of a girl's right to her own life, in a style that makes the medium feel newly invented. Takahata was seventy-eight when it was released.

Look forKaguya's escape from the capital, drawn in furious charcoal strokes. It goes straight back to the emaki of Reel 00.

Your Name君の名は。

Makoto Shinkai · CoMix Wave Films · music by Radwimps

Mitsuha, a high-school girl in the lakeside mountain town of Itomori, longs to escape to Tokyo. Taki is a Tokyo schoolboy with a part-time job in an Italian restaurant. One morning they wake up in each other's bodies, and it keeps happening, a few days a week, so they leave each other notes and diary entries to cope. Then, as a comet passes over Japan, the swapping stops.

It says
Two strangers who forget each other's names stay connected anyway, and a town destroyed by a comet is saved, at least in a story.
It feels
For an hour it is a body-swap comedy. Then a comet falls, and it becomes a race to save a town that is already gone.
It changed
A record-breaking hit across Asia, it made Shinkai the most commercially powerful anime director after Miyazaki.
How it was made

Shinkai's earlier films had been admired and small; this one had Toho behind it and a larger team. Masayoshi Tanaka designed the characters, and Masashi Andō, an animation director on Spirited Away, supervised the drawing. The rock band Radwimps wrote the score and four songs, working with Shinkai from the script stage, and several sequences are cut to their music like music videos. Itomori is invented but drawn from real places: its lake recalls Lake Suwa, and its streets and station come from Hida-Furukawa in Gifu.

Why it lasts

It works for every age at once: body-swap comedy, romance and disaster film, and under them a story about holding on to someone you can no longer remember. Its images, the braided red cord, the twilight hour, the shrine stairs in Tokyo, spread across Asia, and the places behind them became [[seichi|pilgrimage sites]].

Look forThe rhymes. Shinkai builds the film from paired images, a sliding door, a cord, a hand reaching out, one from each side of the story.

A Silent Voice聲の形

Naoko Yamada · Kyoto Animation · written by Reiko Yoshida

At primary school, Shōya Ishida led the bullying of Shōko Nishimiya, a deaf girl new to his class, until she moved schools and he became the outcast. Years later, a lonely teenager who has decided he has no future, he sets out to find her and make amends. He has learned sign language. She is glad to see him, which is harder to bear than anger would have been.

It says
Shōya bullied Shōko as a boy. He learns that atonement is slow, and that hating himself is its own kind of cruelty.
It feels
The camera keeps looking at hands, feet and the ground, the way Shōya can't look people in the face.
It changed
It brought bullying, disability and suicidal feeling into a mainstream film with unusual care, and established Yamada as a major director.
How it was made

Ōima's first version, a one-shot that won a newcomers' award in 2008, waited more than two years for publication while her publisher worried how readers would take a story about a deaf girl being bullied. Her mother is a sign-language interpreter, and her mother and sister helped her get the signing right. Yamada, who had made her name directing K-On!, condensed the manga's seven volumes into two hours by staying close to Shōya and his guilt.

Why it lasts

Forgiveness comes slowly and only in part, and Shōya has to learn to accept it as well as to ask for it. The film never pretends that one apology could be enough.

Look forThe X marks over the faces of everyone Shōya cannot bring himself to look at, and the moments when they fall away.

In This Corner of the Worldこの世界の片隅に

Sunao Katabuchi · MAPPA · from Fumiyo Kōno's manga

Suzu is a dreamy girl from Hiroshima who loves to draw. In 1944, at eighteen, she marries into a family in Kure, the naval port along the coast, and learns to keep house as rationing tightens: foraging weeds for meals, re-cutting kimono into work clothes. The harbour below is full of warships, and for a long time the war is something she sketches.

It says
History happens to Suzu while she is cooking dinner, and the everyday life she loses is the real cost of the war.
It feels
For a long time it is gentle and funny. Then the air raids come, shown as bursts of paint, the way Suzu would see them.
It changed
Crowdfunded, released on a few screens, it ran for years in Japanese cinemas and won the Japan Academy Prize for animation.
How it was made

Katabuchi, an assistant director on Kiki's Delivery Service, spent years researching Kure and Hiroshima as they were in 1944 and 1945, down to the shop fronts and the dates of air raids, so that Suzu's world would be accurate in every detail. When investors hesitated over a quiet film about wartime housework, he turned to crowdfunding: 3,374 people gave ¥36 million, and their names are in the credits. It opened on just 63 screens. One cinema in Ibaraki was still showing it a thousand days later.

Why it lasts

It earns its devastation slowly: by the time the raids come, we know the household's routines so well that every loss lands as a domestic one. It sits beside Grave of the Fireflies as one of the essential animated films about wartime Japan, more patient and, in the end, more hopeful.

Look forThe picture Suzu paints for a boy in her class: the sea, with its whitecaps turned into leaping white rabbits.

Violet Evergardenヴァイオレット・エヴァーガーデン

Taichi Ishidate · Kyoto Animation · from Kana Akatsuki's novel

A war has ended. Violet, a teenage girl raised as a weapon, wakes in hospital with metal arms and one memory that matters: the last words of the major who commanded her, which she did not understand. She takes a job as an Auto Memory Doll, a ghostwriter of letters for people who cannot put their feelings into words, hoping to learn what he meant.

It says
A girl raised as a weapon learns what she feels by putting other people's feelings into letters.
It feels
The light through each window and the lace on each cuff are finished with unusual care, and episode after episode ends with a letter and someone in tears.
It changed
It became, for many viewers, the defining Kyoto Animation work. Its films, completed after the fire, are also memorials.
How it was made

Akatsuki's novel won the grand prize of Kyoto Animation's own fiction award in 2014, the first work to win it in any category, so the studio owned the story from the start. Kyoto Animation, which trains its artists itself and pays them salaries rather than by the drawing, gave it its most lavish production: costumes with every button drawn, light falling across paper, a port city modelled on early twentieth-century Europe. On 18 July 2019 an arson attack on the studio killed 36 people. The two films were completed by those who survived, and opened in September 2019 and September 2020.

I want to know what “I love you” means.

Violet, in the first episode
Why it lasts

Most episodes are a single commission, a letter written for someone else, so the series becomes an anthology of other people's love, grief and pride, and Violet learns a little from each. Episode 10, in which she spends a week writing letters for a mother while the woman's small daughter waits outside the door, is famous for leaving viewers in tears.

Look forViolet's hands: metal prosthetics, animated with exact, mechanical care at the typewriter, and usually hidden in gloves.

Demon Slayer: Kimetsu no Yaiba鬼滅の刃

Haruo Sotozaki · ufotable · from Koyoharu Gotōge's manga

In the Taishō era, Tanjirō Kamado, a charcoal-seller's son, comes home from town to find his family slaughtered by a demon. Only his sister Nezuko survives, and she has been turned into a demon herself, though something of her still recognises him. Carrying her on his back in a wooden box, he trains as a demon slayer, hoping to find a way to make her human again.

It says
Tanjirō kills demons to save his sister and mourns each one, because each was once a person.
It feels
Tanjirō's sword strokes trail water drawn like an ukiyo-e print, and between fights he grieves for his family.
It changed
Episode 19 turned a solid hit into a phenomenon, and set up the biggest box-office run in Japanese history.
How it was made

Gotōge's manga was a moderate seller in Weekly Shōnen Jump until the anime. The adaptation went to ufotable, a studio that does its own digital compositing and had made its name with the Fate series by blending hand-drawn characters with 3D settings and effects. Episode 19, in which Tanjirō first calls on his father's fire dance, set to Go Shiina's song “Kamado Tanjirō no Uta”, trended worldwide the night it aired. Gotōge, who has never appeared in public, is represented in interviews by a drawing of a crocodile in glasses.

Why it lasts

It became a shared, all-ages craze in Japan, of a kind once reserved for Ghibli or Doraemon. Tanjirō's green-and-black chequered haori turned up on face masks and lunchboxes, and in 2019 the manga ended One Piece's eleven-year run as Japan's best-selling series.

Look forThe water techniques, styled like the waves of ukiyo-e prints. The woodblocks of Reel 00 are still here.

2019

波紋

Ripples

In Japan

  • Anime became a way of working through 3.11, in Your Name and again, more directly, in Suzume (2022).
  • Crowdfunding and streaming money opened routes around the production-committee system.
  • The Kyoto Animation fire forced a public reckoning with the safety and conditions of the people who make anime.

Around the world

Simulcasting ended the long wait between Japan and everyone else. Your Name became, at the time, the highest-grossing Japanese film in China. Attack on Titan and Demon Slayer made anime part of mainstream conversation on every continent, and Netflix began commissioning anime for a global audience.

One story

Thirty-six names

After the fire, fans around the world held vigils and raised money. When Kyoto Animation's next film opened that September, its credits listed everyone who had worked on it, including the colleagues who had been killed.

Steins;Gateシュタインズ・ゲート

2011 · TV, 24 episodes · Hiroshi Hamasaki, Takuya Satō · White Fox

Okabe, a self-styled mad scientist in Akihabara, discovers that the microwave he and his friends have wired to a phone can send text messages into the past. The fun of tinkering with history becomes a nightmare as each change costs someone he loves. Adapted from a visual novel, it spends its first half on lazy, joking friendship so that the second half's time loops hurt more.

Wolf Childrenおおかみこどもの雨と雪

Hana, a student in Tokyo, falls in love with a man who is secretly the last of the Japanese wolves. After his death she raises their two children, who switch between human and wolf, alone in a remote mountain village. Hosoda's first film at his own Studio Chizu spans thirteen years of a mother's life, and lets her children choose different worlds.

Your Lie in April四月は君の嘘

2014 · TV, 22 episodes · Kyōhei Ishiguro · A-1 Pictures

Kōsei, once a child piano prodigy, stopped being able to hear his own playing after his mother died. Years later a free-spirited violinist, Kaori, drags him back onto the stage as her accompanist. The series is built around real concert pieces, by Saint-Saëns, Kreisler and Chopin, and around the idea that music is how we speak to the people we have lost.

One-Punch Manワンパンマン

2015 · TV, 12 episodes · Shingo Natsume · Madhouse

Saitama trained so hard that he can beat any opponent with a single punch, and is now bored to despair. ONE's parody of superhero manga began as a crudely drawn webcomic, which Yusuke Murata redrew in lavish detail. Madhouse matched him: director Shingo Natsume and designer Chikashi Kubota made the first season a showcase of [[sakuga]], its fights drawn by some of the best animators working.

Mob Psycho 100モブサイコ100

2016 · TV, 12 episodes · Yuzuru Tachikawa · Bones

Shigeo “Mob” Kageyama is a middle-schooler with immense psychic power, which he keeps bottled up because he is afraid of his own feelings, while working for a charming fake psychic. ONE's second hit became one of the most inventive animated series of the decade, and its ending credits were painted on glass, frame by frame, by the artist Miyo Sato.

Devilman CrybabyDEVILMAN crybaby

2018 · Netflix, 10 episodes · Masaaki Yuasa · Science SARU

Gentle, tearful Akira merges with a demon at the urging of his brilliant friend Ryō, and becomes Devilman. Yuasa remade Go Nagai's apocalyptic 1972 manga for Netflix as ten episodes of sex, violence, rap and grief, released worldwide on the same day. It was named Anime of the Year at the Crunchyroll Anime Awards.

Weathering with You天気の子

2019 · Feature, 112 min · Makoto Shinkai · CoMix Wave Films

Hodaka, a runaway teenager in Tokyo during a summer of endless rain, meets Hina, a girl who can make the sun come out by praying. They start a business selling sunshine, until the cost of her gift becomes clear. Shinkai's follow-up to Your Name turns climate change into a personal question: what would you give up for a clear sky? It took ¥14.2 billion in Japan.
