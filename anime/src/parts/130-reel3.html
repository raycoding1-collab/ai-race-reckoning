Reel 03

1963 — 1973

Mighty Atom and the Living Room

Osamu Tezuka bet that a cartoon could be made every week for a fraction of the usual cost. To do it he cut the drawings to the bone, and turned the limits of cheap animation into a new visual language.

テレビまんが

1 January 1963

Tetsuwan Atom premieres on Fuji TV: the first weekly half-hour animated series made in Japan. It runs for 193 episodes.

¥550,000

The price Tezuka quoted per episode, far below what a show like it actually cost. He made up the difference with manga royalties and licensing.

March 1970

About 800 people attend a Buddhist funeral for Tōru Rikiishi, a boxer who existed only on paper. Days later, hijackers seizing a Japan Airlines flight announce, “We are Ashita no Joe.”

1973

Tezuka's Mushi Production goes bankrupt. Its staff scatter to found or fill the studios of the next forty years.

背景

The world around it

Japanese television began in 1953. The Crown Prince's wedding in 1959 and the Tokyo Olympics in 1964 sent sales of sets soaring, and the new channels needed hours of programmes to fill. Animation was the most expensive thing they could buy. A half-hour of cartoons in the Toei style, drawn on ones and twos, cost several times what a live-action children's show did.

Osamu Tezuka, born in 1928 and raised in Takarazuka, had trained as a doctor and then become the most popular cartoonist in Japan, with cinematic manga such as New Treasure Island (1947), Kimba and Astro Boy. He adored Disney and the Fleischer brothers, and he wanted to animate. In 1961 he set up his own studio, Mushi Production, and in 1963 sold Fuji TV a weekly Tetsuwan Atom at ¥550,000 an episode. It was far below the real cost, and Tezuka covered the gap with his manga income and with licensing: the confectioner Meiji Seika put Atom stickers in boxes of Marble chocolate, an early case of what Japan now calls the [[mediamix]].

To make a cartoon every week for that price, Mushi stripped animation down. Drawings were held for three frames instead of one or two, so a second of film might use eight drawings instead of twenty-four. Shots were reused across episodes from a [[bank]] of stock footage. Faces stayed still while separate mouth cels flapped, a trick called [[kuchipaku]]. Camera moves across a single [[tome|held drawing]] stood in for action. None of these devices was new; American studios such as UPA and Hanna-Barbera used many of them. But Mushi turned them into a system, and then into a style.

It worked. Astro Boy ran for 193 episodes and opened the floodgates. The same year brought Tetsujin 28-gō and Eightman; Toei answered with Ken the Wolf Boy. In 1965 Mushi's Jungle Emperor (Kimba the White Lion) became the first full-colour series made for Japanese TV. Toei's Sally the Witch (1966) began the [[mahoshojo|magical girl]] genre; Tatsunoko's Mach GoGoGo (1967) became Speed Racer abroad; Star of the Giants (1968) led the [[spokon]] boom on television; and Sazae-san (1969) started a run that has never ended.

Not everyone accepted the TV model. At Toei, Isao Takahata directed Hols: Prince of the Sun (1968), an ambitious feature whose budget was frozen mid-production, forcing scenes into still frames. It failed at the box office and Takahata was demoted. Mushi made a trilogy of erotic “Animerama” features for adults, ending with the watercolour Belladonna of Sadness (1973). Gō Nagai's Mazinger Z (1972) put a boy in the cockpit of a giant robot for the first time, and its die-cast metal toys turned robot shows into a merchandising business. In 1973 Mushi went bankrupt. Its people founded or filled Sunrise, Madhouse, Group TAC and Tezuka Productions: the industry's next generation of studios.

意図・内面

What they reached for, and how it felt

Intent. Tezuka had lived through the bombing of Osaka and trained in medicine, and his stories return again and again to the value of life. Astro Boy is a robot boy built by a grieving scientist to replace his dead son, then rejected because he cannot grow up. Named after the atom in the decade after Hiroshima, he is a gentle superweapon who wants to be human and defends a world that does not quite accept him. Osamu Dezaki, who directed Ashita no Joe, wanted stillness to carry emotion. At the peak of a scene he froze the action into a painted [[harmony|“harmony” frame]], or split the screen, or repeated a blow three times from three angles. Takahata, in Hols, wanted a hero who wins only when a whole village acts together, and a heroine, Hilda, torn between the evil that controls her and the people she has come to love. She is often called the first psychologically complex heroine in Japanese animation.

Interiority. Television made anime part of family life. Children watched the same heroes at the same hour every week in the living room and grew up alongside them. The [[spokon]] dramas spoke to the work ethic of the economic miracle, all effort and endurance. Ashita no Joe went further. Joe Yabuki is a slum kid who boxes because it is the only thing that makes him feel alive, and the story ends, in the manga's last panel, with him slumped in his corner, burned out to “pure white ash”. When his rival Rikiishi died in the manga in 1970, the poet Shūji Terayama organised a real funeral and about 800 people came. When student radicals hijacked a plane that month, they named themselves after Joe. For a generation he stood for burning yourself out against a system you cannot beat.

1973

波紋

Ripples

In Japan

  • Weekly anime became a fixed part of childhood, and its sponsors, from chocolate makers to toy companies, learned to sell through characters.
  • Studios learned to work at TV speed, and to subcontract. Low fees became the norm.
  • When Mushi collapsed, its alumni founded Sunrise and Madhouse, which would make Gundam, Cowboy Bebop, Perfect Blue and Frieren.

Around the world

Astro Boy, Kimba and Speed Racer ran on American TV within a few years of their Japanese debuts, renamed and lightly re-edited. Few viewers knew they were Japanese. The scholar Kōichi Iwabuchi later called such exports “culturally odourless”, their [[mukokuseki|stateless]] look helping them travel.

One story

A funeral for a boxer who never lived

In March 1970 the poet and playwright Shūji Terayama held a Buddhist funeral for Tōru Rikiishi, Joe's rival, who had died in the manga. About 800 people came, from schoolchildren to office workers. A priest chanted sutras before a black-framed portrait of a drawing.
